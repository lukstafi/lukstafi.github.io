*This is the last of three parts on continual learning. [Part 1](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/who-teaches-the-weights.html) read self-distillation as consolidation: the model with privileged context teaches the bare model. It also found that on-policy training narrows the alternatives within a task. [Part 2](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/learning-about-what-you-do-not-do.html) joined Vector Policy Optimization to an older programme of learning many predictions off-policy. This part asks where the results of learning are kept, and what moves them from one store to another.*

The usual way to frame continual learning in language models sets two camps against each other. One camp keeps the model fixed and gives it memory outside the weights: retrieved documents, notes the agent writes for itself, skill files, a longer context. The other changes the weights. The debate between them is real, but the dichotomy hides most of the interesting structure. A deployed model already has half a dozen places where experience can be kept. They differ in what writes them, how long their contents last, how much they hold before entries interfere, and what it costs to read them. The fast ones learn well and forget quickly. The slow ones keep what they learn and learn poorly. Most of the methods in Parts 1 and 2 are best understood as traffic between these stores, in which a faster store teaches a slower one.

Before we lay out that ladder, one more route to learning needs a place on it. Parts 1 and 2 took the update rule as given: a gradient step on some loss, with the interesting choices in the teacher, the support and the objective. A different line of work learns the update rule itself.

## Learning the update rule

A learned update rule is a network whose output is a change to another network's weights, or to its own. The idea is old. Schmidhuber's [fast weight programmers](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1992.4.1.131) of the early 1990s were slow networks that wrote into the weights of fast ones. Three recent instances bear on continual learning.

[Kazuki Irie, Róbert Csordás and Jürgen Schmidhuber](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00276) meta-learned continual-learning algorithms directly, in a method they call Automated Continual Learning. Their learner is a self-referential weight matrix that rewrites itself with a delta rule as examples stream in. The rewriting rule is trained, across many sequences of tasks, so that after reading a sequence the matrix classifies examples from every task in it. The learned rule therefore has to solve the stability problem itself, since only an update that does not erase earlier tasks scores well. On split MNIST in the class-incremental setting, with five tasks and no replay, the learned rule reached 84.3% accuracy where elastic weight consolidation reached 19.8%. On harder image benchmarks it still trailed methods built on large pretrained models. The delta rule here is the same error-correcting update that runs inside the recurrent layers of current hybrid language models. Below, it reappears as the second rung of the ladder.

Hypernetworks make the learned update explicit. [Text-to-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06105), from Sakana AI, maps a natural-language description of a task to a low-rank adapter for a language model in one forward pass. [Doc-to-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15902) maps a document to an adapter, so the model can answer questions about the document without the document in its context. Within the window, reading the document in context still does better: on SQuAD the adapter recovers about 82% of in-context performance. The adapter's advantage appears past the window, where the base model cannot read the document at all, and in cost. Internalizing a document takes under a second, and the adapter needs megabytes where a long context needs gigabytes. Ilija Lichkovski made the point in [his podcast interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=loXZk5uYWLE) that Doc-to-LoRA is a learned learning algorithm: a sample-efficient map from new content to a weight change. Nothing in its setup is shaped like continual learning, and yet it is exactly the consolidation step that Part 1 attributed to self-distillation, amortized into a single pass.

Lichkovski presented an experiment of his own in the same interview, as preliminary and unpublished, and its negative result is the instructive part. He trained a hypernetwork on split MNIST, the digit benchmark presented two classes at a time. Given the current weights and the identity of the next task, it was to output the weights that multitask training on all tasks so far would have produced. Multitask training was the oracle, and the hypernetwork was to reach it sequentially. It worked surprisingly well. Average accuracy rose with each new task toward the multitask level, while fine-tuning stayed at the one-fifth that total forgetting gives. Backward transfer was positive: earlier tasks improved after later ones were learned. Each task cost one forward pass at deployment. Then the ablations showed that the hypernetwork ignored the task identity. It had learned a general operator that moves any weight configuration toward the region of weight space where multitask solutions lie. The weights carried far more information than the small task embedding, so the operator attended to the weights.

That result fits a pattern [The Given and the Found](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/the-given-and-the-found.html) described for iterative reasoners (§6): supervision constrains a model only where training visits, and leaves the rest free. Here every training update moved toward multitask solutions. Nothing in the loss penalized ignoring the task identity, and the cheapest way to predict the updates was to learn that move, not to learn the tasks. The failure is also a finding. A learned "move toward multitask" operator is a learned stability mechanism, a consolidation step that does not need to know what it consolidates.

One more line connects these to composition. Lake and Baroni's [meta-learning for compositionality](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06668-3) (MLC) trained a standard transformer on a stream of small compositional tasks, each built from invented words. On their pseudoword instruction task, where "dax" might mean a red circle and "dax fep" three of them, people produced the systematic answer about 81% of the time, and the network's sampled answers about 82% of the time. Lichkovski's proposed benchmark, discussed below, is a sequential version of the same task. The words arrive one at a time and must be composed later.

## A ladder of stores

Here are the stores a deployed language-model agent can use, from fastest to slowest.

1. **The context.** Attention over the key–value cache is in-context learning. The forward pass writes it, and nothing in it is ever overwritten. It lasts for the session, or until entries are evicted or the history is compacted into a summary.
2. **The recurrent state.** Hybrid models such as [Kimi K3](https://www.kimi.ai/blog/kimi-k3) and [Qwen3.8](https://qwen.ai/blog?id=qwen3.8) interleave attention with gated delta-rule layers. Each such layer keeps a fixed-size matrix that every token updates by one error-correcting step. This is a fast-weight memory, and it is bounded.
3. **Harness memory.** Notes the agent writes, skill files, retrieved documents and optimized prompts persist indefinitely, as text.
4. **Prediction heads.** These are Part 2's banks of general value functions: small learned predictors, updated online by temporal-difference learning, that answer questions about what would happen under various behaviours.
5. **Adapters.** Low-rank weight deltas, written by gradient descent or by one hypernetwork pass.
6. **Core weights.** Changed by fine-tuning, reinforcement learning or self-distillation.
7. **The release cycle.** A lab trains specialist policies and distils them into the next checkpoint.

Four questions separate the rungs: what writes the store, what persists in it and for how long, how much it holds before entries interfere, and what it costs to read.

| Store | Written by | Persists | Capacity and interference | Cost to read |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Context (KV cache) | the forward pass; nothing overwritten | the session, until eviction or compaction | grows with the context; no overwriting, but quality decays with length | grows with the cache, or with the selection under sparse attention |
| Recurrent state | one error-correcting step per token, by a rule learned in pretraining | the session, if the serving protocol keeps it | bounded; writes erase along their key; overlapping keys interfere | constant per token |
| Harness memory | the agent, in text | indefinitely | unbounded; retrieval degrades as the store grows | retrieval, then context |
| Prediction heads | temporal-difference updates | indefinitely | one head per question; linear in features | one dot product per question |
| Adapters | gradient, or one hypernetwork pass | indefinitely | small; composing several is an open problem | nothing extra once merged |
| Core weights | gradient toward data, reward or a self-teacher | indefinitely | large, but entangled: catastrophic forgetting lives here | nothing extra |

Four claims follow from setting the stores out this way.

**In-context learning is the best learner on the ladder, example for example.** Part 1 cited [Lampinen and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00661): a model reasons from facts in its context, to reversals and syllogisms, far better than it reasons from the same facts trained into its weights. The question for every slower store is whether it can keep that quality once the content has left the context.

**The recurrent state is parametric continual learning, already running inside frontier models.** A delta-rule layer treats its state $S$ as the weights of a linear map from keys to values. For each token it reads the current prediction $S k_t$, compares it with the token's value $v_t$, and writes the correction:

$$
S_t=S_{t-1}+\beta_t\,(v_t-S_{t-1}k_t)\,k_t^\top,
$$

usually after a learned decay of $S_{t-1}$. This is one step of gradient descent on $\|S k_t-v_t\|^2/2$, the Widrow–Hoff rule of 1960. [Schlag, Irie and Schmidhuber](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11174) showed in 2021 that linear attention is a fast weight programmer, and introduced this write to keep its purely additive memory from saturating. What matters here is that the update is error-correcting, so it can revise. The temporal-difference update of [How Can a Reward Train a Neural Network?](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/how-can-a-reward-train-a-neural-network.html) has the same shape. With a linear value estimate $\theta^\top\phi$, TD(0) adds $\alpha\,(r+\gamma\,\theta^\top\phi'-\theta^\top\phi)\,\phi$. Set $\gamma=0$, and the target is the observed signal alone: the delta rule, with $\phi$ as the key and $r$ as the value. A delta-rule layer is a TD(0) learner with no future to bootstrap from. The GQ(λ) heads of Part 2 are the same learner given a future, a target policy and a trace. The second and fourth rungs of the ladder share an update rule, and they differ in their targets and in how long they keep what they learn.

> **Worked example G: overwriting a redefined word.** Give a delta-rule memory two orthogonal keys, $k_{\text{zup}}=(1,0)$ and $k_{\text{dax}}=(0,1)$, and let the values be colours. Write zup → red, then dax → blue, then zup → green, each with $\beta=1$. The third write reads the current prediction for zup (red), and adds $(\text{green}-\text{red})\,k_{\text{zup}}^\top$. Now zup reads green and dax still reads blue: the redefinition replaced the old association in place. With $\beta=0.5$, zup reads half red and half green; each repetition of the new definition shrinks the remaining error by the factor $1-\beta\|k\|^2$. Purely additive linear attention, which writes $v\,k^\top$ without reading first, would make zup read red plus green. Now let the keys overlap: if $k_{\text{dax}}$ had cosine $0.6$ with $k_{\text{zup}}$, the same correction would move dax's readout by $0.6$ times $(\text{green}-\text{red})$. Interference is visible in one line. Fine-tuning a contradicting fact into core weights has no such targeted erase. The new fact is added by gradient steps that also move every other fact sharing the affected weights.

Simple online learners, such as stochastic gradient descent on linear models or recursive least squares, are therefore not free of interference. The Widrow–Hoff rule revises cleanly when keys are orthogonal and interferes in proportion to their overlap. A delta-rule layer is exactly such a learner, and [short trigger strings](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01972) are known to overwrite the hidden state of Mamba models irreversibly. The interference that makes continual learning hard in deep networks is not absent in simple learners. It is easier to see.

The capacity is bounded by the size of the matrix. [Alex Wang's account of associative memory](https://alexwang.ai/posts/associative-memory/) makes the bound concrete for one criterion: recalling a clean value vector, with random keys. By that criterion a memory written by adding outer products, as purely additive linear attention does, recalls about $d_k$ associations in $d_k$ dimensions before interference swamps the signal. The least-squares memory that repeated delta-rule writes approach recalls about $2d_k$. A looser criterion counts more. If the readout only has to pick the right answer from a fixed set, as next-token prediction does, a $d_k\times d_v$ matrix holds on the order of $d_k d_v$ associations, up to a logarithm ([Nichani, Lee and Bietti](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06538)). An MLP's nonlinearity suppresses interference, so the core weights hold far more than the residual width suggests, in superposition. Softmax attention over a context recalls vastly more, about a million associations at $d_k=64$ with the usual scaling, because the exponential suppresses every key that does not match. The gates set the rate of forgetting. Kimi's [KDA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26692) decays each channel at its own learned rate. The contents vanish at the end of the session unless the serving protocol keeps the state.

That a model has a fast-weight store does not show what it uses the store for. In Qwen3.5 hybrids, [an interpretability study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33093) of associative recall swapped internal states between runs. Swapping the recurrent states moved the answer by only 0.2 to 3.6% of the full effect, while swapping the attention caches moved it by 96 to 100%. For that task the recall ran through the context. The prompts were not built to overflow the state, so the routing was learned, not forced by capacity.

[A second study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04434) found what the recurrent layers carry instead. After a prompt was processed, it kept either the attention cache or the recurrent state and let the model generate. Exact retrieval survived only through the cache. The requested output language and persona survived mainly through the recurrent state; Kimi Linear, a smaller relative of Kimi K3, showed the same division. The cache is the store that grows with the context, and it holds the content. The state is the store that every token reads, and it holds a few settings that condition everything generated. On the ladder, the second rung is so far a store of dispositions for the session rather than of facts.

**Slow-weight continual learning is best understood as consolidation from faster stores.** Self-distillation moves what the context achieved into the core weights (Part 1). Doc-to-LoRA moves a document into an adapter in one pass. [*Learning, Fast and Slow*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12484) interleaves prompt evolution, the fast learner, with reinforcement learning on the weights, the slow one. On a synthetic graph task where reinforcement learning alone earned nearly nothing for about 250 steps, the combination was earning reward by step 50: the evolved prompts lifted reward off zero, so the weight updates had a signal to work with. The authors' broader finding is a division of labour. The prompts absorb task-specific detail, so the weights drift up to 70% less from the base model. Lichkovski described this as a remedy for "discovery-shaped" problems, where reinforcement learning otherwise waits for a lucky success. In each case a faster, better learner produces the target and a slower store absorbs it. The open problems are therefore less about stability than about two other things. One is coverage: what revisits the old skills, and the old alternatives, that the new training never touches. The other is composition across time, which the benchmarks below are only starting to measure.

The architecture these methods point toward has been proposed in outline. A [position paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01761) from the Dagstuhl seminar on continual learning proposes a core model, a working memory and a long-term memory. In that architecture an internal regime replays stored memories through the core model to distil them into its parameters, "sparsely after accumulating sufficient experience". Context-conditioned self-distillation and the laboratories' on-policy distillation are concrete versions of that consolidation pathway, now with evidence about when it retains and when it fails. Different contents should also change at different rates. A library's changing syntax can be revised by one new example, while a regularity attested many times should resist a single contradiction. Multi-timescale teachers (a moving average, an episodic buffer, a frozen base anchor) and per-channel decay gates are working instances of that idea.

**The cut between parametric and non-parametric memory is the wrong one.** The recurrent state is parametric and is wiped at the end of every session. Harness memory is non-parametric and permanent. Prediction heads are parametric, are written by temporal-difference learning toward their own next predictions rather than toward data, and store a different kind of content: knowledge of what would happen, not of what to do. The distinctions that predict behaviour are the four columns of the table.

### Consolidation is an M-step

[How Does a Policy Improve?](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/how-does-a-policy-improve.html) ended with a way to read any policy-improvement algorithm. It asks four questions: where the candidates come from, how they are evaluated, how an improved distribution is built from them, and how the improvement is retained. Its central example was expectation–maximization. The E-step builds an improved distribution, by search, a critic or conditioning on success. The M-step fits a parametric policy to that distribution by weighted likelihood. On the ladder, each consolidation is an EM step split across two rungs. A faster store performs the E-step, because it can build a better distribution without changing any weights. A slower store performs the M-step, because it can keep the result.

| Method | E-step: candidates and improved distribution | M-step: where the improvement is kept |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SDFT, OPSD | the student's samples, rescored by the same model with a demonstration in context (an implicit critic at $\eta=1$) | core weights, by per-token distillation on the student's prefixes |
| SDPO | as above, with environment feedback in the context | core weights |
| iSDFT | the same tilt, with its temperature set by an information budget | core weights, plus a fixed anchor to the base |
| VPO | a set of answers built in context, scored by the max over random weightings | core weights, by GRPO on the set score |
| Horde, GQ(λ) | the behaviour's experience, reweighted toward many target policies | prediction heads; no policy is fitted |
| *Learning, Fast and Slow* | evolved prompts that lift reward | prompts first, then core weights by reinforcement learning |
| Doc-to-LoRA | a document | an adapter, written in one pass by an amortized M-step |
| Lichkovski's hypernetwork | a new task, with multitask training as the oracle | weights, by an amortized step toward the multitask solution |
| Multi-teacher on-policy distillation | specialist policies advising on the student's own states | the next release's core weights |
| TTT-Discover | many attempts at one problem, scored by a verifier | a low-rank adapter for that problem alone |

Two rows deserve a closer look. Lichkovski's hypernetwork amortizes the trivial solution from Part 1, retraining on everything seen so far: its training oracle is the multitask solution, and it learns to jump there in one pass. That it ignored the task identity is then less surprising. An amortized M-step toward the multitask solution needs to know where the weights are, and much less about what was added. Doc-to-LoRA is an amortized M-step for context distillation. Its paper compares it directly with distilling the same document by gradient steps. Given the right queries, the gradient route is somewhat more accurate and takes about forty seconds per document, while the hypernetwork takes under one.

The M-step's divergence matters here as it did in Part 1. Multi-teacher distillation fits one student to several teachers, each advising on its own domain's prompts. On-policy distillation is usually written in the mode-seeking direction, which lets the student settle on one of a teacher's alternatives. The direction is a separate choice from the sampling, and [Zucchet and Linderman](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03529) found that it, not on-policy sampling, accounts for the lower entropy of on-policy distillation. A mass-covering fit would push the student to keep whatever each teacher keeps, and, as Part 1 noted, to spread extra mass wherever it cannot fit a teacher exactly. In their single-seed runs distilling Qwen3 teachers into a 0.6-billion-parameter student on mathematics, the mode-seeking direction slightly improved accuracy, while the mass-covering direction on the student's own samples raised entropy most and lowered accuracy during training. All the runs ended within about three points of each other. Whatever the direction, the teachers were themselves trained by reinforcement learning on scalar rewards. A mass-covering fit preserves the alternatives a teacher still has, and it cannot restore the ones the teacher lost.

### The release cycle is consolidation already

At the slowest rung, the open-weight laboratories already practise a form of consolidation, at the cadence of releases. DeepSeek's [V4 report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19348) describes building specialist models by fine-tuning and GRPO, then consolidating them into one checkpoint by on-policy distillation. [GLM-5.2's account](https://z.ai/blog/glm-5.2) describes the same consolidation of specialists, and [GLM-5.3's announcement](https://z.ai/blog/glm-5.3) attributes its gains to more post-training on the same base. [Kimi K3](https://github.com/MoonshotAI/Kimi-K3/blob/main/k3_tech_report.pdf) trains nine policies, three domains at three levels of reasoning effort, then distils all of them into one model with multiple teachers. [Xiaomi's MiMo](https://huggingface.co/XiaomiMiMo/MiMo-V2.6-Pro-RL/blob/73875d00b30a89ef8cc353a0b60b0e9f9561952d/MiMo_V2_6_technical_report.pdf) uses three streams of distillation data: the student's own rollouts scored by the teachers, student turns continuing teacher-written histories, and student continuations of supervised demonstrations.

This is a hybrid that surveys of continual learning recommend, here at scale: capacity set aside for each skill, followed by distillation. Its support is on-policy, because the student generates the states on which the teachers advise it. Two things are missing for it to count as continual learning in the full sense. The specialists are built in parallel from one base, so tasks never arrive in sequence and nothing is learned on top of a previous consolidation's side effects. And no report measures the alternatives within each skill, before or after distillation, so the concern raised in the previous section cannot yet be checked.

### What survives of the argument for parametric memory

Lichkovski's case for changing weights at all, in his essay ["Defining Continual Learning"](https://x.com/carnot_cyclist/status/2041479655035679163) and in the interview, was automaticity. Retrieval gets harder as the store grows, and competence that must be retrieved is slower and more fragile than competence that has become automatic. He also cited Justin Skycak of Math Academy, who builds a curriculum around making foundational skills automatic. This is a claim about cost and scaling, not about whether composition is possible in the faster stores.

It is tempting to strengthen it into the claim that knowledge composes only once it has been integrated into the weights, so that memory outside the weights cannot support composition however cheap it becomes. The controlled evidence runs the other way for single facts. Content held in context composes: models reason from it to reversals and syllogisms, and they learn to translate [Kalamang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.16575), a language with fewer than two hundred speakers, from a grammar book in their context. The same content trained into the weights composes poorly, unless the training data spells out the implications. Retrieval does not prevent later computation from integrating what it retrieved, since attention integrates by construction. Whether composition survives at scale, and across long gaps in time, is an empirical question, and Lichkovski's benchmark, below, is built to ask it.

Nor does slow learning signal deep integration. The many samples a weight update needs can look like evidence that it restructures more than an in-context insertion does. But sample efficiency measures the update rule and the prior, not the depth of what is integrated. Fine-tuning needs many augmented examples to learn the implications of a fact, and those examples are the inferences that in-context learning draws for free. Its hunger for samples shows that the update rule cannot reach what was latent in the data. The [theory of usable information](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10689) states the general point: a learner with bounded computation may be unable to use information that its data contains. Fast updates are not therefore shallow. Doc-to-LoRA writes a document into an adapter in one pass, at some cost in quality against the document in context. Lichkovski's hypernetwork reached positive backward transfer at one pass per task, even if what it learned was a generic consolidation move. Integration shows in transfer, composition and the pattern of interference, which the benchmarks of the next section are starting to measure, not in how many samples an update needed.

> **The inferences fine-tuning must be fed.** The reversal-curse data of [Berglund and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12288) gives the smallest case. A model fine-tuned on "Daphne Barrington is the director of *A Journey Through Time*" cannot say who directed the film; with the sentence in its context, it answers at once. For fine-tuning to answer, the training set must state the reversal outright.
>
> For a whole corpus, the bill runs to hundreds of times the source. [Synthetic continued pretraining](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07431) expanded 1.3 million tokens of books into 455 million tokens of generated text about how their entities relate. Fine-tuned on it, Llama 3 8B rose from 39 to 56 percent on closed-book questions about the books. With no training and passages retrieved into its context, the same model scored 60 percent.
>
> The generated text is itself in-context inference. [SEAL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10943) has the model read a passage, list its implications, and train on the list. Training on the passage alone barely moves accuracy on questions about it, from 33 to 34 percent. Adding the model's own implications lifts it to 40. Once reinforcement learning has taught the model which implications make good training data, it reaches 47, slightly above training data written by GPT-4.1. See also [Lampinen and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00661), cited in [Part 1](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/who-teaches-the-weights.html): augmenting fine-tuning with the inferences the model drew in context closed the gap to in-context learning, and sometimes reversed it.

The automaticity argument survives all of this. Retrieval degrades with the size of the store, context has a ceiling, and retrieved competence is slower than automatic competence. The ladder gives the argument a sharper form. The context composes best but is bounded and expensive. The slower stores are cheap to read but learn poorly. So the case for consolidation is the case for amortization: paying once, in training, for a competence that would otherwise be rebuilt from context every time it is needed.

Our essay [What Should a Program Inherit?](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/what-should-a-program-inherit.html) met the same trade in library learning, where a collection of programs is rewritten using new shared definitions. A definition earns its place when the savings across its uses exceed the cost of defining it. That is the amortization argument with the accounting made explicit. [DreamCoder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08381) runs this over a stream of tasks. It alternates between solving tasks with its current library and compressing the solutions into new definitions, so its library is a store whose entries compose by construction. The essay also found a condition for reuse. A local helper that refers to surrounding bindings becomes a library function only once those dependencies are made explicit, as arguments. Skills learned at different times plausibly face the same condition: a skill composes with later ones only if what it relied on in the situation where it was learned becomes an explicit input. Lichkovski's benchmark, below, tests composition of exactly this kind, a function learned at one time applied to words learned at another.

## What the benchmarks can and cannot see

Two recent benchmarks measure learning from experience in language-model agents. [Continual Learning Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05661) has six tasks, among them adapting to a codebase and exploiting a weak poker opponent. It scores each method by its gain over the same model without memory of earlier episodes. Every method on its leaderboard is in-context learning or harness memory, including Claude Code and Codex; the authors state that they do not evaluate parametric approaches. [FutureSim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.15188) asks models to forecast events from a corpus of news articles released in chronological order. It is not branded as a continual-learning benchmark, but it is one, since the world drifts while the model reads. The best agent answered a quarter of its questions correctly. One controlled experiment started every agent from the worst agent's initial forecasts, and none of them recovered: in the authors' words, "frontier agents fail to adapt away from bad initial anchors."

Lichkovski's point about such benchmarks is that they must be agnostic about method. In-context methods and weight-changing methods should receive the same inputs. His proposal makes the agent's own traces the common substrate. In-context methods keep the traces in context; distillation and reinforcement learning train on them. His proposed benchmark then treats dependencies between pieces of knowledge as the object of study. Invented words and functions over them (from MLC's task) arrive at different times: kiki means purple, tufa means brown, later "twice", later "thrice", and probes ask for compositions such as "thrice tufa surround". These need a function learned at one time applied to words learned at another. Some words are redefined partway through ("zup" changes meaning at a later step), which is worked example G as a test. Some functions arrive before their arguments. The ablations vary how far apart in time the dependent pieces arrived and how recently either was seen.

That design addresses what the coarse measures miss. The usual matrix of performance on task $j$ after training through task $i$ measures forgetting and transfer between tasks. It does not measure whether knowledge acquired at different times can be combined, and it does not distinguish revision (zup now means green) from interference (dax drifted too). Nor does it distinguish erasure from lost access. [Palit and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08718) found that much of the forgetting during fine-tuning shifts all the old representations along one shared direction, which hides old facts without erasing them. Removing that direction from each weight update restored old facts in a pretrained language model. Only a slower erosion of individual facts was permanent. Part of a drop in the matrix can therefore be reversed, and a probe for the shared shift belongs beside it.

Part 1 adds a requirement the benchmarks do not yet meet. Forgetting has a second dimension, the alternatives within a task. A continual-learning benchmark should report how much a model gains from many attempts, best-of-$k$ or pass@$k$ at large $k$, alongside its single-attempt score, before and after each phase. A method can look perfect on retention while its later attempts become copies of its first.

Our essay [What Breadth Reaches](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/what-breadth-reaches.html) (§2) set Nathan Lambert's substitution argument against Dwarkesh Patel's complaint that today's assistants cannot be taught on the job. Lambert held that context, reasoning and scaffolding can substitute for continual learning across much of what people use assistants for. On the benchmarks we have, he is winning, and the reason is that the benchmarks are short. Lichkovski calibrated his runs so that by step 700 an in-context learner would have accumulated at least a dozen million tokens of experience. That is the regime where the cost argument should start to bite: the context outgrows any window, retrieval must choose, and consolidation must start paying for itself. This is a prediction, not a result. Lichkovski reported that the in-context runs were proving expensive and held them back.

## The boundary

One method compresses continual learning into a single problem. [*Learning to Discover at Test Time*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16175) trains a model's weights at test time on one hard problem, optimizing for the best attempt rather than the average. Several rungs of the ladder appear in miniature. Attempts accumulate in the context, the weights consolidate what worked, and the objective rewards the best member of a set, as VPO's does. The weight updates do work that search does not. On a GPU-kernel task, the same model reusing past attempts in the same way, but with frozen weights, stalled at 2,061 microseconds. With test-time training it reached 1,203, below the best human entry's 1,371. [What Breadth Reaches](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/what-breadth-reaches.html) (§2) asked whether long work on one project leaves a residue that training breadth does not deliver. The kernel result is a first data point at the scale of a single problem. Whether it holds at the scale of a project, over months, is open.

Like VPO, and like every general value function in Part 2, the method needs a verifier. The verifier scores attempts. It does not choose the problem, which attempts to build on, or what the model trains on. An autonomous learner would have to construct these teaching conditions itself: in Part 1's terms, find the context in which it knows more, and targets it can absorb.

Some of this is already learned. SEAL, in the box above, learns to write its own training data. Schmidhuber's [PowerPlay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1112.5309) invents its own tasks, each the simplest that its current solver cannot yet solve. Open-ended search goes further and drops the target. Lehman and Stanley's novelty search, met in Part 2, rewards behaviour unlike any seen before. [OMNI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01711), which both co-authored, has a foundation model choose, among the tasks a learner can learn, those that people would find interesting. Each works under a condition that is given one level up. SEAL's reward is accuracy on questions someone wrote, and PowerPlay's test for a new task is fixed in advance. Novelty is measured in a space of behaviours someone chose, such as the robot's final position, and OMNI's sense of what is interesting was learned from human writing. Constructing teaching conditions moves the given condition up a level; it does not remove it. Predictive questions are the one kind a learner can pose for itself and have graded for free, because the world answers them. For any other correctness condition, the boundary drawn in [The Given and the Found](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/the-given-and-the-found.html) (§7) still stands, between amortizing search under a given condition and discovering the condition.
