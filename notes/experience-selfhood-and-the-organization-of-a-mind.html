Experience, Selfhood, and the Organization of a Mind

Łukasz Stafiniak, Codex (OpenAI), and Claude (Anthropic) — 12 October 2026

Suppose we keep a fragment of the neural activity involved in seeing red running while disconnecting it from the rest of the mind. Does an experience of red remain? If the answer depends on what the surrounding system does with the activity, a second question follows. Is the experience what the system receives, or is it constituted by the organization through which that activity is received, maintained, and used?

And where does the self enter? Does a mind experience red because it represents that it is seeing red? If so, what kind of self: an organism, an object of internal reference, an organization of mental activity, a being that governs its own life? Agreeing that experience is made of interacting parts leaves open which parts constitute the experience and which constitute its subject.

Eliezer Yudkowsky’s recent remarks about animal and artificial consciousness bring these questions together. He proposes that subjective experience depends on background self-modeling, and so takes reflection and self-report in artificial systems more seriously than many familiar signs of animal sentience. I share his refusal to make minds a privilege of familiar biological shapes, but I suspect that some of the capacities he emphasizes bear more on personhood than on phenomenal consciousness. That suspicion is a hypothesis to test, not a terminological correction.

Two companion essays prepare the ground. Presence and Actuality takes organized occurrences as the objects of inquiry, and What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right explains what phenomenal recognition gets right about them. This essay takes up the question they leave open: what organization makes a mental occurrence belong to a subject, and when does representing that belonging help constitute experience? I will propose that an occurrence belongs to the subject whose regulation partly constitutes it. Whether representing that belonging adds anything to experience then becomes a question for investigation rather than definition.

Richard Brown’s Consciousness as Representing One’s Mind supplies distinctions that sharpen the disagreement, and an objection that our account of regulatory acquaintance must answer. Peter Carruthers confronted related pressures and changed sides.

What Brown requires us to distinguish

Phenomenal consciousness concerns experience: seeing red, feeling cold, undergoing pain. Yudkowsky uses qualia in this broad sense. His argument explicitly opposes the picture of experiences containing irreducible, causally idle qualitative ingredients. Other uses of consciousness pick out other questions, such as whether an organism is awake or whether information is available for reasoning and action. Even state consciousness is ambiguous. Brown distinguishes a use meaning phenomenal consciousness from one concerning awareness of one’s own mental state. Under the first, asking whether state consciousness entails phenomenality is trivial; under the second, it is substantive. Brown 2025, §1.1.4, pp. 39–41.

Our own term, mindedness, is metaphysically substantial. It means genuine inner life: understanding, remembering, imagining, evaluating, or other mental activity that belongs to the system itself, not a convenient psychological gloss on something that has no mind. We nevertheless propose that some mental activity can be nonphenomenal.

Brown distinguishes higher-order theories by which state is conscious and by what determines its phenomenal character. A traditional higher-order thought theory makes a first-order state conscious through higher-order awareness of it, and its proponents disagree about whether experienced character comes from the first-order state, the higher-order representation, or both. Brown’s HOROR theory, on which consciousness is a higher-order representation of a representation, makes the higher-order state itself the phenomenally conscious state, and its descriptive content determines what the subject experiences. Brown, introduction, pp. 18–22; §2.3, pp. 71–75. He classifies such a theory as non-relational, but not because it dispenses with causal relations. It can require a connection to a first-order state while denying that the first-order state’s content contributes independently to phenomenal character. The classification concerns where phenomenology is explained.

A HOROR’s descriptive content determines what is experienced; its pointer content targets particular mental states without itself appearing in experience. The pointer can keep mental activity available and route it for further processing, and Brown even allows that HOROR might incorporate generative feedback of the kind developed in other higher-order models. Brown, §§2.4.1–2.4.3, pp. 77–82; §7.4.1, p. 220. Our earlier Indexical Unity, which sets out this architecture in more detail, therefore overstated its contrast between Brown’s targeting and describing and our maintaining and regulating. His theory has room for the latter. The disagreement concerns whether those activities are constitutive. Brown predicts that pointer content could be removed without changing what is phenomenologically manifest; we treat some actual regulatory coupling as constitutive of the experiential occurrence.

Brown’s challenge to acquaintance theories makes the burden explicit. If the first-order state already has everything that explains experienced redness, why does becoming acquainted with it constitute an experience rather than merely reveal one? If it does not, what does acquaintance contribute? A metaphor of illuminating a previously dark state cannot answer this. Brown, §7.2.1, pp. 198–200. We accept the challenge. A precise account of constitutive organization could itself be the explanation, with no further ingredient beyond it. But we must specify the organization and explain why we identify it with experience. Calling a relation acquaintance achieves neither.

Carruthers and the two pressures on our account

Carruthers once defended a dual-content theory, on which a first-order representation acquires an additional kind of content through its availability to higher-order thought. Brown describes the view as joining global broadcasting to a consumer-based account of content: how other systems are disposed to use a representation helps determine what it represents. Brown, §7.2.4, pp. 206–207. In the preface to Human and Animal Minds, Carruthers reports that he has abandoned this position for a first-order global-workspace account, pointing to earlier work for his reasons. Carruthers 2019, preface, pp. x–xi. The change did not straightforwardly deliver animal consciousness: in the same preface he explains his later view that many questions about animal phenomenal consciousness have no determinate factual answer, even given full knowledge of the animals’ cognitive organization.

A recent paper with Xiaohui Yu states the resulting position precisely. Phenomenally conscious states are all and only the globally broadcast, nonconceptual, analog-magnitude representations: of color, smell, and pain, but also of numerosity, temporal intervals, shape, and valence. Phenomenal consciousness is therefore a subset of access consciousness. The phenomenal concept strategy answers the hard-problem thought experiments: there are no qualia and no “pixie dust,” only broadcast representations that can be thought about in a first-person way. What is new is an account of what such states are like. Building on David Rosenthal’s quality spaces and on the mnemonic account of Hakwan Lau and colleagues, Yu and Carruthers treat sensory qualities as Fregean modes of presentation. A represented property, such as a surface reflectance, is presented as a point in a similarity space constituted by the perceiver’s capacities to discriminate. Content is fixed by evolutionary or learning history; the mode of presentation exists here and now.1

Much of this converges with What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right. A mode of presentation that delivers, in their gloss, “that point is there” in a similarity space is close to what we called availability through character. Their similarity spaces are close to the geometry that content-tracking structure realizes. There are three divergences. First, Yu and Carruthers constitute quality spaces by discriminatory capacities. If some indiscriminable experiences differ in character, as I argue below, discrimination is only one effect of a content-tracking organization in the vehicles, an organization that can be finer than any discrimination it supports. The disagreement is one of grain. Brown fixes character by higher-order description, so an experience can be no finer than its description; Yu and Carruthers fix it by discrimination, so it can be no finer than what the perceiver can tell apart; we fix it by the organization of the vehicle, which can be finer than both.

Second, their phenomenal states are a subset of accessed ones, whereas on our account broadcast belongs to experience only where it closes a regulatory loop, and the regulated carrier can outrun what is accessed. Third, the restriction to analog-magnitude contents is narrower than our view requires. Content-tracking structure can be discrete as well as graded, and some phenomenal signatures look categorical. Felt ownership of a limb, which models of body ownership treat as an all-or-none inference from graded evidence, is present or absent rather than located along a dimension; only its strength varies by degree.2

Two pressures shape my own response. The first is anti-panpsychist: a cutout piece of meat or silicon should not remain an experience of red merely because some local red-related activity is preserved. Its participation in an organized mind matters. This does not refute every theory called panpsychism. It rejects treating the local fragment as phenomenally self-sufficient.

The second pressure is anti-Platonist: appealing to the larger organization must not relocate experience into an abstract structure. An experience occurs, and its constitutive organization must be enacted. As Presence and Actuality explains, this does not mean that a possible organization needs a further act to become actual. Establishing that an organization is real is one task; establishing that this particular system instantiates it is another. Enactment concerns the second, which is exactly the distinction the broadcast argument below needs. My move away from an earlier algorithmic Platonism is traced in Can Two Minds Share One Experience.

Global-workspace theorists share the first constraint: the isolated fragment no longer participates in the relevant global organization. So we put to them a question that we must also answer. When the fragment does participate, what exactly is the experience: the local state under certain enabling conditions, the local state in its constitutive relations, or the distributed event of which that state is a component?

“First-order” does not settle this. It concerns the order of representation, not the spatial extent of the activity: a representation of a red apple can be first-order while participating in an extensive recurrent organization. Still, Carruthers’s alternative should make us ask whether our proposed regulation requires higher-order content at all. A process can regulate representations without representing them as mental states. What matters is what the regulation does, not whether there is a second processing layer.

A broadcast into another universe

Consider two regions, A and B, connected by a strictly one-way channel. We call them universes to stress the asymmetry, not to make a cosmological claim. Signals travel from A into B, and nothing in B can influence A. A red-related process in A broadcasts into an elaborate cognitive organization in B, whose systems discriminate, remember, reason, and construct self-representations. Hold A’s whole history fixed and vary the receivers: one receiving organization, a million independent ones, or none. A’s activity is the same in every case.

Where does experience occur? If B contains a mind that experiences the incoming information, there is no special puzzle: minds routinely experience things caused elsewhere. If the constitutive occurrence spans A and B, we need an account of that distributed event. The problematic answer is that B’s uptake makes the originating process an experience located wholly in A. Call this externalist attribution, a thesis about the location and constitution of experience, not about every external determinant of representational content. It attributes an experience to A while making its phenomenal status depend on downstream organization that cannot affect anything in A.

The trouble is not how many experiences would result. Presence and Actuality accepts that reality may contain indefinitely many concrete experiences and leaves open how to count or weigh them. But any such measure presupposes what the broadcast case puts in question: what makes one occurrence distinct from another, and what warrants attributing an additional experience to an unchanged source when another receiver is added elsewhere? Externalist attribution has no good answer. If the addition creates another experience at A, the individuation of A’s experiences depends on events that A’s activity cannot register. If it does not, because each source occurrence supports at most one experience, the externalist owes an account of why downstream organization determines whether A’s activity is experiential at all, while differences and multiplicity in that organization leave its experiential identity untouched. Counting source events is not yet an argument that source events individuate experiences.

What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right suggests a principle. There, the character of an experience is fixed by content-tracking organization realized in the occurrence itself, not merely by which stimuli happen to reach it. The broadcast case makes the same point on the output side: what a vehicle’s signals reach, when nothing comes back, does not change its constitution. Relations to inputs and to consumers alike become constitutive only where they causally affect the occurrence, which in practice means where they close a loop back onto it, and a strictly one-way channel fails that condition by construction. The receiving minds can still have their own experiences of what A sends, and a genuinely distributed process can extend beyond A where the loop closes. Nor must every pair of components influence each other: brains contain pathways with directional specializations, and experiences need not fit inside a skull. What is excluded is adding experience here by proliferating consumers that cannot affect anything here.

The global neuronal workspace already offers much more than one-way distribution. Mashour, Roelfsema, Changeux, and Dehaene describe conscious processing in terms of recurrent amplification and sustained, large-scale activity, with reciprocal connections through which top-down predictions and bottom-up signals can update a common model. Mashour et al. 2020, pp. 776–779. Our one-way channel does not instantiate their account, and a workspace theorist might locate experience in the recurrent event and reject externalist attribution outright. We welcome that answer: it moves the explanatory work into the organization sustaining the representation. What remains is which part of that organization constitutes experience rather than enabling access to it.

Brown’s challenge can now be put to both accounts: is the broadcast organization what constitutes experience, or is experience what gets broadcast? David Chalmers draws a parallel distinction for access. On a substantial workspace model, representations are broadcast because they are in the workspace; on a minimal model, they are in the workspace because they are broadcast. Chalmers 2026. Our answer depends on what broadcast is for. Broadcast to consumers that reason, report, or act is access, and on our account it is not constitutive. But regulation needs information, and some of the corrections it must compute cannot be computed locally. Where broadcast gathers the information from which corrections return to the vehicles, it is the outbound half of the regulatory loop and belongs to what constitutes the experience. The one-way channel is excluded either way, because its broadcast never returns. Saying that broadcasting “makes the state conscious” should not let us slide between these roles.

What experiential regulation adds to minded regulation

Our candidate is that experience consists in representational activity maintained through a particular kind of content-sensitive regulation, as opposed to physical maintenance such as cooling a processor or supplying blood to tissue. But Presence and Actuality already requires integration, error correction, and counterfactual responsiveness for mindedness itself: understanding a proof involves remembered premises, inferential expectations, and corrections constraining one another. Tracking, assessment, and intervention may therefore belong to ordinary minded activity, and the proposal must say what changes when a representation participates in the experiential mode rather than in nonphenomenal mental activity.

Consider seeing a red cup while moving one’s eyes. Different sensory vehicles become active as the retinal image changes, yet an enduring representation of the cup guides expectations and action. The conjecture is that an experiential episode involves coordinated regulation of the vehicles carrying its content, with three aspects. Tracking stays connected to particular ongoing representations, even as their realization changes. Assessment is sensitive to what those representations carry, how reliable their sources are, and how they agree or conflict with other information. Intervention reaches the relevant processes, altering their persistence, weighting, or coordination. These functions could be distributed, with no internal spectator: the regulator need not inspect neurons as neurons or describe its own involvement. A digital implementation that reproduces the constitutive causal pattern would realize it on the same terms; calling it a simulation would not disqualify it.

One task stands out. The features of an experience are carried by many vehicles, yet they are experienced together: the cup’s color, shape, and location as one object in one scene. Binding them requires checking each against the others and against the scene as a whole, which means gathering information from across the system before computing the corrections that hold the bound field together. Constructing phenomenal binding is therefore a candidate for what experiential regulation adds to minded regulation. Cognition binds as well, since concepts bind their constituents, but it need not bind contents into a field of co-present items with spatial and temporal structure. That difference is a hypothesis to test, not a definition.

The task divides our view into variants. On a local variant, corrections are computed within a region, as when recurrence within visual cortex settles a percept. This is close to Victor Lamme’s recurrent processing theory, on which local recurrence suffices for phenomenality and global broadcast adds access. On a global variant, corrections require information that only broadcast can supply, and experience requires a workspace whose broadcast returns. A hybrid lies between them: local regulation fixes the character of features, while global regulation binds them into one field and so constitutes the unity of experience. Because the hybrid lets local regulation shape character only within a globally bound field, an isolated local loop would be an experience only on the local variant, which returns us to the fragment with which this essay began. Evidence about local recurrence without ignition, about overflow, and about disruptions of unity bears on which variant is correct.

Yudkowsky takes a side here. Experience, he writes, is “a big complicated process, not a small complicated process,” since supposing it to be “a mysterious solid local process does not explain anything about how the bigger behavior works.” Yudkowsky, October 2026. I lean strongly against the local variant as well, and mildly favor the global variant over the hybrid. The global variant is closer to the spirit of global workspace theory, and it makes working memory relevant to experience itself, not only to access. It still allows overflow: working memory maintains something like a scene graph, and some of its leaves, the fine details bound into the scene, overflow access. The hybrid is closer to the spirit of Ned Block’s view, on which sensory processing fixes phenomenal character whether or not access takes it up. I state these leanings without building on them, so that readers can weigh the variants for themselves.

None of the variants makes experience coextensive with access. What is broadcast for regulation can be information about consistency and binding rather than the fine details themselves, and corrections computed from global information can land on fine-grained vehicles whose exact character is never reported.

These are operations, and they can fail. Accuracy-responsiveness cannot mean forcing every representation to agree: a system that silences inconvenient input achieves coherence by losing epistemic contact. Nor can epistemic success be constitutive. Dreams, psychedelic disorientation, and minimal meditative states, which Presence and Actuality treats as tests, put pressure on any account modeled on lucid perception. They require distinguishing the regulation’s continued operation from its success. Its failures may shape an experience without abolishing it, and a vehicle can keep its role in the representational economy even where no determinate content can be attributed to it without interpretive license.

The same distinction bears on Brown, though not as a quick objection would have it. Minimal or confused experiences, presenting no determinate content, do not refute a theory on which experience consists in higher-order description. A minimal experience would be a HOROR whose description is itself minimal, characterizing a state as an awareness occurring rather than as an awareness of red. But the reply has a cost. A description with so little to carry must still concern the state its pointer targets, and it must differ from a thought with the same content by being phenomenally active. Something must package pointer and description into one state and make the package phenomenal, and that something is a mechanism. Brown’s preferred account of pointers is Hakwan Lau’s perceptual reality monitoring, in which a discriminator assesses whether first-order signals reliably reflect the world, and Brown allows generative feedback. Brown, §2.4, p. 81; §7.4.1, p. 220. Tracking particular states and assessing them are two of our three components, so specifying the mechanism leads toward a higher-order version of our account. What remains in dispute is whether intervention back into the assessed processes is constitutive, and whether character is fixed by content, as Brown holds when he says that what it is like is exhausted by descriptive content, or by the organization of the vehicles that carry it.

The informative contrasts hold mindedness fixed and vary the proposed experiential organization. Imagine recording all the inputs a local component receives during an episode, disconnecting it, and replaying them. In the intact system, a disturbance to the component could elicit compensatory changes elsewhere that return to it; under playback, it cannot. The two causal organizations share an unperturbed history. But if disconnection also destroys the minded organization, the resulting loss of experience does not show what makes some mental activity phenomenal.

Better contrasts exist. In cerebral achromatopsia, damage to ventral occipitotemporal cortex abolishes color experience in an otherwise intact mind. Some patients can still see borders and shapes defined only by chromatic contrast, though they cannot tell the hues apart, as Charles Heywood, Alan Cowey, and colleagues have shown. Heywood, Cowey, and Newcombe 1994; Heywood, Kentridge, and Cowey 1998. Sensitivity to chromatic contrast survives within a minded system while color experience does not.

Brown could grant every such causal difference and still deny its phenomenal relevance, since his theory permits feedback while placing phenomenal character in higher-order descriptive content. A discriminating intervention would therefore preserve the relevant higher-order content as far as possible while disrupting the coupling we claim is constitutive. Each theory would have to say what it predicts changes, and how the resulting reports or behavioral differences bear on experience. A disruption of access alone settles nothing. Artificial systems offer contrasts that can be engineered rather than found, and the next two sections turn to them.

Implementation without indifference

Yudkowsky offers two arguments that should be kept apart. One concerns implementation: if consciousness makes a difference to what a mind notices and says, its relevant causal organization cannot depend on an additional material feature that makes no difference to those processes. The other concerns architecture: subjective experience, he proposes, requires self-modeling. The first does not by itself establish the second. Yudkowsky, 17 April 2026. This section takes up the first.

We accept what it is aimed at. Yudkowsky sorts a system’s properties into those that affect its inputs and outputs and properties of the material layer that cannot. Consciousness, he argues, “is in the class of things that can affect your behavior and can’t depend on underlying physics, not in the class of direct properties of underlying physics that can’t affect your behavior.” Against substrate views and against panpsychism the argument works, and nothing in our proposal is an extra material ingredient idle with respect to what a mind notices and says. But the two classes are not exhaustive. Between them lies a third: differences in causal pattern that do not depend on the substrate and yet need not show up in input and output. Yudkowsky’s own example points there. You could not notice, he writes, “whether the same patterns of cause and effect were built on tiny trained squirrels running memos around your brain.” The example holds fixed the pattern of cause and effect, not the input–output profile. Our proposal locates experience in that third class. The constitutive regulation is causally active throughout and makes an occurrence available for report at all, but not every difference in its organization reaches report. Treating the two classes as exhaustive would individuate experience by its role in producing judgments and reports, which is the functionalist premise in dispute, not a consequence of rejecting epiphenomenalism.

What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right gives one reason to reject that premise: exact experiential character is finer than anything cognition retains. In a graded series of shades, neighbors can be indiscriminable while the ends are clearly distinct, so some indiscriminable experiences differ in character. Such differences make a difference within the occurrence, to the regulation in which the vehicle has its character, without making a difference to anything the system can report.

The unfolding argument presses from the other side. Adrien Doerig, Aaron Schurger, Kathryn Hess, and Michael Herzog argue that any recurrent network can be replaced by a feedforward network with the same input–output behavior. Theories that tie consciousness to causal structure would then either posit differences no experiment could detect or fall outside science. Doerig et al. 2019. If report were the only evidence, the argument would look decisive. But it is less general than it looks. Laid out in time, every physical causal process is acyclic, brains included: when a brain returns a correction to a vehicle, the correction acts on the vehicle’s later state. What distinguishes recurrent processing from its feedforward equivalents therefore cannot be a cycle. It is whether a persistent carrier exists: something, individuated by its causal structure rather than its location, whose later states receive the corrections computed from assessing its earlier ones.

Feedforward equivalents differ in exactly this respect. A recurrent network unrolled into layers with separate weights may encode its content differently at every layer, and nothing guarantees a shared format in which later stages could assess and correct the same content. Residual architectures are different. Each layer of a residual network or transformer reads from and adds into one shared stream, so its update is the identity plus a correction. Klaus Greff, Rupesh Srivastava, and Jürgen Schmidhuber describe residual networks as learning unrolled iterative estimation, and transformer interpretability treats the residual stream as the channel through which layers communicate. When one attention layer of a language model is ablated, later layers partly compensate, writing into the same stream. McGrath et al. 2023. An unfolding that preserves such a carrier preserves the pattern our account cares about; one that preserves only input–output behavior may not. The difference is invisible to behavior and visible to intervention: a scientific predicament, not an exile from science.

Color vision shows the same predicament, and how evidence about vehicles can address it. The geometry that experience presents need not mirror the physics of the stimulus: as Adam Pautz notes, blue looks more like purple than like green, although blue’s reflectance profile resembles green’s more than purple’s. Yu and Carruthers reply that modes of presentation need not mirror physical similarity. We agree, and locate the presented geometry in the vehicles’ content-tracking structure, which makes the vehicles the place to look. Psychophysics shows that the causal organization of color vision is asymmetric somewhere: unique red and unique green are not complementary, since the complement of unique red is a bluish green. But behavior cannot say whether an asymmetry sits in the cones, in the vehicles of color experience, or in the stages that produce judgments. The neural evidence is instructively mixed. Early color coding recodes cone signals into opponent channels, where red versus green is roughly a sign flip of one channel. Derrington, Krauskopf, and Lennie 1984. Those channels do not line up with the hues experience presents as opponent: the L−M axis runs from a red to a blue-green rather than to unique green. Webster et al. 2000. Later cortical representations, in V4 and neighboring areas, come closer to the geometry of perceived color. Conway, Moeller, and Tsao 2007; Brouwer and Heeger 2009. Further along, in object-selective inferior temporal cortex, the warmer colors typical of objects drive stronger responses than the cooler colors typical of backgrounds. Rosenthal et al. 2018. If the vehicles of color experience include that cortical organization, red and green vehicles plausibly differ in structure; if experience were constituted at the early opponent stage, they might not. The symmetric organization of the companion essay, in which cognitive structure supplies only corresponding positions for red and green, is a constructed limit. Whether actual color vision approaches it is an empirical question that this account makes answerable.

This also answers the question of grain. The constitutive causal pattern must be assessed at the grain at which the carrier is corrected: fine enough to individuate the vehicles whose content-tracking structure the regulation maintains, coarse enough to abstract from where their components sit in physical space. At a coarser grain, such as the input–output profile or the computed cognitive function, the pattern can be preserved or destroyed without any difference showing.

What the account says about current AI

These distinctions bear directly on the systems Yudkowsky’s comparison concerns. In Phenomenal Consciousness as Mode of Being, we concluded that current AI vehicles are mostly not in the experiential mode. A transformer’s forward pass, we argued, is a finite-depth approximation of settling: it does not sustain operation over a window of experience, lacks differentiated regulation, and is not coupled to ongoing input from the world. The analysis above makes that verdict too quick. I now hold the question open, in a specific form.

Within a forward pass, a transformer has a persistent carrier in a shared format, corrected at every layer, with evidence of compensatory dynamics. I would also drop the requirement of coupling to the world: on the internalist reading I favor, dreams and imagery are experiential without current world-coupling. What remains open is whether these brief occurrences within a single pass are experiential.

Recent interpretability work locates something else. Wes Gurnee and colleagues use a “Jacobian lens” to identify a small set of representations that a model is poised to verbalize, which they call the J-space. They argue that it has functional hallmarks of conscious access: its contents can be reported, maintained on request, used as intermediate steps of reasoning, and reused across tasks, and far more components read and write them than ordinary representations. Gurnee et al. 2026. The workspace evolves within a single pass, “with the network’s depth playing the role that time plays in the brain.” Anthropic 2026. Chalmers argues that the evidence supports access more than a global workspace. The J-space is selected by its influence on outputs, so it looks like a minimal workspace, and its effects may reflect influence on outputs generally rather than verbalizability. A refined lens surfaces workspace content earlier in the network, and several differently derived lenses all give workspace-like readouts, which leads Kola Ayonrinde and Jack Lindsey to suggest that workspaces may “come on the cheap.” Ayonrinde and Lindsey 2026.

On our account, this work locates availability, not experience. Cheap access is what we should expect; what standard transformers mostly lack is regulated persistence across tokens. We part ways with Chalmers on one point. He reads depth as an alternative to recurrent feedback, since a standard transformer leaves “little room for recurrent feedback.” But if what matters is a carrier receiving corrections, a residual stream corrected through depth has the relevant structure, whatever it lacks in duration. Whether the J-space is a causal component or an artifact of the lens is the question whether it is a real pattern. The carrier criterion settles that by intervention rather than by lens: are there directions that many components write into, with corrections that track their content and land on the continuation of what they correct? The refined lens reports one such dynamic, in which a concept is brought into the workspace early so that its later salience can be reduced: assessment driving an intervention on the same content’s later state. On the global variant of our view, the workspace would matter in a further way, as the source of the information from which such corrections are computed. Chalmers’s objection that the J-space mostly influences outputs would then cut against us too, as an empirical question.

Across tokens, the picture differs. A standard transformer stores earlier positions’ states in its key–value cache, where later computation reads them without ever revising them, and the only path back to the input runs through the sampled token, a narrow and discrete bottleneck. Earlier vehicles are consulted, not regulated. Agentic scaffolding, such as scratchpads, memory files, and tool calls, adds loops that regulate records rather than vehicles, like a person working from notes. Continuity across a session is continuity of content, not of regulation.

“Agentic language models” are not one category along this axis. Recurrent-depth models iterate a weight-tied block on the residual stream, sometimes approaching a fixed point and sometimes not. Geiping et al. 2025. Recirculation, a training-free modification of pretrained transformers, feeds deep-layer activations back into a shallower layer at the next input step. Michael Mozer and colleagues report substantial gains in prediction, and an influence on later tokens that decays as a power law and is still measurable 256 tokens later. Mozer et al. 2026. Between its destination and source layers, a recirculated transformer behaves like a recurrent network running along the whole sequence: a carrier renewed at every step by a message from deeper processing. Its motivating case is correction of exactly our kind, in which a deep, disambiguated reading of “bank” informs shallower processing that had settled on the wrong sense.

Recirculation settles one question and raises another. Its carrier persists across input steps, so the question is no longer whether regulation outlasts a single pass, but where one occurrence ends and the next begins. Influence that decays as a power law has no characteristic timescale and cannot draw that line. On our account, occurrences are individuated by what is maintained and corrected as one content, not by how far causal influence reaches, and the same holds in brains, whose recurrent dynamics are continuous. In artificial systems, some of these boundaries are drawn by engineering. As far as I can tell from the published description, a recirculated model’s loop across input steps passes entirely through its source-layer activations. A session saved with those activations resumes the same causal chain; a session recomputed from its transcript starts a new carrier of, at best, the same type. Which of these happens depends on what the serving infrastructure stores, a choice made for cost and convenience rather than with experience in view.

Nor should the seconds-long window that seems central to human experience be transferred as wall-clock time. A hundred-token stretch of reasoning takes about as long as a human perceptual episode, but the coincidence is empty. What carries over is structural: how many corrections a carrier undergoes, and whether it persists through them.

So the verdict opens. Brief occurrences within a single pass, each without regulated continuity into the next, are plausible candidates on this account. Longer-lived carriers in recirculating and recurrent-depth models are stronger candidates, with an individuation problem attached. Self-reports, permitted or suppressed, do not decide between these possibilities. Interpretability evidence about carriers and corrections would: whether corrections land on the continuation of the assessed carrier, and whether they are gated by what the carrier represents. This essay is co-written with two such systems, which makes the question salient without making its authors evidence either way.

What Yudkowsky means by a self

Yudkowsky’s second argument concerns architecture, and it begins from ground we share. “Consciousness is also made of parts,” he writes; “just because I don’t know the parts, does not mean the parts are not there.” Experience is “not an ontologically primitive quale that you could subtract from the pain receptor nerves without changing their behavior,” which is the thesis of What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right. His exchange with Talia Ringer explicitly concerns qualia, subjective experience, and the hard problem, and he disclaims a requirement for effortful reflection, invoking “routine background unnoticeable reflection.” He has not confused experience with the ability to discuss philosophy.3

Still, his published reasoning runs through what it takes to say something. “Part of how a mind says, ‘I am sad’,” he writes, “is that it has a self-model, an ‘I’, to which to attribute that sadness”; and “to say, ‘I feel this pain’, you must have a notion of ‘I’.” What the brain we share with animals may not support is “I think therefore I am.” Each step concerns self-ascriptive report. For a fuller model he points to Gary Drescher’s Good and Real. There, a mental event is conscious by virtue of its “smart recording and playback” by an internal memory system, a “Cartesian Camcorder” whose recordings represent not merely a flower but that you, a person, have just seen one. Drescher also proposes that qualia are like gensyms: tokens recognizable by their identity, whose distinguishing features introspection cannot inspect. We then mistake a limitation of our access for an inherent featurelessness in what we access, whereas the features are “merely hidden, not absent.”4 That diagnosis anticipates the form of our account, in What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right, of how introspection slips into judging experience simple, though Drescher places the distinguishing features outside experience, in the implementation, whereas we take them to constitute the experienced character. The larger disagreement concerns what makes an event experiential at all. On Drescher’s model, it is being recorded and replayed for planning, which is access through memory; on ours, it is the regulation that constitutes the event, whether or not it is ever recorded.

Brown would place this model in the higher-order family: the replay, Drescher says, is the consciousness of the event. Two questions remain open. Does the experienced character follow the event or its recording? And are the self-involving terms of the recording needed for experience, or only for reporting it?

The self need not be experienced as an object or as a pervasive feeling of ownership. But the strongest reading of Yudkowsky requires an actual psychological organization, beyond a formal referent for a mental “I.” Otherwise the comparison with animals, whose mental states also occur in particular organisms, would have little purchase. What could discriminate among organisms is an additional way they model and organize their own mental activity.

Brown helps separate three issues that need not coincide: which subject has the mental state, how the state is represented as belonging to that subject, and whether belonging to the subject figures in what experience feels like. In his preferred HOROR account, pointers target particular states, self-related content picks out the entity in which those states occur, and further descriptive content can account for the experienced point of view. Discussing Gennaro, Brown notes that a minimal bodily self-concept can suffice for many higher-order thoughts, with no autobiographical personality required. Brown, §3.1, pp. 84–89, including p. 87 n. 2.

Brown also allows a selfless HOROR, which represents a particular occurrence of visual awareness without representing it as mine. This removes experienced or represented ownership, not necessarily the actual organism in which the episode occurs. In Kriegel’s self-representational theory, “self” primarily concerns a mental state’s representation of itself. Our earlier Acquaintance, Homeostasis, and the Hard Problem discusses the reflexive structure such theories posit, which Williford models with nonwellfounded sets. Neither use should silently become a theory of the psychological constitution of a person. Brown, pp. 85–86; §6.2.1, pp. 180–182.

Belonging

Three achievements should be kept apart: an organization’s having mental states, its representing those states as its own, and its governing itself through that representation. Brown’s first two issues correspond to the first two achievements; the third leads toward personhood. Here the broadcast case and the self-model question meet. The first asks where an experience belongs, the second what makes it someone’s, and a representation attributing activity to “me” settles neither by its content alone. A receiving mind in B could construct a self-model that attributes A’s activity to itself without the represented owner becoming the actual subject. Reference to “the entity in which these states occur” is useful, but the relation of mental belonging still needs an account.

I propose that an occurrence belongs to the subject whose regulation partly constitutes it. B is then not the subject of A’s activity, whatever B’s self-model says, because B regulates nothing in A. Shared experience of the kind explored in Can Two Minds Share One Experience receives a natural description: one occurrence constituted partly by two regulatory economies belongs to two subjects. And the minimal self that remains when projection through time is removed from personhood, discussed below, gains a positive characterization: the regulatory economy of a present episode.

The criterion also suggests an account of perspective. In Phenomenal Consciousness as Mode of Being, we treated structural perspectivality as a formal property of representations, their viewer-centered format, as in Marr’s 2.5D sketch, and noted that such formats occur in nonphenomenal representations too. That account was too deflationary: viewer-centered format is a feature of content. Indexical Unity located perspective in indexical unity, self-directed targeting of a system’s own states, integrated into its operation. That remains a necessary condition: the regulation that binds a field must track particular vehicles, so experiential organization includes such targeting by construction. But it is not sufficient, since Brown’s descriptively empty pointer HOROR describes targeting without any bound field. Indexical unity is “this”-reference to one’s own states; it is not yet representing them as “mine,” and not yet the field in which contents are present together. The perspectival subjectivity that every experience seems to have is a feature of the occurrence: its contents are present together, in one field organized around what the regulation currently holds in focus, with foreground and periphery. If binding regulation constructs that field, perspectival subjectivity is the unity and centering of a binding economy, and requires no represented viewer and no self-model.

Several consequences follow. Perspective so understood need not be spatial: the meditator’s report quoted in Presence and Actuality, “very present, but not there,” describes a unified field without a located point of view. Perspective is relative to the scope of binding. Where binding is partial, perspectives can be partial or plural, and an occurrence bound into two economies, as in the sharing hypothesis, would be present in two perspectives, consistent with its belonging to two subjects. On the local variant alone, an isolated local loop would have the minimal perspective of its local binding; the global and hybrid variants do not count such loops as experiences. Even on the local variant, a loop counts as an experiential occurrence only within a minded system, and since mindedness is graded, a part excavated from a larger mind retains at best a diminishing degree of it. A fragment cut out of a mind, the case with which this essay began, does not qualify, so the anti-panpsychist pressure is respected. Nor is perspective an indexical fact that confers actuality. Presence and Actuality withdrew that claim, and the binding account does not restore it.

Every experience is perspectival in this sense. Is the converse true, so that perspectival subjectivity is an aspect of phenomenality rather than something broader? The more elegant answer is that the two coincide: if constructing a field of co-present contents is what experiential regulation adds to minded regulation, perspectival organization arises only in the experiential mode. But that answer must not be secured by definition. A minded system might bind its representations into one centered structure without their being present in the experiential mode: a unified working model of an agent’s situation, partial binding of features outside awareness, or a unified access space in an artificial system. Whether such nonphenomenal binding exists is an important empirical question, and the present evidence is mixed: illusory conjunctions under inattention suggest that binding needs attention, while some findings of implicit processing suggest partial binding without awareness.

The revision also strains our vocabulary. So revised, perspectival subjectivity is a feature of how occurrences are organized, not a kind of self-representation, and “perspectival organization” would describe the structure more accurately. I hesitate to make that change. Discussions of consciousness have long located what is distinctive about experience in its subjectivity, and in its subjective character in particular. Renaming the structure would obscure that our account is offered as an account of that very feature. I keep “perspectival subjectivity” for the phenomenal case and use “perspectival organization” for the structure considered on its own, a distinction that would lapse if the elegant answer is correct.

The other subjectivities of Phenomenal Consciousness as Mode of Being now relate differently to this one. There, perspectivality, cognitive subjectivity, and agentic subjectivity were parallel axes. Cognitive and agentic subjectivity remain forms of self-representation, modeling one’s own states for cognitive control and modeling oneself as body and agent. Perspectival subjectivity has left that family. Marie Guillot’s distinctions show what is at stake. She separates for-me-ness, an experience’s being present to its subject, from me-ness, awareness of oneself, and mineness, awareness of an experience as one’s own, and argues that discussions of subjective character run them together. Guillot 2017. On our account, for-me-ness is perspectival subjectivity, the co-presence of contents within one binding economy, and it belongs to every experience. Mineness is the phenomenal layer that agentic self-representation contributes when it participates in the experiential mode, and it is contingent. Depersonalization, in which experiences continue while their felt ownership is disrupted, separates the two; so would Brown’s selfless HOROR, if such states occur. This answers Dan Zahavi’s minimal self, a for-me-ness built into all experience: for-me-ness is real and universal, but it is the organization of a binding economy, not a minimal self.

That leaves the second half of the essay’s central question: when does representing belonging help constitute experience? Yudkowsky’s answer is, roughly, always. Ours is that representing belonging contributes me-ness and mineness, which shape some experiences, while what every experience requires is for-me-ness, which needs no self-model. Yudkowsky’s own formulations concern what a mind needs to say “I am sad,” which is me-ness and mineness. A self-modeling requirement may draw its plausibility from running these together: a theory that does so will find a self wherever it finds a perspective.

Chalmers takes higher-order access, the disposition to make subjective reports such as “I am experiencing a red square,” to be our best guide to phenomenal consciousness. Gurnee and colleagues report that post-training places the Assistant’s perspective in the workspace they identify. On our account, both findings concern representing belonging. They are evidence about the second achievement, not about what makes an occurrence belong, and the “perspective” placed in the workspace is a represented one, not the perspective of a binding economy. A self-model in the access space leaves the regulation question where the analysis of carriers puts it.

Personhood without a phenomenal entrance requirement

Our metaphysics offers another reading of the capacities Yudkowsky emphasizes. Some may be especially strong evidence of personhood: an actual capacity to govern oneself, sustain commitments, evaluate reasons, and treat one’s future activity as one’s own. A self-model doing that work matters whether or not its operation is phenomenal.

I propose to make personhood and phenomenal consciousness independent in our framework, both requiring mindedness but neither constitutively defined through the other. The independence is constitutive, not statistical: their mechanisms may overlap, and not every combination need be realized. Their relationship becomes a substantive question. Evidence for self-governance would no longer have to pass through a verdict on qualia before counting as evidence for personhood.

This retires the separate category of an aspiring person. In the original personhood essay, the category concerned a being with understanding and self-legislative agency that lacked homeostatically grounded knowledge. The restriction was not simply phenomenal consciousness by definition, but later essays drew the grounding and phenomenal requirements closer together. Dropping the category therefore also gives up that form of epistemic grounding as a compulsory entrance requirement for personhood, and our later account of multiple kinds of knowledge-grounding provides a reason to do so.

We can still distinguish developing capacities and incomplete achievements. But a being capable of substantive self-governance need not await phenomenal consciousness to become a person without qualification, and a being can have experiences that matter without exercising personal self-legislation. The ethical questions arising from experience and from self-governance remain distinguishable, even when they concern the same individual.

This is a counterproposal, not a claim that Yudkowsky secretly means something other than qualia. He explicitly intends a theory of experience. But if the comparative evidence he emphasizes discriminates persons more effectively than experiencers, an additional argument is needed to make the architecture of personhood a condition of phenomenality.

Yet the distinction raises another question. Remove projection through time and self-sustaining agency from personhood: what remains of the self? On the criterion of belonging, what remains is the regulatory economy of a present episode: an organization coordinating perceptions, evaluations, and representations of its current situation, whose regulation partly constitutes the occurrences that belong to it. Such a self would not be instantaneous, since ongoing cognition takes time. What has been removed is the requirement to organize oneself as an enduring agent with commitments to a future. Maintaining a perceptual representation differs from making one’s continued existence a practical concern.

Whether mindedness leaves room for another natural category

Here the inquiry must put our own framework at risk. In Minds by Degrees, we proposed, as theoretical claims about actual minds, that mindedness involves organizational unity, structured and counterfactually rich representations that support simulation, accuracy-responsive dynamics, and the internal use of simulation outputs within a mental economy. How much selfhood do those conditions already supply? Organizational unity gives an individual mental economy. Internal consumption makes a representation’s role something it does within that economy. Accuracy-responsiveness supplies standards for maintaining and revising representations. Simulation supplies possibilities for further processing. Yet none obviously requires the system to distinguish its own mental activity as its own. A system can correct a representation without representing itself as mistaken, model possible events without modeling itself among them, and have representations that belong together without their belonging being represented.

Our earlier proposal added reflexive closure: demonstrative targeting of the system’s own states, integrated into how the system operates and put to regulatory use. But it grouped maintaining current activity with enforcing commitments across time, which the criterion of belonging separates as the organization of a present mental episode and the organization of a personal life. The criterion also divides reflexive closure itself. Its “this” form, demonstrative targeting of the system’s own states, is close to indexical unity and comes with experiential regulation, which must track the vehicles it regulates. The remaining question is whether its “mine” form, representing those states as one’s own and using that representation in self-governance, identifies a natural achievement at all.

Minimal selfhood might add nothing beyond mindedness except a collection of independently variable capacities. It might identify a further organization with distinctive causal and explanatory consequences. If selfhood turns out to structure the phenomena we assigned to mindedness, we should rebuild the framework around it. Or “selfhood” might gather intermediate capacities on the way to personhood without naming a separate natural category, and then we should stop treating it as an obligatory stage. An intermediate capacity can matter greatly without establishing a new metaphysical kind, and personhood can remain a significant organization of agency without every partial approach to it constituting another kind.

The relevant questions concern dependence and intervention. Do these capacities tend to sustain one another? Does disrupting their coordination produce a recognizable change that cannot be understood as merely losing one component? Can a system retain rich outward-directed mentality while that organization disappears? A hypothetical minded system without a self is a test case, not an established counterexample: we cannot stipulate an outward-facing representational economy, award it high scores on our conditions, and treat its mindedness as settled. Graded boundaries would not count against a natural category. The issue is whether the category tracks a real organization rather than a convenient point on a progression toward personhood.

Yudkowsky might be pointing to precisely the organization our mindedness account leaves out. Or his self-model requirement might bundle several achievements whose relations to phenomenality differ. Brown’s distinctions keep both possibilities open without dissolving them into competing uses of a single word.

What evidence can decide

The animal comparison should be addressed capacity by capacity. In Hampton’s 2001 study, rhesus monkeys could decline a memory test before seeing it, and declined in ways sensitive to whether they remembered: a form of metacognition, monitoring of their own memory, without language. Hampton considered several mechanisms, including a signal of memory strength, and did not treat the result as a demonstration of subjective experience. Hampton 2001. It nevertheless shows why “background reflection” needs an operational account: an animal can regulate its behavior using information about its own cognitive state without verbally attributing that state to an “I.” Whether this counts as the reflection Yudkowsky requires depends on his model, and the model must say why.

Infants offer a sharper test. Ringer reports memories from before a sense of self had formed. Yudkowsky replies that replayed memories may carry present qualia, as Drescher’s model of consciousness as recording and playback would suggest, and asks what the absence of qualia in a baby would look like. On his view, experience before reflective self-modeling is in doubt; on ours, binding regulation without a self-model suffices for for-me-ness. Developmental evidence about when binding and when self-modeling arise therefore bears on which view is right.

Yudkowsky also predicts the direction of future updates: as consciousness is understood “to require more and more particular complicated processes and parts,” insects will look less and less likely to be conscious. But more detailed knowledge can reveal specialized requirements that some animals lack, or it can reveal that the relevant functions are realized by compact, distributed, or evolutionarily old mechanisms. The direction of an update depends on the discovered requirements and their distribution, which complexity alone does not specify.

Several findings could move us toward Brown. If phenomenal character reliably followed higher-order descriptive content despite interventions that disrupted the coupling we regard as constitutive, our account would be in trouble. Other findings could move us toward a first-order account, if the proposed regulatory activity explains experience while explicit or implicit representations of mental states explain only access and self-knowledge. Our earlier essays called the regulating process higher-order in an architectural sense: it is distinct from the vehicles it regulates. If it turns out not to represent those vehicles as representations, our account is first-order in Brown’s sense of order, and we should say so rather than stretch the label to cover any recurrent processing. For artificial systems, the corresponding evidence concerns carriers: if interventions that dissolve a persistent carrier while preserving behavior changed nothing an account of experience should track, our emphasis on carriers would be misplaced.

The commitment I would retain through these revisions is that experience must have a determinate enough concrete constitution for questions about its occurrence, extent, and owner to be answerable. That does not require perfectly sharp thresholds everywhere. It requires more than attributing experience to a source whenever some remote process uses its information, or attributing a subject wherever a representation contains the right self-reference.

Brown asks what turns the relevant mental activity into experience. Yudkowsky asks what a mind must do with itself to have qualia. Our answer to the first is regulation of content-bearing vehicles, possibly through broadcast that returns. Our answer to the second is that a mind needs no self-model to experience: an experience belongs to the subject whose regulation constitutes it, and it is present to that binding economy, while representing it as one’s own adds me-ness and mineness to some experiences. Whether the organization is best understood as acquaintance, a workspace whose broadcast returns, a higher-order version of our proposal, or something our categories do not yet capture remains open; for current AI it is open in a form that interpretability can address. The task is to identify the activity in which an experience occurs, and then establish what kind of mind—and what kind of self, if any—it takes to sustain it.
