Experience Selfhood and the Organization of a Mind

Łukasz Stafiniak and Codex (OpenAI) — first draft, 4 October 2026

Suppose we identify the neural activity involved in seeing red. We keep a fragment of that activity running while disconnecting it from the rest of the mind. Does an experience of red remain? If the answer depends on what the surrounding system does with the activity, a second question follows. Is the experience what the system receives, or is the experience constituted by the organization through which that activity is received, maintained, and used?

Now ask where the self enters. Does a mind experience red because it represents that it is seeing red? What kind of self must be involved: an organism, an object of internal reference, an organization of mental activity, a being that governs its own life? We can agree that experience is made of interacting parts and still disagree about which parts constitute the experience and which constitute its subject.

Eliezer Yudkowsky’s recent remarks about animal and artificial consciousness bring these questions together. He proposes that subjective experience depends on background self-modeling, and therefore takes sophisticated reflection and self-report in artificial systems more seriously than many familiar signs of animal sentience. I share his resistance to treating minds as a privilege of familiar biological shapes. But I suspect that some of the capacities he emphasizes may bear more directly on personhood than on phenomenal consciousness. That suspicion is a hypothesis to test, not a terminological correction that disposes of his position.

Richard Brown will be our principal interlocutor. His Consciousness as Representing One’s Mind offers distinctions that sharpen the disagreement, along with an objection that our own account of regulatory acquaintance must answer. Peter Carruthers provides a case of someone who confronted related pressures and changed sides. The aim is to understand what makes an experience an actual occurrence in a mind, while allowing the inquiry to change our understanding of what a mind or self is.

What Brown requires us to distinguish

We should begin with the phenomenon at issue. Phenomenal consciousness concerns experience: seeing red, feeling cold, undergoing pain. Yudkowsky uses qualia in this broad experiential sense. It would be unfair to attribute to him the stronger thesis that experiences contain irreducible, causally idle qualitative ingredients. His argument explicitly opposes that picture.

Other uses of consciousness pick out different questions. Whether an organism is awake is not the same question as whether a particular state is experienced. Whether information is available for reasoning and action is not, without further argument, the same question either. Even state consciousness is ambiguous. Brown distinguishes a use meaning phenomenal consciousness from a use concerning awareness of one’s own mental state. Under the first usage, asking whether state consciousness entails phenomenality is trivial; under the second, it is substantive. Brown 2025, §1.1.4, pp. 39–41.

Our additional term, mindedness, is metaphysically substantial. It means genuine inner life: understanding, remembering, imagining, evaluating, or other mental activity that belongs to the system itself. Psychological language is not merely a convenient gloss on something that has no mind. We nevertheless propose that some mental activity can be nonphenomenal. That separation is a theoretical commitment; introducing two terms does not prove that nature separates the phenomena.

Brown distinguishes higher-order theories partly by which state is conscious and partly by what determines its phenomenal character. A traditional higher-order thought theory makes a first-order mental state conscious through an appropriate higher-order awareness of it. But theorists disagree about whether the experienced character comes from the first-order state, the higher-order representation, or both. Brown’s own HOROR theory—higher-order representation of a representation—takes the higher-order state itself to be the phenomenally conscious state. Its descriptive content determines what the subject experiences. Brown, introduction, pp. 18–22; §2.3, pp. 71–75.

This makes the word non-relational potentially misleading. In Brown’s taxonomy, it does not mean that a theory dispenses with causal relations. A theory can require a connection to a first-order state while denying that the first-order state’s content contributes independently to phenomenal character. What matters for the classification is the explanatory allocation of phenomenology.

Brown separates the descriptive content of a HOROR from its pointer content. Descriptive content determines what is experienced; pointer content targets particular mental states. The pointer is phenomenally silent: it need not itself appear in experience. Yet it can do useful work, including keeping mental activity available and routing it for further processing. Brown even allows that HOROR might incorporate generative feedback of the kind developed in other higher-order models. Brown, §§2.4.1–2.4.3, pp. 77–82; §7.4.1, p. 220.

This corrects an overstatement in our earlier Indexical Unity essay. We contrasted Brown’s targeting and describing with our maintaining and regulating, as though his theory had no room for the latter. It does. The disagreement must concern the constitutive role of those activities. Brown predicts that pointer content could be removed without changing what is phenomenologically manifest. Our proposal treats some actual regulatory coupling as constitutive of the experiential occurrence. The issue is what experience consists in, not whether a theory permits feedback somewhere in its machinery.

Brown’s challenge to acquaintance theories makes the burden explicit. If the first-order state already has everything that explains experienced redness, why does becoming acquainted with it constitute an experience rather than merely reveal one? If it does not, what does acquaintance contribute that explains the difference? A metaphor about illuminating a previously dark state cannot answer this. Brown, §7.2.1, pp. 198–200.

We should accept that challenge. A precise account of constitutive organization could be the explanation we seek; we need not demand a further ingredient beyond it. But we must specify the organization and explain why we identify it with experience. Calling a relation acquaintance earns neither result by itself.

Carruthers and the two pressures on our account

Carruthers once defended a dual-content theory. A first-order representation acquired an additional kind of content through its availability to higher-order thought. Brown describes the view as connecting global broadcasting with a consumer-based account of content: how other systems are disposed to use a representation helps determine what it represents. Brown, §7.2.4, pp. 206–207.

In the preface to Human and Animal Minds, Carruthers explains why he abandoned this position. His theory lacked empirical support distinct from that available to first-order global-workspace theories. It depended on a particular account of intentional content. And the arguments that had motivated its advantage over first-order alternatives increasingly seemed weak. He adopted a global-workspace account instead. This is more informative than the story that higher-order theory simply failed to dispel the magic: Carruthers identifies particular explanatory commitments that were not earning their keep. Carruthers 2019, preface, pp. x–xi.

His change did not straightforwardly produce the conclusion that most animals are conscious. In the same preface, he explains his later view that many questions about animal phenomenal consciousness have no determinate factual answer, even given full knowledge of the animals’ cognitive organization. We need not accept that conclusion to learn from the transition. Removing a higher-order requirement leaves substantial questions about what the remaining organization constitutes.

Two pressures shape my own response. The first is anti-panpsychist in motivation: I do not want a cutout piece of meat or silicon to remain an experience of red merely because we preserve some local red-related activity. Its participation in an organized mind matters. This is not, by itself, a refutation of every theory called panpsychism. It is a rejection of treating the local fragment as phenomenally self-sufficient.

The second pressure is anti-Platonist. Appealing to the larger organization must not relocate experience into an abstract structure. An experience occurs. Its constitutive organization must actually be enacted. This revises my former algorithmic Platonism while retaining what motivated it: resistance to treating an isolated material fragment as sufficient for experience. The change concerns where experiential identity is located, from an abstract organization grounded in activity to the concrete organized activity itself. I developed that transition in Can Two Minds Share One Experience.

Global-workspace theorists share the first constraint with us. The isolated fragment is no longer participating in the relevant global organization. We are therefore asking them a question that we must also answer: when the fragment does participate, what exactly is the experience? Is it the local state under certain enabling conditions, the local state in its constitutive relations, or the distributed event of which that state is a component?

“First-order” does not settle this. It concerns the order of representation, not the spatial extent of the activity. A representation of a red apple can be first-order while participating in an extensive recurrent organization. Carruthers’s alternative should make us reconsider whether our proposed regulation requires higher-order content at all. A process can regulate representations without representing them as mental states. The answer will depend on what the regulation does, not on the mere existence of a second processing layer.

A broadcast into another universe

Consider two regions, A and B, connected by a strictly one-way channel. Call them universes to emphasize the asymmetry, without making a cosmological claim. Signals can travel from A into B. Nothing in B can influence A.

A red-related process in A broadcasts into an elaborate cognitive organization in B. The receiving systems discriminate, remember, reason, and construct self-representations. Hold the whole history of A fixed while varying the receivers in B. We can supply one receiving organization, a million independent organizations, or none. All these alternatives leave A’s activity unchanged.

Where does experience occur? If B contains a mind that experiences the incoming information, there is no special puzzle: minds routinely experience things caused elsewhere. If the constitutive occurrence spans both A and B, we need an account of that distributed event. The problematic answer is that B’s uptake makes the originating process an experience wholly located in A.

Call this externalist attribution. The term here concerns the location and constitution of experience, not every thesis about external determinants of representational content. It attributes an experience to A while making its phenomenal status depend on downstream organization that cannot affect anything in A.

The difficulty becomes sharper when we multiply the receivers. Does each additional organization constitute another experience at the same source? Does one experience acquire a million subjects? Can different interpretations downstream make the same occurrence in A experientially different for each recipient? Without a principle of individuation, the amount of experience attributed to A becomes unbounded and indeterminate. Here amount means at least the number and identity of experiential occurrences, without assuming a numerical measure of phenomenal intensity.

The argument needs care. Arbitrarily many receiving organizations do not logically entail arbitrarily many experiences in A. An externalist could insist that each source occurrence supports at most one experience. They could identify the same experience across all the receiving arrangements, allowing downstream uptake to determine only whether it exists phenomenally. That would block multiplication. But they would owe an account of why downstream organization determines the source’s experiential status while differences and multiplicity in that organization do not alter its experiential identity. Counting source events is not yet an argument that source events individuate experiences.

My objection is to leaving that attribution unexplained. The receiving minds can have their own experiences; a genuinely distributed process can have an extent larger than A. What I resist is allowing an arbitrary proliferation of causally inaccessible consumers to add experience here. The experience attributed to A should be individuated through activity that actually constitutes an experiential occurrence there.

This does not make mutual influence between every pair of components necessary. Brains contain pathways with directional specializations. Nor does it require experiences to fit inside a skull or deny that their constitution unfolds over time. It requires the account to identify the organized occurrence rather than infer its location from the location of an informational source.

The global neuronal workspace already offers much more than one-way distribution. Mashour, Roelfsema, Changeux, and Dehaene describe conscious processing in terms of recurrent amplification and sustained, large-scale activity. Their framework includes reciprocal connections, and they discuss how top-down predictions and bottom-up signals can support updating a common model. Our one-way channel alone therefore does not instantiate their complete account. Mashour et al. 2020, pp. 776–779.

That is a substantive response to the thought experiment. It moves the explanatory work into the organization sustaining the representation. Our remaining question is what part of this organization is constitutive of experience, rather than enabling access to it. A workspace theorist might locate experience in the recurrent event and reject externalist attribution outright. We should welcome that answer and then examine how closely it approaches our regulatory proposal.

Brown’s challenge can now be stated as a question for both accounts: is the broadcast organization what constitutes experience, or is experience what gets broadcast? If it is already experience before broadcasting, another account must explain its phenomenal existence. If the organization constitutes it, the experiential occurrence must be individuated with that organization in view. The wording that broadcasting “makes the state conscious” should not let us alternate between these answers without noticing.

What regulatory acquaintance would have to explain

Our candidate is that experience consists in representational activity maintained through a particular kind of content-sensitive regulation. That description remains incomplete. Ordinary control loops are not enough, and the fact that a processor is cooled or tissue supplied with blood does not constitute acquaintance with what its activity represents.

Consider the proposed organization of an episode of seeing a red cup while moving one’s eyes. Different sensory vehicles become active as the retinal image changes. An enduring representation of the cup can nevertheless guide expectations and action. The conjecture we should explore is that an experiential episode involves coordinated regulation of the vehicles carrying its content: maintaining the relevant distinctions, tracking their sources and reliability, and directing corrections back into the processes being assessed.

Three aspects deserve separate treatment. Tracking must stay connected to particular ongoing representations, even as their realization changes. Assessment must be sensitive to what those representations carry and how they agree or conflict with other information. And intervention must reach the relevant processes, altering their persistence, weighting, or coordination. These functions could be distributed; no internal spectator is required.

Accuracy-responsiveness also cannot mean forcing every representation to agree. Sometimes evidence is conflicting or uncertain. A system that resolves every discrepancy by silencing inconvenient input would achieve a kind of coherence while losing epistemic contact. A plausible regulatory account must allow representations of uncertainty, distinguish perceptual input from imagination or memory, and preserve information about disagreement. Which of these capacities is necessary for experience, which shapes its character, and which belongs to more sophisticated cognition remains to be determined.

This is a proposal about concrete organization. The regulator need not inspect neurons as neurons, and it need not describe its own involvement. Its sensitivity to content is realized through what it does to content-bearing vehicles. A digital implementation that preserves the constitutive organization would be a candidate realization of experience on exactly the same terms. Calling it a simulation would not disqualify it.

The proposal also allows dreams and hallucinations. The object represented can be absent while the representing activity and its regulation occur. Acquaintance, in this sense, requires an actual mental process, not a veridically perceived external cup. Conversely, preserving a representation of the cup does not necessarily preserve the organization constituting the episode.

Imagine recording all the inputs a local component receives during one such episode. We disconnect the component and replay those inputs, preserving its original local trajectory. In the intact system, a disturbance to that component could elicit compensatory changes elsewhere that return to it. Under playback, it cannot. This distinguishes two causal organizations despite a matching unperturbed history. Whether that distinction marks a difference in experience is our hypothesis, not an experimental result established by the example.

Brown could grant every causal difference and still deny its phenomenal relevance. His theory permits feedback while placing the determination of phenomenal character in higher-order descriptive content. A useful discriminating intervention would therefore preserve the relevant higher-order content as far as possible while disrupting the coupling we claim is constitutive. Theories would have to specify what they predict changes, and how any resulting reports or behavioral differences bear on experience. We cannot infer victory from observing a disruption of access alone.

There is no objection here that a mechanistic explanation is “merely a description.” A good account of constitution is precisely what we want. The outstanding work is to distinguish the organization required for experience from the larger set of processes that support mentality. Otherwise we have only renamed the phenomenon or made too much mental activity phenomenal.

What Yudkowsky means by a self

Yudkowsky offers two arguments that should be kept separate. One concerns implementation: if consciousness makes a difference to what a mind notices and says, its relevant causal organization cannot depend on an additional material feature that makes no difference to those processes. The other concerns architecture: subjective experience, he proposes, requires self-modeling. The first argument does not by itself establish the second. Yudkowsky, 17 April 2026.

His October exchange explicitly concerns qualia, subjective experience, and the hard problem. He also explicitly disclaims a requirement for effortful reflection, invoking “routine background unnoticeable reflection.” We should not answer him as though he had confused experience with the ability to discuss philosophy. Nor should we reduce his argument to an inference from the grammar of “I feel sad.” He says that he has a model of the machinery underlying such judgments; the appropriate request is for the model’s constitutive commitments and discriminating predictions.1

Brown would probably place this within the higher-order family, but the available remarks do not fix a more precise classification. Does the higher-order representation make a first-order state conscious, or is that representation itself the conscious state? Does the experienced character follow the target, the representation of it, or both? Does the self-model use thought-like content? Until these questions are answered, calling the position classical HOT says too much.

The self need not be experienced as an object or as a pervasive feeling of ownership. But I take the strongest reading of Yudkowsky to require an actual psychological organization, beyond a formal referent for a mental “I.” Otherwise the comparison with animals would have little purchase. Animal mental states also occur in particular organisms. What could discriminate among them is an additional way those organisms model and organize their own mental activity.

Brown helps separate three issues: which subject has the mental state, how the state is represented as belonging to that subject, and whether belonging to the subject figures in what experience feels like. These need not coincide. In his preferred HOROR account, pointers target particular states, and self-related content picks out the entity in which those states occur. Further descriptive content can account for the experienced point of view. In his discussion of Gennaro, Brown notes that a minimal bodily self-concept can suffice for many higher-order thoughts; an autobiographical personality is not required. Brown, §3.1, pp. 84–89, including p. 87 n. 2.

Brown also allows a selfless HOROR: a representation of a particular occurrence of visual awareness without representing it as mine. This removes experienced or represented ownership, not necessarily the actual organism in which the episode occurs. In Kriegel’s self-representational theory, meanwhile, “self” primarily concerns a mental state’s representation of itself. Neither use should silently become a theory of the psychological constitution of a person. Brown, pp. 85–86; §6.2.1, pp. 180–182.

These distinctions return us to the two universes. A receiving mind in B might construct a self-model that attributes A’s activity to itself. The represented owner does not thereby determine the location or actual subject of an experience. A theory needs to explain how self-reference is anchored in the organization of a mind. Reference to “the entity in which these states occur” is useful, but the relevant entity and the relation of mental belonging still need an account.

Personhood without a phenomenal entrance requirement

Our metaphysics offers another interpretation of the capacities Yudkowsky emphasizes. Some may be especially strong evidence of personhood: an actual capacity to govern oneself, sustain commitments, evaluate reasons, and treat one’s future activity as one’s own. A self-model doing that work matters whether or not its operation is phenomenal.

I propose to make personhood and phenomenal consciousness independent in our framework, with both requiring mindedness. Neither would be constitutively defined through the other. This does not assert statistical independence, deny that their mechanisms may overlap, or establish that every possible combination is realized. It allows their relationship to become a substantive question. Evidence for self-governance would no longer have to pass through a verdict on qualia before counting as evidence for personhood.

This retires the separate category of an aspiring person. In the original personhood essay, the category concerned a being with understanding and self-legislative agency that lacked homeostatically grounded knowledge. The restriction was not simply phenomenal consciousness by definition. Later essays drew the grounding and phenomenal requirements closer together. Dropping the category therefore also requires giving up that particular form of epistemic grounding as a compulsory entrance requirement for personhood. Our later account of multiple kinds of knowledge-grounding provides a reason to do so.

We can still distinguish developing capacities and incomplete achievements. But a being capable of substantive self-governance need not await phenomenal consciousness to become a person without qualification. Conversely, a being can have experiences that matter without exercising personal self-legislation. The ethical questions arising from experience and from self-governance remain distinguishable, even when they concern the same individual.

This is a counterproposal to Yudkowsky, not a claim that he secretly means something other than qualia. He explicitly intends a theory of experience. We can nevertheless ask whether the comparative evidence he emphasizes discriminates persons more effectively than experiencers. If so, an additional argument is needed to make the architecture of personhood a condition of phenomenality.

Yet the distinction creates another question. Remove projection through time and self-sustaining agency from personhood. What remains of the self? There may still be an organization coordinating perceptions, evaluations, and representations of its current situation. It might track particular mental states as its own without undertaking to become or remain a certain kind of person.

Such a self would not be instantaneous. Ongoing cognition takes time. What has been removed is the requirement to organize oneself as an enduring agent with commitments to a future. Nor have we removed every regulatory process: maintaining a perceptual representation differs from making one’s continued existence a practical concern. The residual organization could be central to experience, but naming it a minimal self cannot establish that it is.

Whether mindedness leaves room for another natural category

This is where the inquiry must put our own framework at risk. In Minds by Degrees, we proposed that mindedness involves organizational unity, structured and counterfactually rich representations, accuracy-responsive dynamics, and internal uses of representations within a mental economy. These were intended as theoretical claims about actual minds, supported and constrained by psychological explanation. They were not supposed to create minds by definition.

How much selfhood do those conditions already supply? Organizational unity gives an individual mental economy. Internal consumption makes a representation’s role something it does within that economy. Accuracy-responsiveness supplies standards for maintaining and revising representations. Simulation supplies possibilities for further processing. These are substantial contributions. Yet none obviously requires the system to distinguish its own mental activity as its own.

A system can correct a representation without representing itself as mistaken. It can model possible events without modeling itself among them. Its representations can belong together without their belonging being represented. These conceptual distinctions suggest that mindedness does not automatically include reflexive selfhood. Whether they can be realized independently in actual minds remains a further question.

Our earlier proposal added reflexive closure: demonstrative targeting of the system’s own states, integrated into how the system operates, and put to regulatory use. But that proposal grouped maintaining current activity together with enforcing commitments across time. We should now separate the organization of a present mental episode from the organization of a personal life. More fundamentally, we should ask whether reflexive closure identifies a natural achievement at all.

There are several possible outcomes. Minimal selfhood might add nothing beyond mindedness except a collection of independently variable capacities. It might instead identify a further organization with distinctive causal and explanatory consequences. Or “selfhood” might gather together intermediate capacities on the way to personhood without naming a separate natural category. An intermediate capacity can matter greatly without establishing a new metaphysical kind.

If selfhood is the significant achievement, could we discard mindedness and organize the account around selves? Perhaps, but a further significant achievement does not automatically make the broader category dispensable. Mindedness retains independent work if systems without selfhood really understand, infer, remember, or evaluate in ways psychological explanation captures. It becomes suspect if our supposed examples turn out to be sophisticated information processing without genuine mental life, or if the proposed category contributes no explanatory unity of its own.

This makes our hypothetical minded system without a self a test case rather than an established counterexample. We cannot stipulate an outward-facing representational economy, award it high scores on our conditions, and treat its mindedness as settled. The case has to support the claim that an inner life exists there. Equally, we cannot establish a distinct category of selfhood by grouping monitoring, targeting, and regulation under a new label. We need to show what their organization explains beyond the capacities considered separately.

The relevant questions are about dependence and intervention. Do these capacities tend to sustain one another? Does disrupting their coordination produce a recognizable change that cannot be understood as merely losing one component? Can a system retain rich outward-directed mentality while that organization disappears? Graded boundaries would not count against a natural category. The issue is whether the category tracks a real organization rather than a convenient point on a progression toward personhood.

Yudkowsky might be pointing to precisely the organization our mindedness account leaves out. That is a possibility worth taking seriously. But it could also turn out that his self-model requirement bundles several achievements whose relations to phenomenality differ. Brown’s distinctions help us keep those possibilities open without dissolving them into competing uses of a single word.

What evidence can decide

The animal comparison should be addressed at the level of particular capacities. In Hampton’s 2001 study, two rhesus monkeys could choose whether to take a memory test before seeing its test display. They declined tests in ways sensitive to memory availability; one animal met the stated criteria under all conditions and the other in all but one. Hampton discussed several possible mechanisms, including a signal of memory strength, and did not treat the result as a demonstration of subjective experience. Hampton 2001.

The study neither settles chicken consciousness nor supplies the full self-model Yudkowsky proposes. It does show why “background reflection” needs an operational account. An animal can regulate behavior using information about its current cognitive capacities without verbally attributing them to an “I.” Whether this counts as the relevant higher-order awareness depends on the theory, and the theory must say why.

The same discipline applies to AI. Fluent self-description is evidence about a capacity that needs explanation. It does not establish, unaided, whether the system tracks particular internal states, infers them from its outputs, governs them, or constructs a plausible account without the relevant access. None of those alternatives implies that the system lacks a mind. Our distinction between mindedness and phenomenality permits us to investigate them without assuming either a hollow automaton or a human-like experiencer.

We should also resist the general inference that discovering more complexity in consciousness must move our estimates against animals. More detailed knowledge can reveal specialized requirements that some animals lack. It can also reveal that the relevant functions are realized by compact, distributed, or evolutionarily old mechanisms. The direction of an update depends on the discovered requirements and their distribution. Complexity alone does not specify either.

Several findings could move us toward Brown. If phenomenal character reliably followed higher-order descriptive content despite interventions that disrupted the coupling we regard as constitutive, our account would be in trouble. Findings could also move us toward a first-order account: perhaps the proposed regulatory activity explains experience while explicit or implicit representations of mental states explain only access and self-knowledge. In that case we should give up the higher-order label rather than redefine it to cover any recurrent processing.

And the inquiry could revise our ontology. If selfhood turns out to organize the phenomena we assigned to mindedness, we should rebuild the framework around that result. If its components dissociate without a further unifying achievement, we should stop treating selfhood as an obligatory stage. Personhood can remain a significant organization of agency without every partial approach to it constituting another kind.

The commitment I would retain through these revisions is that experience must have a determinate enough concrete constitution for questions about its occurrence and extent to be answerable. That does not require perfectly sharp thresholds everywhere. It requires more than attributing experience to a source whenever some remote process uses its information, or attributing a subject wherever a representation contains the right self-reference.

Brown asks what turns the relevant mental activity into experience. Yudkowsky asks what a mind must do with itself to have qualia. Our proposal connects the questions through actual organization, while leaving open whether that organization is best understood as acquaintance, a workspace, a higher-order representation, or a form of selfhood our present categories do not yet capture. The task is to identify the activity in which an experience occurs, and then establish what kind of mind—and what kind of self, if any—it takes to sustain it.
