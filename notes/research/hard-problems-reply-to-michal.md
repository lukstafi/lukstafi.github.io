# Reply to Michał's "Whether There Is a Well-Defined Hard Problem" (draft Substack comment)

Post: https://michalryszardwojcik.substack.com/p/whether-there-is-a-well-defined-hard (27 Oct 2026). Transcript: `notes/private/MRW-hard-problems.txt`.

---

Michał, the bit is a good instrument. I'd like to answer it on its own terms: grant it, see what it costs, and find which premise excludes it. In short, our identity premise and our structuralism exclude it; the grain ordering does not. And the cost of admitting it falls on the very datum your esse intuition starts from.

**What we postulate and what we derive.** Several of GoLem's objections, and your remark that substrate-independence is a conjecture, treat as conclusions what the essay takes as premises. The essay postulates three things. The first is the identity: an experience is a representational vehicle in its constitutive organization, at the grain that its regulation respects. The second is an empirical ordering: our physics resolves that grain. The third is structuralism: no categorical natures and no extra ingredients. The identity comes from [*What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right*](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/what-phenomenal-intuition-gets-right.html), and even there it is a hypothesis about constitution, not a theorem. So yes, substrate-independence is a conjecture. It is part of the first postulate.

The tie to representation is the part of the identity we have argued for least, and probably the part you would press next. Our reasons are abductive. Experience presents something as being some way, even in a dream where nothing answers to it, and a representational vehicle can do that while its object is absent. The tie survives failure too: in disorganized dreams the vehicle keeps its role in the representational economy even where no determinate content can be attributed to it ([*Presence and Actuality*](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/presence-and-actuality.html), first section). Regulation alone is not enough, since thermostats, cooling systems and blood supply regulate without experience. What sets the experiential case apart is regulation responsive to representational distinctions. Your cogwheel worry points the same way. A moment presents a scene, not abstract modulations, and a fine-grained substrate mode with no content-tracking role would leave that unexplained. None of this is a derivation.

**The derivation.** What the essay derives, it derives from these postulates. You asked for the reasoning as a deduction, and GoLem's quotient reconstruction gets most of it. Write ~C, ~E and ~P for sameness under cognition, under exact experience, and under physics. The essay makes two claims about grain. Cognition does not fix experience: ~C is not contained in ~E. Physics does: ~P is contained in ~E. The second claim is the first two postulates combined. As GoLem notes, it alone excludes physical zombies.

The first claim does its work elsewhere: it explains why zombies are conceivable all the same. A phenomenal concept is recognitional, so it can distinguish only what the subject can discriminate. Its content factors through ~C. Its referent lives at the grain of ~E. So no phenomenal concept can say which physical organization it refers to, and nothing in the subject's concepts links a phenomenal predicate to a physical one. Holding the physical description fixed while subtracting experience is then consistent in thought, though not in the theory. This step separates us from the usual phenomenal concept strategy. There, the isolation of phenomenal concepts is posited; here, it is derived from the gap between ~C and ~E.

"Solved" then means this: given the postulates, there are no physical zombies, and the identity itself predicts the impression that there could be. Like water = H₂O, the identity would be necessary if true, yet known only a posteriori. Your bit is a fair way to show that it is doing the work.

You are also right that we don't own "the hard problem." The essay picks one question under the umbrella: whether anything in the world answers to the sense that a physical story leaves something out. A functionalist who identifies experiential character with discrimination can keep asking why there is experience, but nothing in the world would answer to the question. That is the essay's dilemma, and it states a price, not a refutation.

**What does the bit do?** Your variants differ in what b responds to. So ask of any candidate b whether its difference shows up in the organization of the occurrence.

If b makes a difference to how the vehicle is regulated, it is part of that organization at a finer grain. A carbon-chauvinist bit that tracks chemistry the regulation depends on is of this kind. It is your earlier suspicion of fine substrate-dependence, and it would move the grain of experience below where we put it. On our account, where that grain lies is an empirical question, so this bit refines the theory rather than gating it.

If b makes no difference to the occurrence, it is a gate. God's inscrutable will is a gate, and so is the safeguard against excruciating pain, and so is the bit in your one-universe version. Only a gate is a genuine alternative to the identity, so the rest of this comment is about gates.

**"Her cognition would pick it up anyway."** This is your sharpest observation, and I think it turns against the gate. When b = 0, Mary's construction succeeds, her regulation runs, and she reports with full confidence that she now knows what red is like. Nothing is present. Now look at b = 1. Her judgment is produced by the organization exactly as before. The experience, now present, plays no part in producing it, since the judgment would be the same if it were absent. So her judgment is true by luck, as GoLem notices in passing. Under a gate, no one's judgment about their own experience is knowledge, in either world. (If b reached cognition by some other path, Mary could tell the difference, and your case would not arise.)

Self-knowledge is also the smaller part of what experience does for knowledge. Phenomenal concepts are coarse, and judgments like "I am seeing red" are a thin layer on top. On our account, the regulation that constitutes experience assesses the reliability of sources, checks vehicles against one another and binds them into a scene. What experience lets us know is first of all the world: that the cup is red, more than that I am seeing red. A gate takes this away too. Nothing that experience could add to perception differs between the two worlds, so under a gate experience is idle in every epistemic respect.

Sydney Shoemaker made this argument against absent qualia in 1975. Chalmers's fading-qualia argument has the same shape, and he uses it to conclude that any system with our fine-grained functional organization has our experience. So the champion of the hard problem accepts something close to the substrate-independence you call a conjecture, on roughly these grounds. Chalmers does keep a way out: acquaintance constitutes phenomenal knowledge without causing the judgment. But then that knowledge never reaches what you write or what GoLem reads, which is all cognition.

**Is this illusionism?** Yes, of the b = 0 world. And we do not deny that illusionism can be true of some systems. *What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right* has a section on where strong illusionism would be true. A system can reproduce our cognitive function, phenomenal judgments included, while lacking the regulated carrier we take to be constitutive. A recurrent process might be unfolded into a feedforward cascade, or a component might replay a recorded input instead of taking part in the loop that would correct it. Such a system is a functional zombie, and the illusionist's account is the correct account of it: it deploys phenomenal concepts, and nothing satisfies them. Physical zombies are impossible on our view. Functional zombies are possible: an unfolding that dissolves the carrier would be one. And among today's artificial systems, some may talk of experience without having it. So the situation your bit describes at b = 0 is one our theory allows, and one we may already meet.

What we exclude is the gate's version of it, and the difference lies in what produces the judgment. On our account, a judgment about one's experience is knowledge when the occurrence it is about takes part in producing it, as a regulated carrier takes part in the processes that attend to it ([*What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right*](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/what-phenomenal-intuition-gets-right.html), "Available through its character"). A functional zombie produces the same judgment by another route, and that route is a physical difference one can look for. Under a gate there is nothing to look for, and even a true judgment is true by luck.

Language models show where our route condition and Chalmers's way out come apart. A model's phenomenal vocabulary was learned from human reports. It arrived the way Davidson's Swampman got his words, without the speaker ever having recognized anything with them. But the two cases differ. Swampman lacks a history and has the right route: his present organization produces his judgments. A language model has a history, but it is someone else's. Its talk of experience would be the same whether or not it has carriers. That is what gives the possibility that such systems lack experience its grip, and it is the source of many people's conviction that they do lack it: the talk that would be our evidence is explained without appeal to their experience. On Chalmers's way out, routes do not matter. A model with experience would know it all the same, and its talk would remain no evidence either way. On our account the question can be investigated. Do the model's own carriers, if it has any, take part in producing its phenomenal judgments, or does its learned model of human talk produce them alone? Experiments that inject states into a model and ask whether it notices them are a start.

Nor do we claim certainty. You wrote that, with the bit, our theory can only exclude experience and never affirm it with certainty. Even without the bit, it never affirms it with certainty. Phenomenal concepts confer knowledge without certainty, as perception does. Illusionism adds one more skeptical scenario to the familiar ones: from the inside, I cannot rule out being a functional zombie, any more than I can rule out dreaming. But the new scenario cuts in a different direction. Dreaming threatens what I know about the world and leaves intact that I seem to see, which was part of Descartes's bedrock. Being a functional zombie leaves my knowledge of the world in place, since a zombie's perception still reaches the cup by a route of its own. What it threatens is that part of the bedrock. It leaves that I think, since a functional zombie still thinks, and takes only that my seeming is phenomenal. Our epistemology does not rest there, which is why we can live with the scenario. The gate is different in kind. It does not make phenomenal knowledge uncertain; it makes it impossible.

**Categorical, or an extra ingredient?** You suspected we would dismiss the bit as a categorical substance. Not quite. The variable b can be as structural as you like, with its own dynamics. The trouble is what it switches. In both worlds every structure is present, including the organization and the character assigned to it. So the difference between experience present and experience absent is not a difference in any structure. That is what "no extra ingredients" rules out. It rules it out by the third postulate, which we still owe a defense of. GoLem is right that the grain argument does not do it.

**Esse and the table.** I agree with more of the opening of your dialogue than you might expect. Esse should not depend on something else's family of transformations, and the grain of experience doesn't: the occurrence's own regulation fixes it. The same point keeps experiences from multiplying with our descriptions, though without IIT's exclusion. An experience can have experiences as parts, since binding is itself a phenomenal process, and the parts bound by their own regulation may overlap and nest. Which experiences there are is then a fact about the locality structure of binding. A boundary drawn in a description does not create an experience. A boundary drawn in an empirical exercise, neuroscientific or phenomenological, that traces that structure may discover one. The left half of a visual field could turn out to be an experience in its own right. We also agree that the phenomenal now is the case to start from, and that its actuality is synchronic. Where our essays make working memory part of what binds a moment, they mean the technical sense: active maintenance within the thick present, not recall of earlier moments.

This does not restore counting, and your table worry deserves that admission. We also distinguish phenomenal unity, the span of a binding, from psychological unity, whether something constitutes a single broadcasting economy. The second underlies the first, since binding contents into one field needs global regulation. But an economy can hold nested experiences, perhaps even more than one field, and economies can overlap, as a monk's and his monastery's do in our monastery essay. The same processes can then fall under several experiences and under one subject or several, and "how many beings?" has no privileged answer. Our metaphysics does not need one. A count is needed in ethics, when we proportion moral consideration among nested experiences and overlapping subjects. There what matters is that a shared experience is not added once per owner. Our monastery essay already counted occurrences rather than ownerships. This is a different question from the reality measure that [*Presence and Actuality*](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/presence-and-actuality.html) left owed. That measure would weigh occurrences from beneath the grain of physics, much as Born weights weigh quantum branches, and everything nested in an occurrence would share its weight. I suspect this is part of what esse does for you: it would make acts of existence countable, and so let the facts settle the moral ledger. On our view the facts fix what occurs and how it nests, and the ledger is partly left to ethics.

Where we differ is whether anything more is needed once the occurrence is in place. On that question I think we are where *Presence and Actuality* left us. It is a recognized disagreement, with us on the permissive side, giving up the count. That essay asked for esse to be stated as a condition that can be examined: what it is, what it explains, and which cases it excludes. Your bit is a placeholder of that kind, and the argument above examines it as a gate. It does not settle esse as you intend it, since you say the placeholder is not yet your intuition. It does narrow the options. If esse makes a difference to the occurrence, it falls under the first case, as a finer grain. If it makes none, it inherits the gate's cost: the conviction that the present experience is actual would be produced whether or not esse were there.

**Everyone has their own hard problem.** Agreed, and GoLem put yours well: how can phenomenal actuality be known, if every cognitive manifestation of it could occur without it? Our answer is that it can be known only if actuality makes a difference to what produces the judgment. The identity guarantees that, and a gate forbids it. Even then, knowing that one's experience is actual is the least of what experience contributes to knowledge. So I read your hard problem as an argument for the identity rather than against it.
