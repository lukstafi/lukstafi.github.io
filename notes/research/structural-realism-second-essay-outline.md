## Seeds

- The hand-off from the first essay (SR1, "Hand-offs"): its first exclusion is categorical natures and its sixth is brute necessities, and both are argued here.
- The published position. "The identity of experience with that organization is necessary, but we know it a posteriori … On this account the necessity is not brute" (GRAIN:79). "Mary's predicament is a fact about human architecture, not about the world" (GRAIN:103). "Phenomenal recognition fixes its referent through presented character, and the referent's constitution is discovered, as water's was" (WPIGR:203).
- GRAIN already answers Chalmers's argument in a paragraph: "physical descriptions give us only structure and dynamics, and structure and dynamics entail only further structure and dynamics. We accept the premise and reject the conclusion. Experience is structure and dynamics at its own grain. What structural description cannot deliver is the recognitional mode of presentation, and that is a limit of concepts, not a gap in the world" (GRAIN:61). This essay is that paragraph argued against Alter's defence of the conclusion.
- The constructor of GRAIN:97–105, "reasoning by construction rather than by conceptual entailment", and the criterion for cheating at GRAIN:95.
- The account of introspection in WPIGR:87–141: one mistake, "the slip into simplicity", and presence finer than recognition.
- The author's answers of 16 Nov (SRD §8). Four carry the essay. "Some (non-indexical) truths are not structural, but all truths are reducible and all reductions are structural." "The Ruliad is structural in a strong sense." A Mary who has seen red can deduce from the physics that an alien's state is red as hers is: "yes, this follows from the sharing an experience essay." And the limit, a "leap of faith" about how far the account generalizes across experiences and experiencers.
- Alter's own statement of where the argument now rests. He grants that the missing-concept objection is hard to answer for the original Mary, and moves to an "experienced Mary" who has seen red, returned to the room, and is asked about an alien (CL 0:21–0:28).

## Thesis

Alter argues in three steps. Mary knows every structural truth and learns something on release, so some truths about experience cannot be deduced from the structural truths. There are no primitive necessities, so what cannot be deduced is not necessitated. So physicalism is false, or Russellian monism is true and the world has natures that structure leaves out.

The essay grants more of this than a physicalist is expected to. The world is structural in a strong sense. Mary learns something. Some truths are not structural. There are no primitive necessities. Introspection does not reveal how an experience is constituted. Having an experience is not the only way to come to know what it is like.

It disagrees about one thing: what a phenomenal concept is a concept of. For Alter an experience has a phenomenal nature that is fully apparent, and beneath it a constitution that is not. There are two layers, and the concept is of the first. For the essay there is one thing, the organized occurrence, and recognition presents it coarsely. What recognition misses is more of the same character.

The test is a case Alter states and sets aside. Could an experience be indistinguishable from an orange one and be differently organized at the grain where experience lives? He says a physicalist need not allow it. The essay says there could be, and calls it fool's orange. It stands to orange as XYZ stands to water.

If that is right, the concept of an experience works as the concept of water does, and the rest follows on Alter's own principles. The necessity is a Kripke case and is not primitive. A reasoner who has the concept can deduce the phenomenal truths from the physical ones, given the account: that experience is the organization, so the same organization is the same kind of experience. Alter's intuition that she cannot is the intuition that the account is false, and the essay argues for the account on his standard, the balance of evidence. Mary before her release lacks the concept, which Alter grants proves nothing. What she lacks is a path from her understanding into her own vehicles, and that is a fact about human architecture. A reasoner with the path acquires the concept from the description, by construction. The same reasoner can test the account, by building two organizations and comparing them in one field.

So every truth is reducible and every reduction is structural (user). The truths that are not structural are those stated with recognitional concepts, and they follow from structural truths for anyone who has the concepts.

Russellian monism turns the second layer into ontology. The essay says where the idea of that layer comes from, and argues that the Russellian's Mary is no better off than the physicalist's.

The account has a stated limit, which the author calls a leap of faith (§9).

What makes this more than a restatement:

1. **It accepts the bridge** (reading; its consequence is confirmed by the author's answer on the alien). A posteriori physicalists usually deny that necessitation needs an a priori ground. The essay agrees with Alter and Chalmers that it does, and claims the ground exists if the account is true. So the dispute moves from the bridge to the account.
2. **It takes the horn Alter sets aside** (reading, confirmed by the author). Fool's orange is possible, and that is what makes the concept work like WATER.
3. **An argument from overflow against weak Revelation** (accepted, 17 Nov). Alter's weak Revelation says the complete phenomenal nature is apparent and only constitution is hidden. The graded series of shades shows that part of the character is not apparent. So the line between what is apparent and what is hidden does not fall between nature and constitution.
4. **A targeted inaccuracy where he demands a radical one** (reading). Introspection is wrong on one aspect, simplicity, and that is the only aspect that would carry non-structural information.
5. **Genuine and cheating constructors** (user). The route from description to configuration must be structural. A label is not enough.
6. **Idealizing the architecture** (GRAIN:105; user on understanding). The line between what a reasoner can and cannot get from a description depends on the reasoner's architecture. The identity does not.
7. **The leap of faith** (user). The essay says what remains on trust and how much, for us and for a constructor.
8. **Lewis's lesson turned on quiddities** (reading). No lesson in any vocabulary would help Mary, so lessons about quiddities would not help her either.
9. **The constructor as a test** (accepted, 17 Nov). The human Mary cannot check her conclusion about the alien. A constructor can, by building the alien's organization beside its own red. The account is thereby open to an experiment that the Russellian's quiddities are not.

## Sections

### §0 Opening

(proposal) Open on the alien. A woman who has seen red is back in a black-and-white room. She is given the complete physics of a creature from another planet, and asked whether the state it is in when it looks at a tomato is the one she knows as red. Alter says she cannot tell, and calls it a strong intuition (CL 1:01–1:03). The author says she can.

- State why this case and not the original. Alter grants that one cannot expect Mary to derive the concept of phenomenal redness from structural concepts, "and nothing interesting follows from that" (CL 0:21–0:23). So the argument rests on a reasoner who has the concept.
- Say what the essay will do: grant the argument's principles and dispute one premise about concepts.
- Check before using: whether "experienced Mary" is in the book, and where. Chapter 5 or 7.3 are the likely places. If it is only in the conversation, cite Chalmers's version instead: "even when Mary has the relevant phenomenal concepts, she cannot always deduce the relevant phenomenal truths (about other people, for example)" (SRD §8, "Chalmers on Hume's missing shade").

### §1 Alter's argument

State it so that he would sign it.

- The three steps and the three claims under step one: the complete-physical-knowledge claim, the Mary-would-know claim, the learning claim (TMOC 1.4).
- What "structural" means: "expressible by statements containing only logical, mathematical, nomic, and perhaps spatiotemporal vocabulary", with "nomic" narrowed to "the relations of regularity and counterfactual dependence" (TMOC 2.2–2.3). Why physical information is objective on this account: "Sufficiently intelligent Martians could understand such information."
- His explanation of the learning claim: what Mary learns from lessons is structural, and what she learns on release "cannot be deduced from structural truths but can be learned by having relevant experiences" (TMOC 2.1). He calls the case abductive.
- The bridge. A primitive necessity is "an a posteriori necessity that is not necessary in virtue of following a priori from a priori truths and non-modal truths about the actual world". There are none (TMOC 7.2).
- Where it leads: quiddities, integrated into causation (CL 1:32–1:33; TMOC ch. 15, unread).
- His standard of proof: "preponderance of the evidence", and "I do not mean to imply that one cannot reasonably doubt its soundness" (TMOC 6.4.2). The essay argues to the same standard.
- What the argument refutes, in his words: "a narrow kind of physicalism … which says that … the entire world including consciousness is fully structural" (CL 0:41–0:43). That is the author's view.

### §2 What we grant

Six agreements, each with its source in the published essays. The section shows that the disagreement is narrow before §4 says where it is.

- **The world is structural in a strong sense** (user). The first essay's primitive is production: one state coming from another. Production is not a quiddity. So the essay cannot escape by finding something non-structural at the base. (reading)
  - What "structural" claims here (user, 16 Nov): "The thing with the Ruliad is that it's not a determinate, knowable structure. It's only known as structure via the Wolfram program: physics turns out such that a class/range of such structures would support it." So the claim is about kind. Whatever supports physics is of the kind a rule generates, and no particular rule is given, to us or to Mary. P&A:125 says the same of the Ruliad: it is "abstract as an object within the explanatory mechanism of a theory of grounding". An earlier wording here, that a rule describes production completely, is withdrawn.
  - What Mary knows, then (reading). Her complete physical truth is the truth at the lowest accessible level. What lies beneath is not part of it. It is also not where experience is: "experience is organized at a grain far coarser than that of fundamental physics" (P&A:123).
- **Mary learns something.** GRAIN:91: "We agree".
- **Some truths are not structural** (user). They are the truths stated with recognitional concepts: "structure cannot deliver *like this*" (WPIGR:203).
- **No primitive necessities.** The first essay's sixth exclusion. Its account of modality has the two ingredients of Chalmers's principle: something from the level's ontology and something non-modal from below (SR1 §3).
- **Introspection does not reveal constitution.** His wine novice and his remark that introspection has no "constitution detector" (TMOC 8.2.3) say what WPIGR:89 says.
- **Experience is not the only route to phenomenal knowledge.** He rejects that doctrine with Hume's missing shade and Unger's duplicate (TMOC 4.1). GRAIN:99 uses the same case.

A consequence to state here: WPIGR:203 says the objection to type-B views "assumes that phenomenal concepts reveal the full nature of their referents". Alter disowns that assumption and argues that his bridge stands without it (TMOC 8.2). So the published reply does not reach him, and the essay needs a better one.

### §3 The bridge, and what a Kripke case needs

- His account of why "water is H2O" is not primitive: "If the underlying explanatory structure of water is H2O, then necessarily water is H2O" is a priori as part of the concept, and its antecedent is a non-modal truth about the actual world (TMOC 7.2.2). Chalmers's general principle: "For every a posteriori metaphysical necessity 'Necessarily S', there is a nonmodal truth D such that 'If D, then necessarily S' is a priori" (TMOC 7.2.3).
- Jackson's reply to the Kripkean, which Alter adopts: from the whole physical truth, with the premise that water is the watery stuff around here, one can deduce that the mug holds H2O. Kripke's cases show necessity without deducibility only from a partial description (TMOC 1.4.2).
- What the essay owes, in that form (reading). The conditional: if what this recognition picks out is a region R of organizations at grain G, then necessarily an experience of this kind is an occurrence in R. The non-modal truth: what it picks out is R. GRAIN:79 claims the necessity "follows from an identity together with a discovered ordering of grains", and WPIGR:203 gives the two parts.
- Whether the conditional is a priori depends on what the concept is a concept of. If the concept is of a nature that is fully apparent, the conditional is false: nothing about organization is part of that nature. If the concept is of whatever is present when the recognition fires, the conditional is a priori in the way the one about water is. This is the question of §4. So the conditional is a priori given the one-layer account, and the account is argued in §5 on the balance of evidence. The essay does not claim that Mary's conclusion follows from the physics and her concept alone (accepted, 17 Nov).
- A model for how such a question is settled (proposal): Pereboom's, which Alter cites. A concept's structure is a set of conditionals over worlds "considered as actual", and water "could have turned out" to be a disjunctive kind or a functional one. The essay asks what we say about experience when the cases of §4 and §5 are considered as actual.

### §4 One layer or two: fool's orange

The crux. Claude's reading, confirmed by the author on 16 Nov in its two main points: fool's orange is possible, and the referent is the region.

The essay does not re-argue the account of phenomenality here (user). Overflow, types and exact character, and functional duplicates are cited from GRAIN and WPIGR. What is new is their use against Alter's weak Revelation, in §5.

- **Alter's picture.** Weak Revelation: the complete nature of an experience's type is apparent, and that nature "does not concern whether and how that type is (actually) constituted" (TMOC 8.2.2). He rejects the strong form, on which constitution is apparent as well.
- **The case he states for Goff and rejects.** If experiences were structurally constituted, there could be an experience that "feels exactly the same" as an orange one and is not orange, being differently constituted; then one could not be confident that one's own experience is orange. His reply: standard physicalism "is perfectly compatible with counting any O experience—including an O experience that is not structured as yours is—as a phenomenally orange experience" (TMOC 8.2.3).
- **That reply treats the concept as unlike WATER.** Anything with the presentation counts, whatever it is. He says as much in conversation: he is "disinclined to think that … feeling pain, seeing red … are … Putnam–Kripke natural kind terms" (CL 1:07–1:08).
- **"Feels exactly the same" has two readings.** The same to recognition: the subject cannot tell them apart. The same in what is present: the occurrences have the same exact character. The published essays hold that the first does not give the second. "Two occurrences that fall under the same type, and so share a matching profile, need not share exact character" (GRAIN:33). Exact character "is not known in the sense of being represented. It is present" (WPIGR:137).
- **Three ways two experiences can differ in constitution**, to keep apart (Claude's division; the author confirmed the third case).
  - Below the grain of experience: neurons or silicon. This makes no difference to the experience. "Their material may vary, but the constitutive dynamics must actually be present" (C2M:59). Alter is right about this case, and it is the one his reply fits.
  - At the grain of experience and below the grain of recognition, within one architecture. The subject cannot tell the two apart, and they differ in exact character. A replacement of substrate that preserves cognitive function "need not preserve exact character, although it plausibly preserves its type" (WPIGR:139).
  - At the grain of experience, with only the cognitive role preserved. "A functional description, specified at the grain of cognition, really does underdetermine experience" (GRAIN:57). Here the same judgments and discriminations can go with a different experience, or with none. This is the full analogue of XYZ. GRAIN:45 has the case and its hedge: a duplicate of our discriminative organization that "keeps some regulated carrier would plausibly have experiences of the same types, differing in exact character. One that dissolves the carrier, as some unfoldings may, would be a candidate for having none."
- **The referent is the region** (user). "Orange" names the region of organizations that this recognition tracks, and not a single occurrence. This is "simply the 'phenomenality overflows cognition' thesis": recognition is coarser than what it responds to, so one recognition answers to many occurrences.
- **So fool's orange is possible, and the concept works like WATER.** It fixes its referent by how the referent is presented, and the referent has a grain the presentation does not show. What has the presentation without the organization is not orange. What has both, and differs below the grain of recognition, is orange and differs in exact character. The second is to orange as heavy water is to water†, and only the first is XYZ.
- **Where the analogy with water stops** (reading). H2O draws water's boundary without help from how water looks. Organizations vary continuously, and the boundary of orange is drawn by recognition's own lumping. So the kind is natural in what it is a region of, and recognition-relative in where the region ends. Fool's orange comes from outside the space of such organizations, or from elsewhere in it.
- **Why read the concept this way** (reading). Two shades that look the same apart are distinct at a shared edge (WPIGR:135), and we say they differed all along. We defer to what was present over what recognition delivered, which is how one treats a kind term.
- **The price Alter names.** How can one be confident one's experience is orange? Reply from WPIGR's "Presence without infallible recognition": confidence attaches to judgments at the grain of recognition, about kind and similarity, and those are mostly right. Exact character is present without being known.
- **Chalmers's argument against opacity does not touch this.** He shows that a pure phenomenal concept is not a demonstrative that "blindly point[s]" (TMOC 8.2.1). The essay agrees. The concept gives access "through actual character" (WPIGR:95) and gets presence, representational character and distinctness right (WPIGR:51). It is neither radically opaque nor transparent. Alter grants that a "semantic explanation for the epistemic gap … would undermine the inference to the modal gap", and considers only radical opacity. The essay offers the middle kind.

### §5 Why the apparent nature is not the complete nature

§4 says the two pictures differ. This section argues for one of them.

- **The argument from overflow** (accepted, 17 Nov). Take a graded series of shades in which each is indiscriminable from the next and the ends differ (WPIGR:135; GRAIN:27–35; Raffman†). The experiences differ in character, and the difference is not apparent to recognition when the shades are seen apart. So part of the phenomenal nature of each is not apparent. Weak Revelation says the complete nature is apparent. It is false, and for a reason that has nothing to do with constitution.
- **The line is in the wrong place.** Alter puts the apparent on one side and constitution on the other. The wine novice shows that the line runs through character. What the novice misses and the expert gets is in the taste.
- **Chimerical colors.** Churchland predicted, from a model of opponent processing, afterimages in colors no surface has, and they were then seen. Alter reports the case (TMOC 4.3) and reads the remaining surprise as evidence that Mary "was not able to do the relevant deduction". The essay reads the prediction: the structure of the vehicles told us of a character before anyone had it.
- **Answering 6.4.** Against Pereboom he says a physicalist needs introspection to be inaccurate in fact, and radically, since "if any aspect of the nature of phenomenal redness is as introspection represents that nature as being", Mary gains information about that aspect.
  - The essay's claim is not radical. Introspection is "otherwise largely correct" (WPIGR:119). The mistake is one: "because the constitutive complexity is not presented in recognizing an experiential character, the character is taken to have no constitutive complexity" (WPIGR:89).
  - The aspects it gets right are structural, and those Mary could already derive: "a complete description of the vehicles entails that an experience of red is more like an experience of orange than one of green" (WPIGR:203).
  - The one aspect that would not be structural is simplicity, a nature "exhausted by that access" (WPIGR:95). That is the aspect misrepresented. A targeted inaccuracy does the work he says needs a radical one.
  - Evidence that it is actual, which he asks for: the overflow, the chimerical colors, and the explanation at WPIGR:89–91 of why a complex activity available as a unit would be taken for simple.
- **His point about Locke.** A Lockean about color keeps the feeling and moves it into the mind; the parallel move for the feeling has nowhere to go. WPIGR:113 answers: the relocation "transfers a feature of represented content to the representing occurrence", and "an experience of a homogeneous red surface need not be homogeneous or simple". Denying simplicity feels like denying the feeling for that reason.
- **Stoljar and Mendelow** (SRD §4.4). Use their point that nothing in introspection or theory shows phenomenal properties to be non-structural. Do not use Stoljar's ignorance hypothesis: the essay agrees with Alter that complete physical knowledge leaves the gap for Mary.
- The nearest published relative is Loorits (2014): consciousness is "a complex neural pattern that misperceives some of its own highly complex structural properties as monadic and qualitative" (SRD §4.4). Say what the essay adds: the grain, and a mistake confined to simplicity.

### §6 What the experienced Mary can deduce

- **The deduction, in Jackson's form** (accepted, 17 Nov, in this form). Mary here is the human of Alter's case. The phenomenal kind red is what this recognition of mine picks out. What it picks out is a region R of organizations at grain G: a physical truth about her own brain. The alien's state lies in R, or does not: a physical truth about the alien. So the alien's state is red, or is not. The first premise carries the account. Without it, what the recognition picks out could be a nature that merely goes with R.
- **The author's answer**: she can, and "this follows from the sharing an experience essay." The identity is "at the level of a concrete dynamical organization" (C2M:59). Sameness of kind is sameness of organization, which the physics settles.
- **Why Alter's intuition is strong.** She cannot check the conclusion phenomenally. She has never had the alien's state beside her own, and as a human she never can. The deduction gives her a truth and no new recognition. For her the leap of faith is full size and reaches across experiencers (§9). His intuition that she cannot tell is the intuition that the account is false, and the argument against it is §5's.
- **Hard cases** (proposal). An alien whose quality space has no counterpart of her red: the answer is "no", and it is deduced. An alien whose organization lies near the edge of her region: the answer is as vague as the edge, which her own recognition draws (§4), and how close two experiences are is a matter of degree, as C2M:159 says of copies. A bat: she can deduce that its states are not of any kind she has a concept for.
- Brown holds the same: with the concepts, the conditional from physical to phenomenal truths is a priori, and he is "a structuralist and an ontic structural realist probably" (CL 0:47). Alter grants that Brown's use of Chalmers's front-loading "sounds like an omission" in the book (CL 1:07). An ally to cite, once his published statement of it is found.

### §7 What Mary lacked

- **The concept, and why she could not get it.** A recognitional concept gets its mode of presentation "from undergoing" (GRAIN:93). Humans can write into their own vehicles only coarsely: "We can interpolate within quality spaces we already have, and cannot add a dimension to them. That is why Mary is stuck" (GRAIN:99).
- **The premise the essay denies.** Alter's ground for the complete-physical-knowledge claim is that a textbook of the physical truth "could be understood by any sufficiently intelligent creature, regardless of their sensory capacities" (TMOC 1.4.1). The author: "Acquiring understanding requires reconfiguring representational substrate." And of Chalmers's notion of the a priori: "I find Chalmers' take on facts dry, shouldn't facts exhaust understanding and do his facts do that?" (user)
  - Reading: Mary can state every structural truth and derive every other. Understanding the description of an organization at the level of the organization means being able to recognize an instance. For most organizations an instance can be built on paper or in imagination. For an experience the recognizing is done by the experiencing system, so the instance has to be built in its own vehicles.
  - Mary is the limiting case of something ordinary (user for the scope; the rest is reading). Asked whether every level has some truths that are not structural, or only experience: "I think every level except base?" "Not structural" describes how a truth is stated: with a concept applied by recognition, which does not itself say what structure it picks out. That happens in degrees at every level above the base. We recognize a stone or a glider without the concept saying what it is made of, and nobody infers a gap in the world from that. "Red" is near the far end, and not at it, since similarity and distinctness can be derived (WPIGR:203).
  - Alter's own text shows this for physics (reading). To meet Stoljar's point that spatial and temporal concepts are tied to experience, he replaces the physical concepts by counterparts defined through Ramsey sentences (TMOC 2.3). So the physics Mary is taught is purely structural only after its concepts have been stripped of what recognition contributes.
  - Physics as the test case (user, 17 Nov: "Physics should be the easiest test since it's the one 'accused' of being purely structural"; the three points are Claude's proposal).
    - Newman's problem. A theory whose every term is defined by its role is satisfied by any set of the right size, so a purely structural physics would say nothing. Alter grants this and keeps the nomic concepts "primitive", not defined by role (TMOC 2.3). So the physics his argument needs is not purely structural by his own construction.
    - What anchors it. Physical concepts are applied through measurement: a track in a detector is recognized as an electron's. The concept does not say what structure beneath physics it picks out, as "red" does not say what organization it picks out.
    - What makes physics seem purely structural. Its recognizers can be swapped. Any instrument and any observer will do, which is Alter's point that "sufficiently intelligent Martians could understand such information". For "red" only one kind of recognizer will do, the experiencing system. So objectivity is the degree to which the recognizer can be replaced, and Mary's case is the degree zero.
  - This is the general account the author accepted: "truths at any level are reached by building an instance from the lower-level description and recognizing it under the higher level's ontology" (accepted). The first essay's notion of concreteness, location in a lower level, says what an instance is.
- **Keep the claim specific.** WPIGR:97 calls the observation that "a scientific description does not reproduce an experience" "true but weak": "Reading a description of viscosity does not make the page viscous either." The claim is about "an architecture that can recognize a concrete difference without representing the complexity constituting it".
- **A rival explanation of the learning claim.** His is abductive, so the essay offers a competitor. Mary learns on release because the concept she lacked can be had only by a system in which the organization occurs, and nothing in her could bring that about from a description. A reasoner with a write path would learn nothing on release. Lewis's point, that lessons about non-physical things would not help either "if those further lessons are also conveyed in black and white" (TMOC 2.1), is predicted by this explanation: a lesson in any vocabulary is a description.
- **His two stipulations.** Mary "does not imagine in color" (TMOC 1.4.1). And in answering the new-concepts objection he says that if the concepts are physically explicable, her teachers could give her the explanation, and "that should enable her to construct the relevant concepts" (TMOC 9.4.3). For a human the second is false and the first is not needed. For a reasoner with the path the second is true and the first removes it by fiat. Either way the three claims of step one cannot all be kept once construction counts as a way from the physical truth to a phenomenal one.

### §8 The constructor, and what counts as cheating

- **The constructor.** It "reads the physical description, derives the organization at the grain of experience, configures its own vehicles to match, and brings them into its regulation. Then it undergoes and recognizes the result" (GRAIN:97). The three things it needs are at GRAIN:101.
- **The criterion.** "A technology cheats when it causes the experience independently of what the reasoner knows" (GRAIN:95). The photograph cheats in three ways. The constructor does not: "a garbled description would yield a different result".
- **A second clause** (user). "Dennett's Locked RoboMary can also be a genuine one and a cheating one. The cheating one would obtain the redness information non-structurally, e.g. via a label 'red' on a configuration / module without knowing why that configuration should be red."
  - As Alter quotes Dennett, the robot finds the state B her model enters on seeing a tomato and "puts herself into state B", which "is, by definition, not an illicit state of color experience; it is the state that such an illicit state of color experience normally causes" (TMOC 4.2.1). That reaches her as "the state a tomato causes", which is a label. It is the weak version. (reading; Dennett's own text is unread)
  - The genuine constructor can say why this organization holds red's place: what it is more like, what it opposes.
  - A label is a lookup table. The cheating constructor stands to the genuine one as the dust man's map (CCI:39) stands to an admissible map, so the criterion is the first essay's admissibility clause applied to the route. (proposal)
  - A genuine constructor is possible only if red is structural. If red were atomic there would be no "why", and every route would cheat. So "is there a constructor that does not cheat?" and "is red structural?" are one question. (proposal)
- **Alter's verdict on the robot.** "She does not deduce phenomenal truths from physical truths. Instead, she puts herself in a dispositional state … This is hardly better than if she had simply unlocked her color-vision system." And the same "open epistemic possibilities" hold "with respect to the content of B" (TMOC 4.2.3).
  - The strongest form of this (Claude's formulation): producing the experience shows that the organization suffices, and a dualist with psychophysical laws predicts the same success. Deduction would show that the phenomenal truth follows from what the physical truth says.
  - The reply: the essay does not rest the necessity on the production. The necessity is derived, as a Kripke case (§3, §6). Construction is how a reasoner without the concept gets it from the description.
  - On the "open possibilities": the constructor builds both candidates, binds them in one field, and knows which organization is which (§9).
- **The constructor's second job** (accepted, 17 Nov). It is what would test the account. It uses the account for two things only: the step from the same organization to the same experience, and differences below the grain of recognition. Everything else it can check by binding. If it built two organizations alike and could tell them apart when bound, the account would be wrong (§13).
- **His trapezoid cases, and Kind's.** The geometer with a proof deduces. The geometer with a device that puts anyone contemplating the axioms into the state of knowing does not: "that process is plainly not deduction" (TMOC 4.2.4). The device fails GRAIN:95's test, since a garbled axiom would change nothing. Kind's third case is the geometer who needs a diagram: if Euclid's diagrams are indispensable his proofs are not pure deduction, and using one is no foul (SRD §8). GRAIN:97 puts the constructor with that geometer, through Kant.
- **What he concedes.** "Perhaps Mary could in some way exploit her physical knowledge to acquire phenomenal knowledge of color experiences without having color experiences. Dennett's RoboMary case indicates that such a thing is possible" (TMOC 4.5).
- **Chalmers on the small case.** He classes Hume's interpolation as "a priori reasoning from concepts of intrinsic properties one has direct acquaintance with" (SRD §8). The constructor differs from Hume's subject in the power of its write path.
- **Whether to call it a priori.** GRAIN:105: "We need not dispute the word." The author would now dispute it (§7). The essay can say: Chalmers's ideal reasoner "is idealized in capacity but not in architecture"; idealize both, and the knowledge argument's premise fails for that reasoner.
- **His own definition lets the constructor in** (proposal; the passages are in SRD §8, "Ideal in which modality"). "If I cannot know that P independent of experience, but another less limited being could do so, then it is a priori that P." The constructor is such a being. What keeps it out is a second clause, that the reasoning is "restricted to a priori reasoning", which he says is sometimes "a stipulation". So the question is what the constructor's undergoing contributes. On §6 it supplies the concept, and the conditional is then justified by deduction. Experience that only supplies a concept does not make knowledge a posteriori on the usual view†. If that holds, the essay need not fight for the word: the conditional is a priori by his definition, given the account. The account itself is not claimed a priori.

### §9 The leap of faith

The essay's stated limit. Mostly (user).

- **The author's statement.** The account "might have weak phenomenal generalizability across experiences and experiencers (a 'leap of faith' that particular abstract correspondences have particular phenomenal shifts)."
- **It arises within one subject.** "The 'faith' point can be seen within a subject once we notice that phenomenality 'overflows' recognition. Since 'blue' is structural rather than atomic, it's a leap of faith that different experiences don't differ in a way we don't notice across them."
- **The constructor's position.** It "knows it in the limit, the constructor is in a stronger epistemic position than humans / animals." It "can bind two different blues of its choice beside each other, so to say." It "shares our limits on what can be known phenomenally, but it has a good grasp on the space of phenomenal differences. E.g. it knows whether the gradations of blue that look indistinguishable are the same experience or different experiences, but it doesn't know it phenomenally, only deductively from our account. So there is still a leap of faith, but it's a leap of 'insignificant difference' rather than of potentially big difference."
- **What this comes to** (reading). Phenomenal knowledge is bounded by recognition for every architecture. The constructor has two advantages. It chooses what to bind, so whatever can be told apart side by side is told apart. And it knows the organization it built. What stays on trust is that the account is right about differences no binding can reveal, and those make no recognizable difference to the subject.
- **A caveat and a reply** (proposal). "Each comparison supplies a new context, and the context is part of what fixes exact character" (GRAIN:33). Binding two blues is a new context. The constructor built the joint organization, so it knows what the binding preserved in each part.
- **Why to say this to Alter** (proposal). He holds that the argument can reasonably be doubted and asks only for the balance of evidence. The essay should be as plain about its own gap. The gap is also smaller than his: he grants that we do not understand quiddities at all (§11).
- **How the leap sits with §3** (accepted, 17 Nov). It differs by reasoner. The human who has seen red concludes that the alien's state is red as hers is by the account, and cannot check; her leap is full size. The constructor builds the alien's organization, binds it beside its own red and recognizes; its leap is the small one. So three things are kept apart in the essay: what the concept refers to, which is settled by cases; the account that organization fixes character, which is a theory argued on evidence; and the rest, which is physics.

### §10 Chalmers's dilemma, and which label

- **The strategy Alter attacks in chapter 9.** A posteriori physicalists accept the epistemic gap, deny the modal gap, and explain the first by features of phenomenal concepts: recognitional concepts (Loar†), concepts that contain their referents as if in quotation marks (Papineau†, Balog†, Block†).
- **The dilemma.** Let C say that we have those features, in topic-neutral terms. If a physical duplicate without C is conceivable, C is not physically explicable. If not, zombies have C, and "zombies do not share our epistemic situation", so C does not explain ours (TMOC 9.2). The explanation wanted, in Chalmers's words as Alter quotes them, is one "that makes transparent why some high-level truth obtains".
- **Zombies** (user). "Philosophical or 'physical' zombies are only conceivable by forgetting the grain of physics." GRAIN:61 names the forgetting: the intuition "extends the verdict from functional descriptions to physics", because "our frame presents functional and physical descriptions alike as third-person structure", and "functional and physical descriptions differ in grain, not in kind, and the frame has no way to register the difference." So a duplicate at the grain of cognition may differ in experience or lack it (§4), and a duplicate at the grain of physics cannot.
- **Two cases** (user).
  - A human, given the complete physics. "Philosophical human zombie is only prima facie conceivable."
  - A creature whose physics is coarser than its own experience, which is the creature of GRAIN:75. "It is maybe ideally conceivable that there exists a creature whose philosophical zombie is conceivable, but I suspect that such creatures don't exist / aren't metaphysically possible." The author confirmed that this creature is the one meant. Claude had read the sentence as about a creature whose experience is not structure at any grain, and that reading is withdrawn.
- **What this adds to GRAIN:75–79.** GRAIN says the ordering of grains "is not a priori" and "could have turned out" otherwise, and that for such observers "two systems duplicated at the grain of their physics could differ in experience" while "their physicalism would still be true" (GRAIN:77). The author now suspects the ordering could not have been otherwise. The two fit if "not a priori" is about what can be known and the suspicion is about what can be. They need a note if the suspicion is argued (see "What the essay changes elsewhere").
- **The ground for the suspicion** (user). "The suspicion is via the availability of exploitable mechanism. It's a guess that if evolution can make the organization do the relevant work, measuring devices can access that organization too."
  - Reading: the claim is about what devices can reach, so about a creature's physics in principle. At a stage of its science a creature's physics can be coarser than its experience; ours was.
  - Reading: it is a guess about mechanisms and not an argument from concepts. The author does not claim it a priori.
  - How it meets GRAIN (reading). The grain of experience is the grain "at which its regulation corrects" the vehicle (GRAIN:35). Regulation is work the organization does, and evolution built it. So the organization that matters to experience is organization a mechanism already exploits. Claude had proposed a ground of its own, from regulation as an interaction that interventions can reach; it is a special case of the author's and is dropped in its favour.
  - Its status. Claude proposed that the guess is in the end a mathematical matter, which ideal reflection would settle. The author's remark on the Ruliad (§2) removes the premise: there is no determinate structure to reason from. The proposal is withdrawn. The guess stays a guess, and whether the creature is conceivable without being possible stays open.
  - The essay does not need the guess. For us the ordering of grains is a discovered fact, and GRAIN:79 rests on that.
- **The essay is not where the dilemma aims** (reading, confirmed by the answer above). It is addressed to those who grant that zombies are ideally conceivable. On §3 and §6, a reasoner who has the concept can deduce the phenomenal truths given the account, so for that reasoner the physical truth without them is conceivable only by setting the account aside. Zombies are conceivable at first look, and GRAIN:49–53 says why: "the conceptual frame in which we conceive it cannot express grain dependency"; a concept saying that experience is the organization at a grain would block the subtraction, and "we have no such concept. So the subtraction goes through in thought, whatever the facts are."
- **How GRAIN already differs from the strategy.** "The usual strategy treats the isolation of phenomenal concepts as a basic feature of how they work. Here it follows from the overflow" (GRAIN:55). And GRAIN:21 says of the usual strategy what Chalmers's second horn says: nothing in the world answers to the apparent omission.
- **Is the account a transparent explanation?** Alter's test, against Howell: "a sufficiently capable reasoner who understands C would be well-positioned to understand our epistemic situation" (TMOC 9.4.1). GRAIN:93 gives the explanation in structural terms: a concept that closed the gap "would have to specify the grain of its referent, as physical concepts do, while bearing its mode of presentation, as recognitional concepts do", and recognition is reached only at the grain of discrimination. A reasoner who read that would predict that Mary learns, that zombies seem conceivable, and that the seeming is "partly veridical" (GRAIN:57).
- **Prinz and Howell.** Prinz: "[m]aintaining availability is an actual neural process, and Mary cannot undergo that neural process, or any other, simply by reading about it." Howell: phenomenal knowledge "requires actually having the phenomenal states in question" (as quoted in TMOC 9.4.2 and 9.4.1). These are the nearest published positions to §7. Alter answers each with the dilemma. The essay's difference from both is §6: given the concept, the entailment is a priori.
- **The new fact thesis** (TMOC 7.1). Learning an old truth under a new guise generally brings a new truth about the guise. Reply (reading): the guise is itself an organization, coarser than what it presents, and truths about it are structural truths Mary already had. The "physically omniscient observer" who "may have no idea" what it is like for Mary lacks the concept; one who has it can deduce.
- **The label** (reading; a question for the author below). WPIGR:203 says "an epistemic gap therefore remains, and the position is type B." On Chalmers's taxonomy a view that denies the ideal conceivability of zombies is type A, and he names "the under-discussed missing-concept reply … which allows that P⊃Q is a priori". The essay's position is that reply, with an account of why the concept is missing and how it could be had. It is not analytic functionalism: the a priori link runs through reference fixed by recognition, and not through a functional analysis at the grain of cognition. GRAIN:91 and GRAIN:103 stand for a reasoner without the concept.

### §11 Russellian monism

The author's scope is Russellian monism as a whole. The section should stay short of a survey: one diagnosis and four arguments.

- **The view.** Structuralism about physics, realism about quiddities, and quidditism about consciousness (SEP-RM). Alter's route to it: if the knowledge argument is sound there are probably structural and non-structural properties, and "a theory is going to be more likely to be true if it integrates all the kind[s] of properties into causation" (CL 1:32–1:33).
- **The diagnosis.** The quiddity is §4's second layer given a place in ontology. GRAIN:85: the one alleged instance of a non-structural intrinsic nature is our own experience, and "the paradigm of an intrinsic nature is structure below the grain of recognition. The Russellian takes the one place where structure hides from us as evidence of something that is not structure." GRAIN:87: for the Russellian experience lies beneath physics; for us "physics is structure at one grain and experience is structure at another".
- **Lewis's lesson, turned** (reading). Alter says the view is reductionist: given the quiddities too, Mary "has got to be able to deduce … all the facts about consciousness" (CL 1:34–1:35). By Lewis's point, lessons about quiddities in black and white would not help her. So the truths about quiddities that matter can be grasped only by acquaintance, and the Russellian's Mary needs what the physicalist's needs. Pereboom saw the neighbouring point for powers: "Wouldn't Mary be in a position to fully understand such powers while she is still in the room?" (SRD §3.3)
- **Parity, which he grants.** Asked why the argument cannot be rerun against quiddities, he says "by hypothesis we don't understand these properties". Brown replies that the physicalist can say the same of a future theory. Alter: "there may be parity", and his advantage "might be a Pyrrhic victory" (CL 1:36–1:46). The essay's claim is that the structural side has an account of the gap and his side has a placeholder: "a big mystery at the center of it" (CL 1:59–2:02).
- **An unknown beneath physics, on both sides** (reading). The Russellian says physics leaves out what underlies it. The author also declines to say what lies beneath the lowest accessible level, and holds that the Ruliad is not a determinate, knowable structure (§2). The difference is in what the unknown is asked to do. The Russellian's unknown is a nature, and experience is made of it. The author's is more structure of the same kind, and experience does not depend on which structure it is, because experience is organized far above it. This answers in advance the charge that the essay has quiddities of its own. The author, asked whether experience depends on which structure lies beneath physics: "As we said for humans and their machines the grain of experience is substantially above the grain of physics." The claim is made for humans and their machines, and the essay should keep that scope.
- **Idle in causation.** Because the tie between quiddities and dispositions is contingent, quiddities are "just along for the causal ride" (Howell 2015, via SEP-RM). His causal-integration argument wants the opposite. Chapter 15 must be read before this is pressed.
- **The combination problem, and grain.** "How do (proto)phenomenal quiddities … combine to constitute macrophenomenal properties" (SEP-RM). The Russellian owes a construction from the small to the experienced, which is a structural story, and the grain account already is one. Sellars's grain problem† is the classical form of the worry and shares the essay's key word; check whether GRAIN cites it.
- **What the first essay contributes.** Its first exclusion. Relata are individuated by relations, with production as the one primitive and no nature beneath it (SR1 §2–§4). Alter's own section 2.3 shows why some primitive is needed: a theory whose every term is defined by its role is satisfied by any set of the right size. His primitive is the nomic. The author's is production.
- Against structuralism Alter gives one sentence: such views "seem to yield a world devoid of substance or qualities" (SEP-RM §1.1). GRAIN:85 is an explanation of that seeming.

### §12 Two notions of possibility

(proposal) Short. It is the one place where the two essays' accounts of modality meet Chalmers's. The author's question of 16 Nov, in which modality the reasoner is ideal, shows that §10 needs it: his definition of ideal conceivability uses "a possible subject".

- For Chalmers the basic modality is tied "to rational consistency, apriority, or conceivability", and "to introduce a further primitive, restricting the space of worlds, is to introduce an unnecessary wheel" (TMOC 7.2.5).
- For the author a description does not make a possibility. "Describing arbitrarily intense or prolonged suffering does not prove the possibility of its full embedding" (P&A:153). Possibility is what can be rebuilt down to the base (SR1 §3).
- These agree on what the essay needs: no necessity without a ground. They disagree on whether every coherent description is a possibility.
- **Ideal in which modality?** (user's question; the rest is reading.) Chalmers says "it is not obvious that an ideal reasoner is possible or coherent", takes "certain rational notions as primitive", and defines: S is ideally conceivable "when there is a possible subject for whom S is prima facie conceivable, with justification that is undefeatable by better reasoning." He grants the circle and says possibility enters "in such a roundabout way that the thesis clearly remains substantive". For him the possible subjects are the coherently describable ones, since he has one space of worlds. For the author a possible subject is one that can be built, down to a base, and under the Church–Turing bound (CCI:53). So the two notions of possibility give two notions of ideal conceivability.
- **Conceivability without possibility?** (reading of the author's statements in §10.) For humans the essay denies the conceivability. For a creature with a physics coarser than its experience, the author allows that it may be ideally conceivable and guesses it is not possible, because what evolution can exploit a device can measure. Whether that is a case of conceivability without possibility depends on whether the guess could be settled by reasoning alone. The author's remark on the Ruliad (§2) suggests it could not. The essay can leave it open, since nothing in the reply to Alter rests on it.
- **What "rebuilt" means** (user, 16 Nov). The first essay says a situation is possible when its support can be rebuilt down to the Ruliad. The author: "By "rebuilt" I meant metaphysically, a metaphorical God could do it. Or as a proxy via a toy model: one could imagine the rebuilding in a hand-wavy way."
  - Reading: possibility is a metaphysical matter, and it does not wait on our knowing the structure beneath physics. Our access to it is by proxy. An earlier reading here, that some modal questions are answered only by guesses, is replaced by this.
  - Reading: this places conceiving. To conceive a situation is to imagine its rebuilding in a toy model. The proxy is good where the toy model keeps what matters and fails where the hand-waving skips a step. The zombie is the second kind: the step skipped is the grain of physics (§10).
  - Reading: a possible reasoner is then one that a metaphorical God could build, which answers the question of which reasoners the idealization ranges over.
  - No modal notion at the bottom (user, 16 Nov). "A metaphorical God could do it" uses "could". The author: "Modality from the physics level reduces into sets at the base level, or what the reality fluid measure turns out to be concerned with. At the base level there is no further counterfactual structure, just the generative structure."
  - What this answers in Chalmers (reading). He says that restricting the space of worlds introduces "a further primitive", "an unnecessary wheel" (TMOC 7.2.5). The author's restriction adds no modal primitive. It removes the one Chalmers keeps: his basic modality is tied to rational notions he takes as primitive, and the author's reduces, at the base, to sets and generative structure. Chalmers's question "why should there not be just one metaphysically possible world, or thirty-seven?" then has an answer of the form: as many as the generative structure yields.
  - What this does to the bridge (reading; narrowed after the author asked "how so?"). Alter's principle asks that every necessity follow a priori from a priori truths and non-modal truths about the actual world. The author's account gives something related and not the same.
    - The chain. Modal facts at a level are constructed from the level below, using that level's ontology (SR1 §3). At the physics level they reduce to sets at the base. The base has generative structure and no counterfactuals. So no modal fact is left without a ground that is not itself counterfactual.
    - Where this falls short of Alter's principle. The ground is the base facts together with each level's typing, and the typing comes from a family of interactions. The ground need not be "about the actual world" in his sense, since the sets may include what has small measure and is not actual for us. And his principle wants the step from ground to necessity to be a priori, which cannot be shown for a base that is not a determinate, knowable structure.
    - So the claim to make is the narrower one: no necessity is brute. For the identity of experience with organization the essay shows the a priori step itself (§3), at accessible levels, and that case does not wait on the base.
  - Both sides have a metaphorical God (proposal). Alter pictures physicalism as God having no further work to do once the physical truth is fixed (TMOC 1.4.3). Chalmers's God chooses among coherent descriptions. The author's builds from below, and in building the physics of a human has already built the organization at the grain of experience.
- **What conceiving a zombie comes to on each** (proposal). On his, finding no contradiction in a description. On the author's, the nearer notion is his positive conceivability, imagining a situation in detail. To imagine a physical duplicate in the detail of P is to specify the organization at the grain of experience. The zombie is conceived only at a coarser detail than P, which is the author's "forgetting the grain of physics".
- A test case to put to the author: a device that computes what no Turing machine can is coherent to describe, and CCI:53 makes the Church–Turing bound part of what a base is. Is the device impossible, and would Chalmers call that a primitive necessity? His "impossicycle" (TMOC 7.2.4) is the same kind of case.

### §13 What would change our minds

- A case of introspection being right about simplicity: evidence that some experiential character has no constitutive complexity at any grain.
- A failure of structure to predict character where the model is good. Chimerical colors went the right way; a comparable prediction that failed would count against.
- A demonstration that no write path of the kind GRAIN:101 describes can exist, for a reason of principle.
- An argument that the phenomenal concept does not have the structure §3 needs, drawn from cases considered as actual, where the essay's answer is plainly the wrong thing to say.
- Evidence that organization at the grain of experience does not fix character: two systems that a constructor builds alike and that it can tell apart when bound. This is the test the constructor makes possible (§8), and the one item on this list that only a constructor could supply.
- From the first essay: its hostage, the unproved requirement that physics is stable under a shift of base (SR1 §12).

### §14 Closing

Return to the woman in the room with the alien's physics. She can work out the answer. She cannot feel that it is right, and the essay has said why and how much that matters. Then the two things Alter and the essay share that most of his critics deny: nothing is necessary without a ground, and Mary learns.

## Answers

The author's answers of 16 Nov 2026 are in SRD §8 under "Second essay: decisions so far" and the entries after it. In summary. The published position stands and may be updated where it is lacking. The illusionist label is rejected, as in WPIGR. "Some (non-indexical) truths are not structural, but all truths are reducible and all reductions are structural." The scope is Russellian monism as a whole, in a standalone essay. Truths at any level are reached by building an instance and recognizing it. The author would dispute Chalmers's use of "a priori": understanding requires reconfiguring a representational substrate. The constructor can be genuine or cheating, and the cheating one has a label. The leap of faith holds within a subject and shrinks, for a constructor, to insignificant differences. The Ruliad is structural in a strong sense. A Mary who has seen red can deduce that an alien's state is red as hers is. Fool's orange is possible, including the duplicate that reports orange and has no experience of it: "we already cover that in earlier essays" (GRAIN:35, GRAIN:45). The referent of a phenomenal concept is the region, and the coverage of phenomenality is by pointing to the earlier essays. The human Mary deduces given the account, and the constructor is what would test it (17 Nov). Every level except the base has some truths that are not structural (17 Nov, tentative). Zombies "are only conceivable by forgetting the grain of physics"; a human zombie "is only prima facie conceivable"; and a creature whose zombie is conceivable is "maybe ideally conceivable" and, the author suspects, not metaphysically possible.

Corrections of Claude's readings made along the way are recorded in SRD §8 and are not repeated here.

## Open questions for the author

1. **The label.** Say that, on Chalmers's taxonomy, the position is the missing-concept reply and nearer type A, or keep "type B" with a qualification? Claude's recommendation, revised 16 Nov: decline both labels, as WPIGR:201 declines "functionalism". State the three positions instead: Mary learns something; physical zombies are conceivable only at first look; no necessity is brute. Add that the taxonomy assumes concepts come free. WPIGR:203's "the position is type B" then gets a dated note. The author asked for a lean under pressure (17 Nov), as WPIGR:201 admits "a fine-grained functionalism" while avoiding the label. Claude's answer: the lean is A. The taxonomy is defined for an ideal reasoner, and for that reasoner the essay denies that zombies are conceivable and denies brute necessities, which are the marks of type A. Type B describes our own predicament: for a human the identity is known a posteriori and the gap stays open. So: type A in the limit, for a reasoner with the right architecture; type B for us. And it is a fine-grained type A, since the entailment runs through organization at the grain of experience and not through cognitive function. The leap of faith is the residue no type-A view admits.
