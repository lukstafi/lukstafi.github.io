# Disunity of consciousness: source dossier for an essay on personhood and the parts approach

*Compiled 17 October 2026 for the planned essay on personhood and selfhood. Source material, not a draft.*

**Guiding question.** Can one subject (a regulatory economy, in the blog's sense) sustain two or more phenomenal fields? Can fields and subjects come apart in other ways?

## How to read this dossier

Every claim carries one of these tags.

- **[read]**: I read the text itself (full text, or the part cited) during this pass.
- **[abstract]**: I read only the abstract or the publisher's summary.
- **[reported]**: the claim comes from a secondary source (SEP, a review, a search-engine summary). I did not see the primary text.
- **[inference]**: my own reasoning, not a finding of any cited source.

Quotations are verbatim from the text I read unless the tag says otherwise. Page numbers appear only where I saw them; elsewhere the section or the reference is given.

Most of the books are not open access. Section 8 lists what I could not read and what to read before drafting. Every DOI below was matched against Crossref, except where marked.

## 0. What the essays already say (do not repeat)

- *Experience, Selfhood, and the Organization of a Mind* (12 Oct 2026).
  - Belonging criterion: "an occurrence belongs to the subject whose regulation partly constitutes it."
  - Binding is the candidate for what experiential regulation adds to minded regulation. The variants: local (regulation inside a region suffices), global (a workspace whose broadcast returns), hybrid (local regulation fixes the character of features, global regulation binds them into one field).
  - Perspectival subjectivity (for-me-ness) is "the unity and centering of a binding economy". It needs no self-model. Mineness is the contingent layer that agentive self-representation adds (Guillot 2017). Depersonalization separates the two.
  - The minimal self that remains after removing personhood is "the regulatory economy of a present episode". Personhood and phenomenal consciousness are made independent.
  - Open question: is selfhood a further natural category? The suggested tests concern dependence and intervention.
- *Can Two Minds Share One Experience*.
  - A field can be drawn as a graph of experiences, but the edges must be specified (represented relations, experienced co-presence, regulatory dependence).
  - "Experienced beside" is not transitive. A triangle of pairwise-shared occurrences (A–B, B–C, C–A) does not make one perspective.
  - Distinct subjects might share an occurrence. An encompassing subject might coexist with smaller ones. The text explicitly declines an exclusion rule like IIT's.
  - The Hogan twins and the split-brain Pinto paper are both treated as motivation only. "Neither a simple two-subject story nor an equally simple one-subject story should be built into the example."
- *Is the Monastery Minded?* (draft, 17 Oct 2026; line numbers in the file shift as it is revised).
  - Reach, latency and bandwidth of coupling decide whether a collective has a field of its own.
  - Closing paragraph of the "An additional field?" section: "A field is an experience, composed of experiences and adding their being present together, so it is owned rather than an owner. If a field were a self, the self would be a category of its own. Whether one subject can sustain several fields, as cases of divided consciousness suggest, is a question for a later essay on selfhood."

This dossier supplies the literature for that last sentence.

## 1. Terms: literature versus the blog

The literature uses "subject", "stream", "mind" and "person" with senses that cut across the blog's. Misreading this table produces false agreements.

| Literature term | Source | Nearest blog term |
|---|---|---|
| "Conscious subject" = a bundle of experiences "individuated by maximal relations of phenomenal unity" | Schwitzgebel & Nelson 2025, §2 [read] | **Field**, not subject. Their indeterminate counts are counts of fields (§6 below). |
| "Subject", "mind", "centre of consciousness" for a hemisphere or alter | Schechter 2018, Braude 1995 [reported] | Possibly **regulatory economy**. Schechter's individuating test is "direct" versus "indirect" interaction (§2.3), which resembles the blog's reach criterion [inference]. |
| "Stream" | Bayne, Schechter, Pinto | A field extended in time. |
| "Person" | Schechter ("one person, two minds"); Braude ("not one thing") | The blog's planned "personhood": self-governance, independent of phenomenality. |
| Phenomenal unity: subsumption by a single experience, or primitive "co-consciousness" | Bayne & Chalmers 2003 §2–3 [read]; Lockwood 1994 [read] | Field-formation by binding; the "experience of the joints" in the brief's wording. |
| Access unity, agency unity, unity of self-consciousness | Bayne & Chalmers 2003; Schechter & Bayne 2021 [abstract] | Not field unity. Schechter & Bayne's *agency-based* versus *experience-based* unity is the nearest the literature has to the blog's person/field split. |
| "Self" (IFS) | Schwartz | Not the blog's "self". See §5. |

**Counting.** The literature runs two questions together that the blog separates: how many fields are there, and how many owners (economies)? Schechter's "two subjects, one person" separates a third.

## 2. Split-brain

### 2.1 The landscape [read: SEP §3.1; reported for the rest]

The SEP entry on the unity of consciousness (Brook & Raymont) §3.1 sorts the options as follows. [read]

- **Two-streams model**: two simultaneous independent streams, one per hemisphere.
- **Switch model** (Bayne): one stream, hosted by one hemisphere at a time, switching with attention.
- **Partial-unity model** (Lockwood 1989): one subject whose experiences are not all unified with each other.
- **Hybrids**: for example, disunified under demanding experimental conditions, unified in undemanding everyday ones (Hurley 1998, Tye 2003, Downey 2018, Godfrey-Smith 2022). Godfrey-Smith (2020, ch. 6) combines two streams with partial unity [reported].

Two recent models add to the list.

- **"Conscious unity, split perception"** (Pinto, de Haan & Lamme 2017): "one conscious agent who experiences two parallel, unintegrated streams of information" [abstract].
- **Layered unity** (Lopez 2025): local experiential layers can break down while global ones stay unified [abstract].

### 2.2 Bayne

- *The Unity of Consciousness* (OUP 2010). The split-brain chapter is ch. 9, "The Split-Brain Syndrome", pp. 189–222. Earlier chapters on phenomenal unity are ch. 2 "Mereology" (pp. 20–46) and ch. 3 "Closure" (pp. 47–72). Ch. 1 is "The Phenomenal Field". DOI: [10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199215386.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199215386.001.0001). I did not read the book. [reported]
- Switch model first in "The Unity of Consciousness and the Split-Brain Syndrome", *Journal of Philosophy* 105 (6), 2008, 277–300, [10.5840/jphil2008105638](https://doi.org/10.5840/jphil2008105638). [reported]
- The SEP's account of the switch model [read]:
  - "split-brain subjects have a single unified stream of consciousness that alternates between the hemispheres, depending on which hemisphere wins the competition for focal attention."
  - Bayne's argument runs from the Unity Thesis plus the premise that patients remain single subjects.
  - Bayne's three explanations of everyday unity: (a) a single conscious hemisphere predominantly controls behaviour, (b) rapid switching creates the illusion of unity, (c) non-focal attention allows a unified stream across both hemispheres.
  - Challenges, as the SEP lists them: evidence of inter-hemispheric integration; the near-absence of signs of inter-hemispheric conflict, which Bayne himself "admits... remains the most serious challenge"; and Hill's (2018) objection that competition for focal attention is mostly intra-hemispheric.
  - The SEP's own observation: "the switch model is structurally similar to the two-stream model." The difference is whether both hemispheres are conscious at once. The dispute therefore turns on how attention relates to consciousness.
- Bayne's unity thesis, as one source puts it: "Necessarily, for any conscious subject of experience (S) and any time (t), the simultaneous conscious states that S has at t will be subsumed by a single conscious state—the subject's total conscious state." [reported; verify wording against the book]. Bayne & Chalmers 2003 state the thesis as "Necessarily, any set of phenomenal states of a subject at a time is phenomenally unified." [read]

### 2.3 Schechter

- *Self-Consciousness and "Split" Brains: The Minds' I* (OUP 2018), [10.1093/oso/9780198809654.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198809654.001.0001). [reported only; book not read]
- Thesis, as Godfrey-Smith's NDPR review puts it: "within the body of a split-brain patient, in at least some cases, two experiencing subjects exist", yet the patient is "just one person"; "those two units need not line up in a simple way." [reported: NDPR review, Godfrey-Smith 2020]
  - The reconciliation depends on "how each of us is transformed by self-consciousness" [reported].
  - The review adds that the book "hardly discusses" partial unity (an appendix finds the evidence from affective states "not very convincing") and gives switching "some, but very little, consideration" [reported].
- The SEP's rendering of her reason for two subjects: "in some split-brains the two hemispheres interact mainly indirectly, where indirect interaction is interaction between mental states that is mediated by action and perception. Since direct interaction is the mark of the singularity of subjects... there are two subjects." [reported: SEP note 52]
  - **[inference]** This is close to the blog's reach-and-latency criterion (monastery draft, pairs P2/P4). It is the same move: the count of subjects tracks whether coupling reaches the regulated vehicles or only their finished outputs.
- Her maxim, as the SEP reconstructs the argument against partial unity [reported: SEP §3.1.4]:
  - Suppose E1 and E2 are not unified with each other but both are unified with C3.
  - Then a single experience E3 (with content C3) is shared between two perspectives P1 and P2.
  - If P1 and P2 belong to distinct subjects, E3 would belong to two subjects. That "conflicts with the maxim that we should partially individuate experiences in terms of their subjects."
  - The SEP's reply: if subjects are grounded in physical facts, two subjects may share physical correlates sufficient for an experience.
  - **[inference]** The maxim is exactly the exclusivity premise *Can Two Minds Share One Experience* rejects.
- Related Schechter papers (not read): "Individuating Mental Tokens: The Split-Brain Case", *Philosophia* 38 (1), 2010, 195–216, [10.1007/s11406-009-9187-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11406-009-9187-3); "The Switch Model of Split-Brain Consciousness", *Philosophical Psychology* 25 (2), 2012, 203–226, [10.1080/09515089.2011.579417](https://doi.org/10.1080/09515089.2011.579417); "The Unity of Consciousness: Subjects and Objectivity", *Philosophical Studies* 165 (2), 2013, 671–692, [10.1007/s11098-012-9970-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-012-9970-z); "The Subject in Neuropsychology: Individuating Minds in the Split-Brain Case", *Mind & Language* 30 (5), 2015, 501–525, [10.1111/mila.12088](https://doi.org/10.1111/mila.12088); "Partial Unity of Consciousness: A Preliminary Defense", in Bennett & Hill (eds.), *Sensory Integration and the Unity of Consciousness*, MIT Press 2014, 347–374, [10.7551/mitpress/9780262027786.003.0015](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/9780262027786.003.0015). Précis in *JCS* 29 (1), 2022, 142–152, [10.53765/20512201.29.1.142](https://doi.org/10.53765/20512201.29.1.142), with commentary by Godfrey-Smith (153–159, [10.53765/20512201.29.1.153](https://doi.org/10.53765/20512201.29.1.153)).

### 2.4 Pinto et al. 2017 and the critiques

**The paper.** Yair Pinto, David A. Neville, Marte Otten, Paul M. Corballis, Victor A. F. Lamme, Edward H. F. de Haan, Nicoletta Foschi, Mara Fabri, "Split brain: divided perception but undivided consciousness", *Brain* 140 (5), 2017, 1231–1237, [10.1093/brain/aww358](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/aww358). [read, full text]

- Two patients, D.D.C. and D.D.V., operated at ages 19 and 22 and tested 10–16 and 17–23 years after surgery. The authors raise this themselves: the unity might be learned or transient.
- They replicate the standard finding that stimuli cannot be compared across visual half-fields (Experiment 2).
- But each patient responded to stimuli in either hemifield with either hand or verbally. They showed "full awareness of presence, and well above chance-level recognition of location, orientation and identity of stimuli throughout the entire visual field". Confidence ratings were used as the index of conscious awareness, and response type did not affect performance on high-confidence trials.
- Conclusion, verbatim: "severing the cortical connections between hemispheres splits visual perception, but does not create two independent conscious perceivers within one brain."
- Four reasons given against cross-cueing:
  1. Cross-cueing carries about one bit, but localisation needs more.
  2. Hemifield capabilities differed, yet response type made no difference.
  3. The set-up prevented the hands touching.
  4. Ipsilateral and contralateral responses were nearly equally fast (1229 ms versus 1307 ms, P = 0.13) and equally accurate.
- Theoretical claim: a challenge to Global Workspace and IIT; "may fit well" with local recurrent processing. In their closing words: "This 'split phenomenality' combined with 'unity of consciousness' is difficult to grasp introspectively."
- **[inference]** On the blog's variants, the pattern (no cross-field comparison, but unified report and control) is the signature of absent cross-hemifield binding plus a shared executive/response route. Which variant that favours depends on whether the shared route reaches the regulated vehicles. See §2.6.

### 2.5 2019–2026 updates

- **Consensus paper.** E. H. F. de Haan, P. M. Corballis, S. A. Hillyard, C. A. Marzi, A. Seth, V. A. F. Lamme, L. Volz, M. Fabri, E. Schechter, T. Bayne, M. Corballis, Y. [read]
  - "The current consensus is that the body of evidence is insufficient to answer this question."
  - "callosotomy leads to a broad breakdown of functional integration ranging from perception to attention. However, the breakdown is not absolute as several processes, such as action control, seem to remain unified."
  - They add that "the answers might not be a simple yes or no but that intermediate conceptualizations need to be considered."
  - The conclusion lists four candidate intermediates: (i) a non-whole number of conscious agents (Nagel; Perry 2009); (ii) a whole number of agents with partially unified consciousness (Lockwood; Schechter); (iii) a depersonalization-like reading in which "the number of agents is not altered, but the agent feels depersonalized in some situations"; (iv) the usual yes/no options.

  - Starting point: patients "can consciously perceive and identify two stimuli presented simultaneously but separately in opposite hemifields. Yet, they seem to lack a conscious experience of the two stimuli together, as they cannot judge whether these are the same or different."

  - **[inference]** This is the hybrid variant's shape: local character, global binding, with a seam in the local layer.
- **Santander et al. 2025**, "Full interhemispheric integration sustained by a fraction of posterior callosal fibers", *PNAS* 122 (43), e2520190122, [10.1073/pnas.2520190122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2520190122) (authors include Gazzaniga, Volz, Miller). [abstract]

Complete callosotomy patients showed "sweeping disruptions".

Gottlieb & Fischer, "Counting subjects", *Inquiry* 69 (4), 2024, 2450–2477, [10.1080/0020174x.2024.2370016](https://doi.org/10.1080/0020174x.2024.2370016), is cited there for methods of establishing multiple subjects in one organism. ### 2.6 What the split-brain data bear on, in the blog's terms [inference]

- **Variant test.** The global variant requires a workspace whose broadcast returns. Severing the callosum leaves either two such loops, one hemisphere's, or a subcortical loop shared by both. The data (cross-field comparison fails; control is unified) fit all three. Pinto et al. themselves read the data as favouring local recurrence. The blog's monastery draft has disowned the purely local variant, since it leaves field-formation outside phenomenality.
- **Where counts come apart.** Schechter ("two subjects, one person"), Bayne (one subject, one field at a time) and Pinto (one agent, two unintegrated streams) can all accept the same data. They disagree about which of three things the data show: unity of agency, unity of field, or the number of economies.
- **Reach, latency, bandwidth.** The data cannot be sorted until one asks whether the residual routes (subcortical, ipsilateral motor, cross-cueing) reach vehicles still under regulation, fast enough to correct them, at their grain. The three hypotheses of the consensus paper are three answers to that question.

## 3. Partial unity

### 3.1 Lockwood

- *Mind, Brain and the Quantum: The Compound 'I'* (Blackwell 1989), pp. 87–94 on non-transitive co-consciousness (page range from Lockwood's own citation in 1994). [reported; book not read]
- Primary text read: Michael Lockwood, "Issues of Unity and Objectivity", in Christopher Peacocke (ed.), *Objectivity, Simulation and the Unity of Consciousness*, *Proceedings of the British Academy* 83, 1994, pp. 89–95 (the PDF is the commentary on Hurley). [read]
- Hurley's illustrative case (after Sergent): a split-brain patient has a "6" projected into one hemisphere and a "7" into the other. Neither side can report whether the numbers match, which "implies that the perception of '6' is not co-conscious with the perception of '7'". Yet both sides can report that the left number is lower than the right. Lockwood's gloss: the awareness of "6 as lower" is co-conscious with the perception of "6", and with the awareness of "7 as higher", which is co-conscious with the perception of "7". "One way of making sense of all this is to suppose that co-consciousness is a non-transitive relation."
- Hurley's definitions as Lockwood quotes them (Hurley 1994, "Unity and Objectivity", same volume; p. 57 for "indexed agglomeration", p. 68 for weak unity):
  - *strong unity*: every token conscious state of a subject at a time is co-conscious with every other.
  - *weak unity*: "Each token conscious state of that subject at that time will be co-conscious with some other token conscious state of that subject at that time, rather than with every other."
- Lockwood's repair. The weak-unity definition is "either far too weak, or implicitly circular". A split-brain patient with no bridging co-consciousness would satisfy it, and counting that patient as two subjects makes the definition depend on what determines one subject. "The correct definition of a weakly unified consciousness is, of course, that it is one in which any two simultaneous token conscious states stand to each other in the ancestral of the relation, is co-conscious and simultaneous with." So in a weakly unified consciousness any two states are "linked by a chain of simultaneous states, each of which is separately co-conscious with its two immediate neighbours in the chain."
  - **[inference]** This is the "joints" structure stated as a graph property: the field is connected by chains of pairwise joints, but not complete.
- Lockwood's motive for non-transitivity was a gradual-severance thought experiment (callosum cut fibre by fibre): "the idea of a successive 'snipping' of relations of co-consciousness between mental states, with its implication of a failure of transitivity, accordingly came to mind." This makes sense of Nagel's suggestion that unity is "not an all-or-nothing affair". (The same shape appears in Roelofs's merging series, which Schwitzgebel & Nelson adopt, §6.)
- His doubt, also from 1994: "I am still by no means wholly persuaded that the concept of a merely weakly unified consciousness really does make sense. Like Nagel (1979, pp. 160, 163), I am still unable to project myself into the position of a subject with a partially unified and partially disunified consciousness." He is also now "agnostic" between duplication of contents and non-transitivity for the Sergent-type affective case.
- On the data: Anthony Marcel's commentary in the same volume (pp. 79–88) disputes Hurley's reading of Sergent's data: "the validity of empirical data and their relevance to particular theoretical issues are highly difficult to establish". [read, first page only]

### 3.2 Hurley

- *Consciousness in Action* (Harvard UP 1998). Hurley's remark on imaginability, quoted in Bayne & Chalmers 2003 §3 [read]: "Therefore, we cannot imagine what it is like for there to be partial unity. That doesn't show partial unity is unintelligible, because being partially unified isn't the sort of thing there could be anything it is like to be. We shouldn't expect to be able to imagine what it is like." (Hurley 1998, p. 165, as cited by Bayne & Chalmers.)
- "Action, the unity of consciousness, and vehicle externalism", in Axel Cleeremans (ed.), *The Unity of Consciousness: Binding, Integration, and Dissociation*, OUP 2003, pp. 72–92 (Crossref pagination), [10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198508571.003.0004](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198508571.003.0004). [chapter not read]
- Hurley's position as the SEP reports it: unity in the split-brain everyday case may be sustained by vehicle externalism, with reafferent sensorimotor feedback outside the brain unifying the hemispheres (also developed by Downey 2018 and Godfrey-Smith 2020/2022). [reported: SEP §3.1.2]
  - **[inference]** The external loop through body and world is a candidate for the "reach" the blog's criterion asks about. Hurley's proposal is that a loop through action and perception closes fast enough to unify. The blog's monastery draft treats speech as too slow and narrow. A bodily sensorimotor loop within one organism is much faster than a conversation, so the blog's own latency criterion leaves this case open.
- Downey, "Split-brain syndrome and extended perceptual consciousness", *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 17 (4), 2018, 787–811, [10.1007/s11097-017-9550-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11097-017-9550-y). [title only]

### 3.3 Bayne's objection: what is confirmed

- **Confirmed [read].** Bayne & Chalmers 2003, §3 ("Access Unity and Phenomenal Unity") argue as follows.
  - Phenomenal unity is *subsumptive*: two states are unified when a single state subsumes both.
  - They distinguish the Phenomenal Unity Thesis ("any set of phenomenal states of a subject at a time is phenomenally unified"), the pairwise thesis and the total thesis, and argue that the total thesis captures "the central intuition".
  - They explicitly consider partial unity ("two phenomenal states are each unified with a third state, but not with each other") and quote Hurley's reply above.
  - Their verdict: "a case in which the unity of consciousness breaks down would be precisely a case in which there is no total phenomenal state of the subject... Such a subject would have states with a local phenomenal character, but there would be no global phenomenal character involved in having these states. It is certainly very hard to see how this could be the case. Indeed, one might suspect (as we do) that such a scenario is impossible and perhaps incoherent."
  - They add that this is not triviality: "it is nontrivial that there will be something it is like to be in the conjunctive state."
  - Bayne & Chalmers, "What Is the Unity of Consciousness?", in Cleeremans (ed.) 2003, pp. 23–58, [10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198508571.003.0002](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198508571.003.0002). Free PDF: consc.net/papers/unity.pdf.
- **Reported.**
  - The SEP lists Nagel 1971 (pp. 409–410), Bayne 2008, Bayne 2010 and Dainton 2000 as sources of the a priori skepticism, and notes that "even Lockwood expresses skepticism about the cogency of the concept of partial unity (1994, 95)." [SEP note 50]
  - It distinguishes a simple version (we cannot imagine it) from a stronger one, quoted from Schechter 2014: "there is nothing that would even count as successfully imagining what it is like to have a partially unified consciousness." [SEP §3.1.4]
  - Schwitzgebel & Nelson 2025, note 23 and note 24, classify: *defenders* of intransitive unity are Lockwood 1989, Tye 2003, Schechter 2014, Salisbury 2023, Yetter-Chappell (in draft); *objectors* are Dainton 2000, Hurley 2003, Bayne 2010, Vogel 2014. The imaginability objection is attributed to Dainton (2000) and Bayne (2010). Salisbury and Vogel details not checked. [read the notes; sources behind them not read]
  - Schwitzgebel & Nelson 2025, n. 25, cite Dainton (2000, ch. 4, p. 98) and Schechter (2014) for a similar reply to the inconceivability challenge, even though they list Dainton among the objectors in n. 23. [read the note; Dainton not read]
- **Not confirmed.** Bayne's own formulation in the 2010 book (chs. 2–3 and 9) and in the 2008 paper. The wording "unintelligibility objection" in the brief is not a phrase I found in any text I read. I could not open the book or the 2008 paper (all copies blocked or paywalled).
- **Reconstruction [inference]:** Bayne's argument has two components. (a) A *closure* premise: if a subject has experiences S1 (subsuming A and B) and S2 (subsuming B and C) at one time, the subject has an experience subsuming S1 and S2, hence A, B and C. (b) An *imagination* premise: nothing could count as what it is like to have the S1, S2 pair without the subsuming state.

### 3.4 Replies

- **Hurley** (above): unintelligibility of a state does not follow from the inability to imagine what it is like, since partial unity is not the kind of thing there is anything it is like to be.
- **Schechter 2014**, as the SEP renders it [reported]:
  - The imaginability argument, the SEP says, is hard to confine to partial unity, since there is no single subjective point of view from which to imagine two separate streams either. So it cannot be used to prefer the single-stream view on a priori grounds.
  - Impossibility of fully projecting into a partially unified perspective is explained: "any perspective that would reflect the subjective point of view of such a subject would be either partial... or it would represent it incorrectly".
  - Her own objection to partial unity is empirical and about individuation, via the maxim (§2.3).
- **Schwitzgebel & Nelson 2025**, §6.2.2 [read]: the demand is "implicitly self-contradictory": the objector "seeks to imagine, in a transitively unified way, what it would be like to have intransitively unified experiences."
  - "Intransitively unified experiences aren't like any one thing, or part of any one phenomenal perspective."
  - A creature with intransitively unified imagination could imagine them in a similarly distributed way ("Maybe Marcel's Arm 1 is unified with his head, and his head is unified with Arm 6, but Arms 1 and 6 are not unified"; Marcel is the Proust character here, not the psychologist Anthony Marcel).
  - Our inability is "human limitations, not metaphysical or nomological impossibility", on a par with bat echolocation.

### 3.5 Does a "joints" framing avoid Bayne's objection? [inference throughout]

Setting. In the blog's draft vocabulary: a field is an experience composed of experiences, adding the experience of their being present together. Partial unity then reads: there are experiences A, B, C; a joint-experience J_AB (A and B present together) and J_BC; no J_AC; and no J_ABC.

1. **Closure is the premise at stake, and the blog already denies the analogous premise elsewhere.** A mereological sum of J_AB and J_BC exists as a physical event (the union of their vehicles). Whether it is an *experience* depends, by the blog's own account, on whether a regulation binds it: contents are "checked against each other and against the whole, and the corrections that hold them together return to the vehicles" (*Experience, Selfhood*). The same essay denies that a locally organized fragment becomes an experience for that reason, and says cognition binds without producing a field. So the joints framing rejects closure at no extra cost: if the economy runs two overlapping bindings and no third, there is no total experience and nothing for closure to guarantee.
2. **That answers the metaphysical form of the objection but pays in organization.** The framing requires one economy to run overlapping local bindings without a global one. This is the hybrid variant with the global layer missing. Whether such an economy is possible turns on propagation: if A's state constrains B's and B's constrains C's, then A and C are co-regulated indirectly. The blog's sharing criterion demands compatible regulatory involvement "sustained over the relevant interval" at the level of the vehicle. So partial unity needs B to act as an insulated relay: its two bindings must transmit no A–C constraint at the grain of the vehicles. Lockwood's chain definition and Hurley's 6/7 example have exactly this shape. Whether human anatomy provides such an insulated relay is empirical and, as Marcel noted, contested for the Sergent data.
3. **It does not remove the imaginability form.** The joints framing inherits the replies of §3.4. It adds one point: each joint is individually familiar (what it is like for A and B to be together). What is unfamiliar is the absence of a joint, and an absence is not an experience. So the objection demands a single experience that contains an absence of unity, which is Schwitzgebel & Nelson's "paradoxical demand".
4. **A gain: ownership is no longer the obstacle.** Schechter's maxim says E3 cannot belong to two subjects. In the blog, ownership goes with regulation, so the same overlapping structure has two readings that the field-topology cannot tell apart.
   - *Intra-subject partial unity*: one economy sustains J_AB and J_BC, with B owned once.
   - *Shared field*: two economies each sustain one field, overlapping in B (the Hogan-twin pattern).
   The two readings differ in regulatory topology, not in the graph of experiences. This is precisely a field/subject dissociation, and the evidence that decides between them is about regulation (§7), not about what the field is like.
5. **A cost: counting.** Overlapping fields are countable as sets (Schwitzgebel & Nelson 2025, §6.2.3: "determinately two sets that share 99.9999% of their contents"), but they add that calling each one a "subject" "seems wrongheaded" in a Massive Overlap case, where one green dot in the left periphery and one red dot in the right are the only things not unified. The joints framing has to say when to count overlapping fields as two fields and when as one field with a seam (Lopez's "local layers", the S&N "ever-so-slightly disunified" subject).
6. **Net assessment.** The joints framing is a principled way to deny closure and to let the imagination objection fail for the standard reason. It does not by itself show that partial unity is realized, and it creates a new empirical question: can one economy keep two overlapping bindings from merging? The view stands or falls on that, not on conceivability.

- Dimitrova et al. 2024, *Journal of Psychiatric Research* 174, 220–229, [10.1016/j.jpsychires.2024.04.026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2024.04.026): 14 patients, 16 simulators, 16 healthy controls; "increased cognitive control over self-relevant trauma-related knowledge processing... calls for the redefinition of 'inter-identity amnesia' to 'inter-identity avoidance'."

**What these findings do and do not show** [inference]:

- **Information is shared.** What differs across alters is what the economy *does* with accessible content (avoid, disown), which is regulation, not storage. This is consistent with the blog's picture of subjects as regulatory configurations.
- **The states are not mere enactments** (simulators differ), so identity states are real differences in regulation over shared machinery.
- **No study tests concurrency.** Everything here concerns successive states or reports. The simultaneous "co-conscious" cases the SEP discusses have not been tested with no-report or perturbation methods that could separate two fields from one field containing an other-voiced content.
- **Shared perceptual stream.** The SEP point that alters share the room across a switch suggests *one field passed between regulatory configurations* (a field with changing owners), not two fields. That is the owner/field dissociation of kind II in §7.

## 5. The parts approach: Internal Family Systems

### 5.1 What IFS claims [read: IFS Institute model outline; Schwartz, *No Bad Parts* excerpt]

Primary texts: (a) "The Internal Family Systems Model Outline", IFS Institute, ifs-institute.com/resources/articles/internal-family-systems-model-outline, fetched 17 Oct 2026; (b) Richard C. Schwartz, *No Bad Parts: Healing Trauma and Restoring Wholeness with the Internal Family Systems Model* (Sounds True, 2021), publisher's excerpt PDF on the IFS Institute site (pagination in the excerpt: pp. 7, 14, 22). I did not read Schwartz's clinical text (Schwartz 1995; Schwartz & Sweezy, *Internal Family Systems Therapy*, 2nd ed., Guilford 2020).

*Parts* (Outline):

- "It is the nature of the mind to be subdivided into an indeterminate number of subpersonalities or parts."
- "Subpersonalities are aspects of our personality that interact internally in sequences and styles that are similar to the ways in which people interact."
- "Parts may be experienced in any number of ways -- thoughts, feelings, sensations, images, and more."
- "While experiences affect parts, parts are not created by the experiences. They are always in existence, either as potential or actuality."
- Roles: exiles ("Young parts that have experienced trauma and often become isolated from the rest of the system"), managers ("Parts that run the day-to-day life"), firefighters ("react when exiles are activated").
- Extreme parts carry "burdens", which are not part of their nature, and can "unburden".

*Self* (Outline, sections I and IV):

- "Different level of entity than the parts -- often in the center of the 'you' that the parts are talking to or that likes or dislikes, listens to, or shuts out various parts".
- "Everyone has a Self, and the Self can and should lead the individual's internal system."
- "When completely differentiated from all parts (Self alone), people describe a feeling of being 'centered.'"
- "When the individual is 'in Self'... the Self is experienced along with the non-extreme aspects of the parts."
- Schwartz (2021, p. 22): clients asked what part of them is the calm, curious one answer "That's not a part like these others, that's more myself"... "the Self is in everybody. Furthermore, the Self cannot be damaged, the Self doesn't have to develop, and the Self possesses its own wisdom about how to heal internal as well as external relationships."
- The "8 Cs" of Self-energy (calm, curiosity, clarity, compassion, confidence, courage, creativity, connectedness) are widely reported; I did not find them in the two primary texts above. [reported, Wikipedia citing Schwartz 2019]

*Blending and unblending* (Outline):

- "Parts that have lost trust in the leadership of the Self will 'blend' with or take over the Self."
- Blending as a danger: "keeping the feelings of the part from overwhelming the Self."
- Technique: "Helping Self to distance from/unblend from parts."

*Relation to DID* (Schwartz 2021, p. 7): "What are called alters in those people are the same as what I call parts in IFS, and they exist in all of us. The only difference is that people with Dissociative Identity Disorder suffered horrible abuse and their system of parts got blown apart more than yours and mine, so each part stands out in bolder relief and are more disconnected from and polarized with each other." He also says (p. 14) the model began when bulimic clients talked about their parts "as if they had a lot of autonomy—as if they could take over and make them do things they didn't want to do", and he feared "an outbreak of Multiple Personality Disorder".

It is the nearest tested relative of IFS's claim that the mind contains sub-systems with their own perspective, and Schwartz himself identifies parts with alters. ### 5.4 Mapping blending and unblending onto field and subject [inference throughout]

1. **What a part is, in the blog's terms.** IFS says parts are "experienced" as "thoughts, feelings, sensations, images" and that they address "you". So parts show up *as contents of one field*, with the "you" as centre. IFS does not say that each part sustains a field of its own. The natural reading: **parts are partial regulatory configurations (sub-economies) inside one economy, with their contents present in one field**. A part becomes a candidate for a field of its own only in the DID limit that Schwartz equates with a more extreme version.
2. **Blending versus unblending as a change in mineness, not in the number of fields.** A blended part's regulation dominates how the field is centred and its contents are felt with mineness ("I am furious"). When unblended, the content stays in the field but is no longer constitutive of the centring, and is felt as that part's ("a part of me is furious"). On the *Experience, Selfhood* distinction this alters mineness (and the agentive layer) while for-me-ness (co-presence in one binding economy) persists. Unblending would then resemble a controlled, reversible and benign version of depersonalization's loss of felt ownership, restricted to one content. **Testable** with ownership and mineness measures: unblending should lower mineness for the target content with no loss of field unity or access. Unblending should also not reduce a content's causal role until the part has "stepped back"; if it did, that would suggest the part's regulation really does hold the field's centre.
3. **What IFS's Self might be.** The outline's own wording gives three candidates.
   - (a) *The owner*: the whole regulatory economy when no part dominates. That fits "a different level of entity than the parts".
   - (b) *The centre of the field*: "the center of the 'you' that the parts are talking to". On the blog's view that is perspectival organization, which is a feature of the owned field, not an owner.
   - (c) *A mode of regulation*: the 8 Cs read as properties of non-defensive integrative regulation. This reading drops the claim that Self is an entity.
   The blog's slogan "a field is owned, not an owner" blocks reading (b) as a self. Schwartz's claim that "the Self cannot be damaged" is hard to square with (a), since regulatory economies can be damaged; it fits (c) only if the capacity is latent and recoverable. That claim is the point at which IFS's Self is a candidate for a distinct natural category of the kind *Experience, Selfhood* asks about. The test is the one that essay sets: do the capacities sustain one another, does disrupting their coordination do something other than lose one component? No such study exists that I found.
4. **"Parts exist before experience" versus construction.** IFS says parts "are always in existence, either as potential or actuality". The empirical analogue is the sociocognitive versus trauma debate on DID (§4.2). A test: do independent elicitations of one person's parts converge (inventories, behavioural and physiological signatures) or track the interviewer's prompts? I found no such test for non-clinical IFS.
5. **Continuity with DID is an empirical prediction.** If alters are "the same as" parts in more extreme form, non-clinical parts should show attenuated versions of identity-state-dependent physiology and of "inter-identity avoidance" (§4.2). If, conversely, DID alters sustain separate fields and parts do not, the continuity claim fails at exactly the field/subject boundary this dossier is about.

## 6. Schwitzgebel & Nelson and indeterminate counts of subjects

### 6.1 The 2023 paper [read: the author's preprint dated 29 June 2023; I did not see the typeset version]

Eric Schwitzgebel and Sophie R. Nelson, "Introspection in Group Minds, Disunities of Consciousness, and Indiscrete Persons", *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 30 (9–10), 2023, 188–203, [10.53765/20512201.30.9.188](https://doi.org/10.53765/20512201.30.9.188). Responds to Kammerer & Frankish's call to widen the notion of introspection.

- **Case.** A computer, Aleph, in orbit, and 200 humanoid robots in ten platoons, in constant satellite contact (modelled on Leckie's *Ancillary Justice*). If all cognition is in Aleph there is one mind; if all in the robots, 200; the target is the intermediate case where connectivity is "swift, thick, and direct, but not so much that the ancillary entity constitutes a determinate case of a single, unified mind." Perception, action and memory are worked through (conflict-resolution procedures, action-plan strength, external memory).
- **Counts.** The determinate number might be 1 or 201, but "202 minds (one for each robot, one for Aleph, and one for the whole ancillary system), or 10 minds..., or a large number of partly overlapping minds".
- **Thesis.** The system "occupies a gray zone with respect to personal identity and subject individuation, neither determinately one person or subject nor determinately many". Some of its processes are "neither determinately introspection within a mind nor determinately communication between minds."
- **Argument for indeterminacy.** A mind-merging sorites series (Roelofs 2019): connect two minds bit by bit; if the individuation of minds rests on degrees of connectivity, there must be borderline cases.
- **Three resistances** they discuss: the Body View (counting bodies; set aside), the Phase Transition View (a sudden functional change at the true boundary; "empirically implausible" as a general claim) and **Discrete Phenomenal Realism** (phenomenality is necessarily discrete: either there is something it is like to be each robot, or something it is like to be the whole). They note DPR "fits nicely with Bayne's (2010) account" and sketch the picture: loci with neat co-occurrence relations, "no free-floating experiential states had by no subject", "the result will always be a whole number".
- **Two problems for DPR**: sharp ontological lines atop fuzzy continua, and cases of "apparent partial experiential overlap".
- **The overlap case ("the box").** Robots A1 and A2 share a hovering box in which most cognition occurs; A1 has experiences α (local) plus β, γ, δ (from the box); A2 has β, γ, δ plus ε. The DPR theorist has three options: Impossibility, **Sharing** (two minds, token-identical β, γ, δ) or **Similarity** (two minds, type-identical but numerically distinct β, γ, δ). Sharing "violat[es] the standard view that phenomenal co-occurrence relationships are transitive". Similarity gives a "peculiar doubling of experience tokens".
- **The switch dilemma.** Suppose a switch turns off the processes giving α and ε, so that A1's and A2's total experiences become identical. On Sharing, either the minds collapse to one, so "a relatively small change in the phenomenal field can result in a radical reconfiguration of the number of minds" (worse with 200 robots and 999,999 shared experiences plus one unique each), or they do not, so "multiple distinct minds could have the very same token-identical experiences grounded in the very same cognitive processors", with "metaphysical differences but not phenomenological ones". Similarity faces parallel problems.
- **Earth cases (§4, one paragraph).** DID (Braude 1995; Radden 1996; Maiese 2016), split-brain (Schechter 2018), craniopagus twins (Cochrane 2021) and perhaps octopuses (Godfrey-Smith 2016). "Faced with the Hogan twins, or with Jekyll-Hyde, the correct answer to the number of minds or persons might be neither one nor two but something indeterminate between." The paper does not analyse these cases beyond naming them.

### 6.2 The 2025 paper [read: preprint of 28 May 2025]

Schwitzgebel & Nelson, "When counting conscious subjects, the result needn't always be a determinate whole number", *Philosophical Psychology* 39 (3), 2025, 847–867, [10.1080/09515089.2025.2520364](https://doi.org/10.1080/09515089.2025.2520364).

- **Definition (§2).** "Conscious subjects, as we'll understand them here, are just such bundles of experiences, individuated by maximal relations of phenomenal unity." They say outright that these need not be identical to "cognitive systems, bodies, persons, or even selves". They take no stand on top-down (subsumption; Bayne & Chalmers) versus bottom-up (primitive co-consciousness; Dainton) analyses of unity.
- **Two ways of failing whole-number countability** (§6.2): *indeterminate or intermediate unity among determinately conscious experiences* (the strength of unity is graded or vague), and *intransitive unity* (overlapping fields sharing token experiences).
- **Intransitive unity** (§6.2.2): the Tiny Overlap case (two alien brains share one faint motor sound), the Massive Overlap case (a million shared experiences; a green dot in the left periphery and a red dot in the right are the only ones not unified, so "a single conscious subject who is ever-so-slightly disunified"), and the continuum between them as the aliens' brains fuse: "two partially overlapping fields of experience that would plausibly count as neither determinately one nor two subjects."
- **Countability recovered** (§6.2.3): overlapping fields can be counted as sets or regions, "determinately two sets that share 99.9999% of their contents". But calling each a "subject" "seems wrongheaded" in Massive Overlap, and "this restoration of countability... works only for the intransitive unity case."
- **Inconceivability replies** (§6): the "paradoxical demand" reply (§3.4 above).
- **Also in the paper.** A zero-to-one quadrilemma; the point that on Global Workspace, higher-order and "fame in the brain" theories, workspaces or higher-order systems can be partial and overlapping; Dennett's multiple drafts as precedent (Dennett 1991, pp. 424–425: "refusing to countenance the possibility of quasi-selves, semi-selves, transitional selves"); IIT's mathematics "will always generate a whole number" (n. 14) and so creates implausibly sharp lines.
- **Footnote 7** cites Schechter 2018 for "split-brain patients are two conscious minds in one body" and Gottlieb & Fischer 2024 for methods of determining that one organism hosts multiple conscious subjects.
- **The footnote 21 triad** is the paper's own caution on indeterminate (as opposed to intermediate) unity: if there is no vagueness at the fundamental level, and phenomenal unity is fundamental (Roelofs), then phenomenal unity cannot be vague.

### 6.4 What this means for the blog's terms [inference]

- **The S&N count is a field count.** Their conscious subject is the maximal unity bundle, which is the blog's field. The blog has a second count (economies) and, with the personhood essay, a third (persons). Their indeterminacy argument concerns the first. Whether regulation-based economies are also borderline-countable is a separate argument, though the sorites reasoning transfers: regulatory coupling comes in degrees.
- **The switch dilemma is a test case for the blog's separation.** S&N's dilemma for Sharing assumes that the number of minds must follow the shape of the field (flip the switch and the minds collapse, or not). On the blog's criterion, ownership follows regulation. Flipping the switch changes the *fields* (A1 and A2 now have the same field contents) but not the *economies* (each still regulates its own part and the box jointly). So there is no collapse of minds, and the difficulty reappears only if one insists that minds are individuated by fields. The remaining oddity ("metaphysical differences but not phenomenological ones") is real, and the blog accepts it: subjects can differ in regulation where their fields are alike.
- **DPR and the blog.** The blog's identity claim (an experience is a concrete regulatory occurrence) does not require discrete counting, and *Experience, Selfhood* says its commitment is to "a determinate enough concrete constitution" with no requirement of "perfectly sharp thresholds everywhere". That is compatible with S&N, and incompatible with exclusion-style rules such as IIT's.
- **No free-floating experiences.** DPR denies "free-floating experiential states had by no subject". The belonging criterion gives the same result by construction: an occurrence constituted partly by someone's regulation has that someone as an owner, so a field with *no* owner is excluded. The open possibilities are fields with many owners and owners with many fields.

## 7. Mapping the cases onto the blog's terms

Kinds of dissociation, as used below.

- **I. More than one field per subject.** One economy, two or more fields (or overlapping fields).
- **II. A field without a single owner.** One field, more than one economy (shared, passed on, or indeterminate owner). A field with *no* owner is excluded by the belonging criterion.
- **III. Overlapping fields only.** Fields overlap or alternate, but the economy/field relation is the ordinary one (one economy per field, or one field at a time).
- **IV. Subject versus person.** The economy and the self-governing agent come apart.
- **V. Felt owner versus actual owner.** The field and the economy are ordinary; the representation of ownership (mineness) is what changes.

| Case | Best-supported reading(s) | Kind | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Split-brain: two streams** (Schechter) | Two economies, two fields, one person | **IV** (and a plain two-economy case) | Contested. The reasoning from "indirect interaction" is a reach criterion. |
| **Split-brain: one agent, split perception** (Pinto et al.) | One economy; either two unintegrated fields or one field with a seam | **I** if two fields; **III** if one field with a seam | Conceptually open (Schechter & Bayne 2021). The labels "agent" and "consciousness" in the source are not the blog's. |
| **Split-brain: switch** (Bayne) | One economy, one field at a time, re-hosted by hemisphere | **III** (field–vehicle dissociation only) | Contested: no conflict signs, attention objection. |
| **Partial unity** (Lockwood, Hurley, Schechter 2014) | One economy, overlapping fields, chain of joints | **I** | Coherence defended (§3.5); realization unproven. Same structure as a shared field with two economies. |
| **Layered unity** (Lopez 2025) | One global field with broken local layers | **III** | Model proposal. |
| **Hogan twins, S&N box, Goff–Roelofs sharing** | One field, two economies | **II** | Cochrane argues token sharing; no direct test. Already in the blog. |
| **Ancillary mind** (S&N 2023) | Indeterminate economy count | **II** with an indeterminate owner | Thought experiment. |
| **DID, successive alters** | One perceptual field passed between regulatory configurations of one organism | **II** (an owner passing the field on) | Identity-state physiology is real; information transfer survives reported amnesia; concurrency untested. |
| **DID, co-conscious alters** | Either two fields, or one field containing an other-voiced content | **I** if two fields; otherwise not a dissociation | Untested. |
| **Hypnosis (hidden observer)** | Not assessed | I? | Hilgard's work and Bayne's ch. 8 not read. |
| **IFS parts** | Sub-economies whose contents appear in one field; blending changes the centring and mineness | **V**, with a **I**/**II** candidate in the DID limit | Untested as a model of subject or field. |
| **Depersonalization** | Ordinary field and economy; mineness impaired | **V** | Clinical and imaging evidence on self-awareness; nothing on the field count. |
| **Indeterminate counts** (S&N 2025) | Counts of fields may be non-integer | A counting thesis across I, II and III | Philosophical argument; no empirical test. |

**What would decide each** (the evidence types are the ones the blog's own criteria name; the proposals are inference):

1. **Reach, latency and bandwidth of the residual couplings** decide I against II against a single field. The blog's monastery criterion asks whether a channel lands on vehicles still under regulation, fast enough to correct them, at their grain. Applied to the split-brain: do the subcortical, ipsilateral and cross-cueing routes (the three hypotheses of the 2020 consensus) reach the vehicles of both sides in time? Measurement: perturb one route (cooling or stimulation of a route such as the superior colliculus; timing manipulations of cross-cueing) and test the effect on the *other* side's contents at the grain of the vehicles.
2. **Joint tests at the grain of experience, not report.** Cross-field comparison fails in the split-brain (Pinto et al. Experiment 2) but report is a bottleneck. A field-level test needs no-report markers of binding across the seam (grouping, apparent motion, gist), as listed in the consensus paper's references. Presence of binding signatures says "one field", absence of them with intact responses says "joints missing". Cases where B binds with A and with C but A and C do not bind give partial unity.
3. **Insulated relay test for partial unity.** Is there a structure that binds with both sides yet does not transmit A–C constraints? This is the Lockwood–Hurley 6/7 pattern. Needs a replication with modern no-report methods, since Marcel disputed the original data.
4. **Concurrency probes for DID and hypnosis.** Present a stimulus to an alter's concurrent state while another is in control; measure subjective ratings, confidence-based awareness, neural markers (ignition, recurrent processing) and implicit effects, with simulators as controls. The current literature tests memory transfer between states and physiology within states, not simultaneity.
5. **Owner-level markers separate IV and V from I–III.** Unity of agency (self-governance, commitment, control of the body) is what Schechter & Bayne contrast with unity of experience. A case with unified agency and disunified experience is kind I; the reverse is kind IV.
6. **Intervention and dependence tests for IFS "Self".** The two questions in *Experience, Selfhood* ("Do these capacities tend to sustain one another? Does disrupting their coordination produce a recognizable change...?") can be put directly: do the 8 Cs covary, and does unblending shift them together? Does unblending lower mineness for a content while leaving field unity and access intact (§5.4)? No such study exists that I found.
7. **Elicitation independence for parts.** Whether parts are discovered or constructed is tested by whether independent elicitations converge, as the simulator-controlled DID work tests whether states are enactments.
8. **Counting.** The indeterminacy thesis is tested only by constructed series (S&N 2023/2025 build thought experiments). In biological cases, a parametric manipulation of coupling is the nearest thing: Santander et al.'s fibre-fraction result shows coupling can be varied in patients, though not on a consciousness measure.
