# Citation check for the mindedness outline: philosophy and memory science

18 Nov 2026. Checks the † citations of `mindedness-essay-outline.md` §1, §3, §4 and "Before drafting" (items 2 and 8).

Labels. **Confirmed (primary)**: text read. **Confirmed (abstract)**: only the abstract read. **Reported**: secondary source, named in the entry. Bibliographic data are from Crossref unless an entry says otherwise.

## Findings that bear on the argument

1. **Clark and Chalmers give four features, not three**, and the first two concern access, not trust. The first has a relevance clause (D).
2. **Rupert's telephone-directory case hits "No index, no extension".** Otto "remembers how to dial up the operator", so he holds an index into the directory. On the two-jobs split the directory, like the encyclopedia, would hold his standing states, unless reach or past endorsement joins the standing-state test (D).
3. **The forgotten-notebook pair does not separate the outline from Clark and Chalmers**: their first feature already fails there. A separating pair fixes the four features and varies how retrieval is keyed.
4. **Nichols and Stich support the second pass on assent.** One store serves pretence and hypothetical reasoning, and its outputs reach action as believed conditionals (B).
5. **Leslie states the quarantine rule**: inference applies inside the decoupling marks and keeps them (B).
6. **Carruthers's account covers attitudes only**, not sensory states or felt affect (B). This bears on §5 and on models' self-reports.
7. **Chaining is the account Lashley refuted** for skilled behaviour (E).
8. **Wearing's "just awakened" state is classed as a delusion**, and both patients still learned skills and habits (F).

## A. Fragmented belief

**Lewis, D. (1982). Logic for Equivocators. *Noûs* 16(3): 431–441.** Confirmed (primary). Lewis believed that Nassau Street ran roughly east–west, the railroad roughly north–south, and the two roughly parallel. "My system of beliefs was broken into (overlapping) fragments. Different fragments came into action in different situations, and the whole system of beliefs never manifested itself all at once." Inconsistencies are "quarantined" this way. Claim to lean on: one believer holds an inconsistent corpus. Use in outline: correct.

**Stalnaker, R. (1984). *Inquiry*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press (Bradford Books). Ch. 5, "The Problem of Deduction".** Reported (later fragmentation papers). An agent can be in several belief states at once, each operative in different contexts. Failures of deduction are failures to integrate them. The sentence usually quoted from p. 83 ("A person may be disposed, in one kind of context...") was not verified. Use in outline: correct.

**Cherniak, C. (1986). *Minimal Rationality*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press (Bradford Books). Ch. 3, on memory.** Reported (Stich's review, *Philosophy of Science* 56(1): 171–173). A finite agent cannot search all of long-term memory, so inconsistencies across its compartments go unnoticed. This "is not plausibly viewed as a quirk or a defect". Use in outline: correct.

**Egan, Andy (2008). Seeing and Believing: Perception, Belief Formation and the Divided Mind. *Philosophical Studies* 140(1): 47–63.** Confirmed (abstract). We have "distinct, compartmentalized systems of belief" that drive behaviour in different contexts. This is not merely excusable given limited resources: belief-forming mechanisms, perception included, can be built so as to "make it better to be fragmented than to be unified". Use in outline: correct, and stronger than "survives".

**Bendaña, J. and Mandelbaum, E. (2021). The Fragmentation of Belief. In C. Borgoni, D. Kindermann and A. Onofri (eds.), *The Fragmented Mind*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, pp. 78–107.** Confirmed (abstract). "One finds strong evidence against a unified web of belief and for a fragmented model of belief storage." Use in outline: correct; a claim about storage, which suits §4's records.

## B. Pretence, supposition, mindreading

**Leslie, A. M. (1987). Pretense and Representation: The Origins of "Theory of Mind". *Psychological Review* 94(4): 412–426.** Confirmed (primary). A pretend representation is a copy of a primary one with its reference, truth and existence relations suspended. Two additions to the primary code do this: decoupling marks (quotation marks) and an informational relation. "Pretend metarepresentations might thus have the general form: Agent–Informational Relation–'expression'" (p. 417). The inference rule: "if the input is decoupled, then so is the output... inference rules apply within the decoupling marks and do not remove them" (p. 419). The decoupler is the performance model. BELIEVE is another informational relation, so the same form serves false-belief attribution. Use in outline: correct. Leslie's index is a constituent of the vehicle; Nichols and Stich's is where the run is held.

**Baron-Cohen, S., Leslie, A. M. and Frith, U. (1985). Does the Autistic Child Have a "Theory of Mind"? *Cognition* 21(1): 37–46.** Reported (Leslie 1987's summary, read). In the Sally–Anne task 80% of autistic children failed. Use in outline: needs correcting. This paper tests false belief only. The pairing with absent pretend play is Leslie's (1987, pp. 423–424). Cite Leslie for "the two go together".

**Nichols, S. and Stich, S. (2000). A Cognitive Theory of Pretense. *Cognition* 74(2): 115–147.** Confirmed (primary: the authors' preprint, "revised and abbreviated" in print; the published abstract matches). **Nichols, S. and Stich, S. (2003). *Mindreading: An Integrated Account of Pretence, Self-Awareness, and Understanding Other Minds*. Oxford: Clarendon Press**, ch. 2 (pp. 16–59): Confirmed (abstract).
- One store for both: "the mind uses the PWB for a variety of tasks including conditional planning, reasoning about hypothetical situations and mind reading", and for pretence.
- Same code and inference mechanism as belief. Quarantine is the store's functional role.
- How a run reaches action: "the pretender comes to have beliefs of the form: If it were the case that p, then it would (or might) be the case that q1 & ... & qn". These beliefs, with a real desire, drive behaviour. This is the outline's "outputs are admitted under their index". The run carries no index.
- Against Leslie: his quarantine is "a notational variant" of theirs; the agent–attitude form is "unnecessary and unwarranted", and the autism data are "simply irrelevant" to it. The decoupling mechanism may still be shared with mindreading.

Use in outline: correct on all three points.

**Gordon, R. M. (1986). Folk Psychology as Simulation. *Mind & Language* 1(2): 158–171.** Reported (from memory). We predict behaviour by practical reasoning run on pretended inputs. **Heal, J. (1986). Replication and Functionalism. In J. Butterfield (ed.), *Language, Mind and Logic*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, pp. 135–150** (pagination not verified; reprinted in Heal, *Mind, Reason and Imagination*, 2003). **Heal, J. (1998). Co-cognition and Off-line Simulation. *Mind & Language* 13(4): 477–498.** Reported (Stich and Nichols's reply). Co-cognition is "thinking about the same subject matter" as the other. Use in outline: correct. Heal best fits "running one's own machinery on their premises".

**Goldman, A. I. (2006). *Simulating Minds: The Philosophy, Psychology, and Neuroscience of Mindreading*. New York: Oxford University Press.** Confirmed (chapter abstracts). "Simulation... invites the risk that one's own genuine states will contaminate the process", and egocentric errors are the evidence (ch. 7). Use in outline: correct, with two notes. Goldman's pretend states resemble genuine ones; that playing and having differ only in the index is the outline's inference. And his quarantine failure runs the other way: one's own states leak into the simulation. It is a fourth failure for the outline's list.

**Carruthers, P. (2011). *The Opacity of Mind: An Integrative Theory of Self-Knowledge*. Oxford: Oxford University Press.** Reported (Byrne's review, *Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews* 2012.05.11, quoting by page). Precursor confirmed (abstract): Carruthers (2009), How We Know Our Own Minds, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 32(2): 121–138: "our knowledge of our own attitudes results from turning our mindreading capacities upon ourselves". The theory: one mindreading faculty with sensory inputs (behaviour, circumstances, imagery, inner speech). Access to "non-sensory aspects" of one's mind is "no different in principle" from access to other minds (p. 1). Use in outline: needs qualifying. Carruthers keeps transparent access to "sensory states" and "current feelings of wanting or liking" (p. 135). "No direct access" holds for judgments, decisions, beliefs and intentions, not for experience or felt affect.

**Apperly, I. A. and Butterfill, S. A. (2009). Do Humans Have Two Systems to Track Beliefs and Belief-Like States? *Psychological Review* 116(4): 953–970.** Confirmed (abstract). "A cognitively efficient but inflexible capacity for tracking belief-like states" persists beside a later, flexible and demanding one. Use in outline: correct.

## C. Acceptance and index failures

**Bratman, M. E. (1992). Practical Reasoning and Acceptance in a Context. *Mind* 101(401): 1–16.** Reported (abstract via PhilArchive). Practical pressures can make it reasonable to accept a proposition in one context and not another; what is accepted need not be "limited to what one believes". Use in outline: correct.

**Cohen, L. J. (1992). *An Essay on Belief and Acceptance*. Oxford: Clarendon Press.** Reported (wording from memory). Belief is an involuntary disposition to feel that p; acceptance is a voluntary policy of taking p as a premise. Use in outline: correct.

**Johnson, M. K., Hashtroudi, S. and Lindsay, D. S. (1993). Source Monitoring. *Psychological Bulletin* 114(1): 3–28.** Confirmed (abstract). Source is attributed from "qualities of experience" by judgments "subject to error"; cases include cryptomnesia and "incorporation of fiction into fact". Use in outline: correct. Here a memory's index is reconstructed at retrieval, a third option beside the outline's two.

**Garry, M., Manning, C. G., Loftus, E. F. and Sherman, S. J. (1996). Imagination Inflation: Imagining a Childhood Event Inflates Confidence That It Occurred. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 3(2): 208–214.** Confirmed (abstract). Use in outline: correct.

## D. Extended mind

**Clark, A. and Chalmers, D. (1998). The Extended Mind. *Analysis* 58(1): 7–19.** Confirmed (primary: Chalmers's online text). Otto "suffers from Alzheimer's disease". Hearing of an exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art, he "consults the notebook, which says that the museum is on 53rd Street". The features (§5): "First, the notebook is a constant in Otto's life – in cases where the information in the notebook would be relevant, he will rarely take action without consulting it. Second, the information in the notebook is directly available without difficulty. Third, upon retrieving information from the notebook he automatically endorses it. Fourth, the information in the notebook has been consciously endorsed at some point in the past, and indeed is there as a consequence of this endorsement." The fourth is "arguable"; the first three "certainly play a crucial role". Their summary: "a high degree of trust, reliance, and accessibility".

Use in outline: needs correcting. Four features, not three; "constant availability" merges the first two. They also reject a neighbour of the outline's reading: explaining Otto's action by a "standing belief that the Museum is on the location written in the notebook" is "one step too many". The outline's index is a retrieval route, not a belief cited in the explanation, and the essay should say so.

**Objections that turn on retrieval.**
- **Rupert, R. D. (2004). Challenges to the Hypothesis of Extended Cognition. *Journal of Philosophy* 101(8): 389–428.** Confirmed (primary). Internal memory shows interference and the generation effect; "Notepads do not generate associations". And the first three features "can be satisfied far too easily": by directory assistance, "so long as he remembers how to dial up the operator". Only past endorsement blocks this.
- **Adams, F. and Aizawa, K. (2001). The Bounds of Cognition. *Philosophical Psychology* 14(1): 43–64.** Reported (from memory). Cognition needs non-derived content; coupling is not constitution.
- **Weiskopf, D. A. (2008). Patrolling the Mind's Boundaries. *Erkenntnis* 68(2): 265–276.** Confirmed (abstract). Beliefs are "informationally integrated with each other, and sensitive to changes in the person's overall system of beliefs"; notebook entries are not. This is the outline's *reach*, as a test for belief.
- **Michaelian, K. (2012). Is External Memory Memory? *Consciousness and Cognition* 21(3): 1154–1165.** Confirmed (abstract). Biological memory itself fails most of the four criteria.

**Has anyone argued that the notebook depends on an internal index?** Not in those words, as far as this search found. Nearest:
- **Arango-Muñoz, S. (2013). Scaffolded Memory and Metacognitive Feelings. *Review of Philosophy and Psychology* 4(1): 135–152.** Confirmed (abstract). The "selection problem": whether to solve a task "internally or externally"; metamemory decides.
- **Sparrow, B., Liu, J. and Wegner, D. M. (2011). Google Effects on Memory. *Science* 333(6043): 776–778.** Confirmed (abstract). Expecting future access, people show "lower rates of recall of the information itself and enhanced recall instead for where to access it".

Use in outline: "No index, no extension" appears to be the author's own. Weiskopf is a source for reach.

## E. Memory science

**Teyler, T. J. and DiScenna, P. (1986). The Hippocampal Memory Indexing Theory. *Behavioral Neuroscience* 100(2): 147–154.** Confirmed (abstract). The hippocampus forms "an index of neocortical areas activated by experiential events". **Teyler, T. J. and Rudy, J. W. (2007). The Hippocampal Indexing Theory and Episodic Memory: Updating the Index. *Hippocampus* 17(12): 1158–1169.** Confirmed (abstract). "A partial cue that activated the index could activate the neocortical patterns." Use in outline: correct, with a limit. The theory concerns episodic memory, and cortex, unlike the notebook, has associative retrieval of its own.

**Collins, A. M. and Loftus, E. F. (1975). A Spreading-Activation Theory of Semantic Processing. *Psychological Review* 82(6): 407–428.** Reported (index abstracts). Activation spreads from concept nodes through a semantic network, which explains priming. Use in outline: correct.

**Nader, K., Schafe, G. E. and LeDoux, J. E. (2000). Fear Memories Require Protein Synthesis in the Amygdala for Reconsolidation after Retrieval. *Nature* 406: 722–726.** Confirmed (abstract). Consolidated fear memories in rats, "when reactivated, return to a labile state": anisomycin then produces amnesia. Use in outline: correct for lability. For "rewrites" add **Hupbach, A., Gomez, R., Hardt, O. and Nadel, L. (2007). *Learning & Memory* 14(1–2): 47–53** (confirmed, abstract): after a reminder, new items intrude into the old memory.

**Roediger, H. L. and Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention. *Psychological Science* 17(3): 249–255.** Confirmed (abstract). Restudy wins at 5 minutes, testing at 2 days and 1 week. Use in outline: correct.

**Anderson, M. C., Bjork, R. A. and Bjork, E. L. (1994). Remembering Can Cause Forgetting: Retrieval Dynamics in Long-Term Memory. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 20(5): 1063–1087.** Confirmed (abstract). Retrieval practice impairs later recall of a category's unpractised members. Use in outline: correct; the best of the three for "the keying itself answers to fit".

**Chaining.** **Lashley, K. S. (1951). The Problem of Serial Order in Behavior. In L. A. Jeffress (ed.), *Cerebral Mechanisms in Behavior*. New York: Wiley, pp. 112–136.** Reported. **Rosenbaum, D. A., Cohen, R. G., Jax, S. A., Weiss, D. J. and van der Wel, R. (2007). The Problem of Serial Order in Behavior: Lashley's Legacy. *Human Movement Science* 26(4): 525–554.** Confirmed (abstract): Lashley "rejected reflex chaining accounts" for hierarchical plans. At the neural level: **Long, M. A., Jin, D. Z. and Fee, M. S. (2010). Support for a Synaptic Chain Model of Neuronal Sequence Generation. *Nature* 468: 394–399.** Confirmed (abstract): zebra-finch HVC recordings are "consistent with a synaptically connected chain of neurons". Use in outline: needs correcting. "Each step calls up the next" is the account Lashley refuted for behaviour. Cite Long et al. for chains of neural states.

## F. Amnesia

**Corkin, S. (2002). What's New with the Amnesic Patient H.M.? *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 3(2): 153–160.** **Corkin, S. (2013). *Permanent Present Tense*. New York: Basic Books** (book data not checked). Reported (from memory). H.M. formed almost no new episodic memories after 1953, with intelligence and conversation intact. Complications: **Corkin, S. (1968). Acquisition of Motor Skill after Bilateral Medial Temporal-Lobe Excision. *Neuropsychologia* 6(3): 255–265**; and **O'Kane, G., Kensinger, E. A. and Corkin, S. (2004). *Hippocampus* 14(4): 417–425** (confirmed, abstract): H.M. recalled the last names of 12 of 35 people who became famous after his operation.

**Wilson, B. A., Baddeley, A. D. and Kapur, N. (1995). Dense Amnesia in a Professional Musician Following Herpes Simplex Virus Encephalitis. *Journal of Clinical and Experimental Neuropsychology* 17(5): 668–681.** Confirmed (abstract). "He believes he has just woken up", which "appears to be the result of a delusion rather than the consequence of his amnesia". **Wilson, B. A. and Wearing, D. (1995). Prisoner of Consciousness: A State of Just Awakening Following Herpes Simplex Encephalitis. In R. Campbell and M. A. Conway (eds.), *Broken Memories*. Oxford: Blackwell, pp. 15–30** (some listings: 14–30). **Sacks, O. (2007). The Abyss: Music and Amnesia. *The New Yorker*, 24 September 2007.** Confirmed (primary). Diary entries such as "this time finally awake". "He has gained other implicit memories, too, slowly picking up new knowledge, like the layout of his residence."

Use in outline: the cases support "lives each one". Two corrections. Say "forms no new episodic memories", since skills, habits and some semantic knowledge were acquired. And Wearing's reports of first awakening are not plain testimony.

## G. Foundations in §1
