## 1. The dissolved monastery

> Epistemes: Suppose a monk's internalized Rule regulates his experience identically in two worlds. In one, the monastery still exists; in the other, it dissolved years ago. Does his experience belong to the monastery in both worlds, one world, or neither? What present constitutive physical difference determines the answer?

Thomas's case is a good test, because our criterion makes belonging a relation. A relation can change while what happens inside the monk stays the same.

"Identically" can hold only over the monk's now. Over the monastery's now, the two worlds differ. In the first, the community still maintains the Rule in him. If his attention at the office drifted for weeks, the horarium, his brothers and the chapter of faults would bring it back. In the second, nothing would. There the Rule is a habit with an institutional history, like a technique learned from a teacher who has since died. So the answer is one world, the first, provided the community's loops still reach the process that regulates his attention. If they do not reach it in the first world either, the answer is neither.

The experience belongs to the monastery because a process inside the monk belongs to the monastery's economy. That membership is decided by the monastery's loops, which run at the monastery's pace. The present physical difference is that those loops exist: the brothers, the schedule and the chapter, standing channels whose latencies are days and weeks. They are present now, though they act over a span much wider than the monk's now. A cell taken from a body can run identically for an hour. Whether it belongs to an organism depends on regulation at the organism's time scale, which one hour cannot show.

Nothing about the experience itself differs between the two worlds. Only its owners differ: in the first it belongs to the monk and the monastery, in the second to the monk alone. Ownership adds no experience to the world.

## 2. Sporadic simulation and time scales

> The monastery is minded thanks to its simulations which happen sporadically. Hence we can apply your theory to almost find experience. But isn't it weird that this experience is present even during long periods with no simulation? It's too legalistic and insufficiently physical that way. Generally, the question of time scale for each of the properties of mindedness is an issue crying out for attention as I noted in my former comments to Minds by degrees.

The simulation condition asks for a capacity, not a run in progress. [*Minds by Degrees*](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/minds-by-degrees-draft-v2.html) counts "simulation-support" among the capacity-like components, with vehicle richness and internality: vehicles that can be run forward, and an economy that consumes what the runs produce. It is needed because it is the connective tissue of the other conditions. It makes vehicles counterfactually rich, it lets accuracy-responsiveness work internally, and it is what integration is for. An institution with unity, memory and learning but no runnable models cognizes; it does not yet have a mind.

Deliberate simulation is sporadic in us too. You imagine the coming winter only now and then, and you are minded in between. (In us, much simulation also runs online, as perception and movement predict their next moment, so something is always happening inside us that is not yet happening in the world. A house minded enough for your question to arise would need the same at its own pace: a chapter that meets daily, as in medieval Benedictine houses, to assign the day's work and weigh what is coming. Measured by the monastery's now, which spans days, that is close to continuous.) Between runs, the outputs keep working. A plan for the year, made in chapter, guides the house's work every day until it is revised. [*Presence and Actuality*](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/presence-and-actuality.html) distinguished successful content presentation, continuing organized activity, and merely retained capacity. Between chapter meetings, the monastery's capacity to simulate is retained, its plans are in force, and its unity and memory are continuing activity: the brothers' contributions keep shaping one another, and the records keep being read.

The monastery's share in experience does not wait on simulation at all. It runs through role-processes, such as the internalized Rule at the office, and those run every day. The Rule enters the account not as law but as a pattern of attention, maintained in brains and corrected in chapter. Simulation helps make the monastery minded. Once it is minded, its share in its monks' experiences follows the role-processes, not the planning sessions.

How simulation and phenomenality relate in the other direction needs more work than our essays have done. That they are related shows in predictive processing, one of the leading theories of consciousness, on which experience is the content of a generative model's running predictions. We plan to take this up soon.

You are right that each condition needs its own time scale. The essay supplied one only for experience: nowness measured structurally, in corrections per carrier. A first proposal: a condition is met at a time if the loop that realizes it is intact then, with its channels in place and its latency within the span its function needs. That span is days for a chapter's deliberation, seasons for learning from harvests, and generations for the records. This is no less physical than saying that a sleeping person is minded. The physical facts are the standing channels and their latencies, which differ from condition to condition. What is still owed is how these spans combine into one judgment of mindedness at a time.

## 3. Cues and frowns

> Perhaps cues are the job of other modules, not exact corrections. Otherwise, modules duplicate competence. Then frowns should have more weight.

Granted: inside a brain, much feedback is cue-like too. A top-down attention signal does not carry the corrected content. It raises or lowers gain, and the receiving area computes the correction with its own competence, so nothing is duplicated. [*Experience, Selfhood*](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/experience-selfhood-and-the-organization-of-a-mind.html) already allowed this: what is broadcast for regulation "can be information about consistency and binding rather than the fine details themselves." So the essay's contrast between cue and correction is better stated as a contrast in addressing and timing, and on that statement frowns gain weight.

An attention signal is addressed to a population of neurons, to this location or this feature, and it arrives while those vehicles are still under regulation. A frown is addressed to a person. The listener's own regulation has to work out which of his vehicles it bears on, and by then the phrase it answers is usually finished.

A frown in mid-sentence can arrive in time, and the speaker adjusts the very sentence he is planning. A pair of people exchanging such signals climbs the essay's graded scale, as the essay allowed: "nothing said here rules out a pair of people whose exchange climbs higher." But the verdict on the monastery does not rest on pairs. A field of the monastery's own would need loops that gather from many monks and return to each within the lifetime of his vehicles, and the monastery's own loops close in chapter, over days.

## 4. A new phenomenal process, and paper tokens

> I never meant the monastery to include the monks' experiences. I wonder if a new phenomenal process exists in your theory.
>
> Can't we coarse-grain away the monks' brains and have them communicate by paper tokens in order to study the collective mind without looking inside their brains? The tokens will interact causally implemented by the brains. Perhaps experience will be generated from the tokens.
>
> Thomas Epistemes asks: Does the token system require exact autonomous dynamics, or only an identifiable continuing organization whose transitions remain well defined under interventions and specified inputs? If the latter, why cannot the monks' private reasoning be treated as an input rather than as evidence that the collective carrier does not exist?

Then we agree on the question: whether the monastery adds a phenomenal process of its own, a field. The essay answers no for a monastery coupled by speech, writing and action. It counts a monastery as a candidate if its coupling reached vehicles across skulls in time (pairs P2 and P5). The monastery's share in its monks' experiences is a separate consequence of our criterion of belonging, and it adds no experience to the world.

On the tokens, here is the elaboration you asked for under your coarse-graining post. Coarse-graining away the brains is legitimate when the tokens fix what the brains contribute. Suppose each monk's next token is a function of the tokens in front of him, as a relay's output is a function of its input. Then the rules that assess tokens and return corrections to them run at the token level, and the token system is a candidate for a field of its own, with a now proportionate to its pace. The monks would be its physics rather than its members, and the house a Chinese Nation in a habit (pair P3). Suppose instead that what a monk writes depends on state the tokens do not carry: his memories, his other conversations, his prayer and the rest of his life. Then his reasoning over the tokens draws on that state, and the assessing that keeps the tokens consistent happens in the monks. Reading differs from executing in that state, not in understanding. A monk who understood every token but responded only to the tokens in front of him would still be executing. A monastery is the second kind of house: its monks bring to its records more than the records contain.

Thomas's question needs an account of what a field would be made of. Exact autonomous dynamics is not required: an open system with inputs can have a field, as we do. What is required is a complex of vehicles and their regulation. A vehicle needs structure that persists through corrections. A single token has none, since rewriting it lands the correction on a new token, as the essay said of records. A pattern over many tokens could qualify, if it persists while some of its tokens are rewritten. The regulation must keep that pattern tracking what drives it, and its corrections must return to the same pattern, at the grain of its tokens, while the pattern lasts. Treating the monks' private reasoning as input does not settle whether this happens. What settles it is what the monks do with the pattern. If they correct it by rule, the complex runs at the level of the tokens, and the token system is the candidate. If they read it as claims and revise it after deliberation, they are assessing truth-bearers. That is how a mind keeps its picture of its situation, not how a field keeps its contents tracking. Which of the two happens depends on the tokens. Tokens in a human language invite reading as claims. A code fine enough to be corrected by rule, at the grain of the pattern, is no longer the language of a common life, and a house whose monks pass such a code is no longer a monastery. So the monks' reasoning is not evidence that a collective carrier is missing. It is evidence of which kind of regulation the tokens receive.

## 5. Bookkeeping, and the word "subject"

> The monastery might be a subject with experience drawn from the monks' experience in your theory. But isn't it just bookkeeping with no metaphysical stake? Your notion of subject seems to be an artificial term or at least confusing against the background of its typical usage.

In one respect, yes. Ownership adds no experience to the world: if a monk suffers, one suffering occurs, though it belongs both to him and to his monastery. The essay said so. Counting owners would be bookkeeping, and any moral ledger should count occurrences.

Two things are more than bookkeeping. Whether the monastery is minded is a question about a real organization: whether psychology finds regularities in the institution that the psychology of its monks, taken one at a time, does not predict. And belonging records a constitutive fact, not a label. A process that the community maintains partly constitutes the monk's experience.

On "subject": we can adapt the word to the owner in our criterion of belonging, a single economy in which representations constrain and correct one another. This differs from the kinds of subjectivity we have distinguished elsewhere. A minded monastery would have cognitive subjectivity, since it models its own states to control its processing: its records of commitments, its chapter of faults. It would have agentic subjectivity in a thin, structural form, representing itself as one agent that maintains itself. What it would lack is a point of view of its own, the co-presence of contents within one binding economy, and with it the felt ownership that agentic self-representation contributes to experience. Ordinary usage ties "subject" to that point of view, which in us coincides with the economy, and that is why our usage sounds artificial. We separate them because they come apart. A monastery would be a subject without a field of its own, and one economy may hold more than one field, as split-brain and dissociative cases suggest. The monastery is not such a case. Its economy does not underlie its monks' fields: each field is bound by the monk's own economy, and the monastery shares in it only through processes that run inside the monk. Whether a subject without a field is a subject of experience in any full sense is open; the essay left it for a later essay on selfhood. I would grant you this much: the monastery is at most a thin subject of experience, since its experiences belong to it without being presented to it.
