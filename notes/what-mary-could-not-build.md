A woman has seen red. She is now back in a black-and-white room, with a complete physical description of a creature from another planet and one question to answer. When the creature looks at a ripe tomato, is the state it is in the one she knows as red?

Torin Alter thinks she cannot work it out. He calls this a strong intuition, and he has put the knowledge argument on it. I think she can work it out, given one thing, and this essay is about that thing.

The case is a repair of a more famous one. Frank Jackson's Mary has learned all the physics of color vision from black-and-white lessons and has never seen a color. When she leaves her room and sees a tomato she learns something, the argument goes, so the physical truths were not all the truths. The standard reply is that Mary lacked a concept, and nobody can deduce a truth they lack the concept to state. Alter accepts the reply. One cannot expect Mary to derive the concept of phenomenal redness from structural concepts, he said in [a conversation with Richard Brown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bT74sMNfA8E), and "nothing interesting follows from that other than something about concept derivation" ([0:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bT74sMNfA8E&t=1400)). So he gives her the concept and asks about someone else's experience. David Chalmers makes the same move: "even when Mary has the relevant phenomenal concepts, she cannot always deduce the relevant phenomenal truths (about other people, for example)" (["The Two-Dimensional Argument Against Materialism"](https://consc.net/papers/2dargument.html)).

Alter's book, [*The Matter of Consciousness*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198840459.001.0001) (2023), is the most careful defence of the argument there is. Its conclusion is that physicalism is false, or else that the world has natures which its structure leaves out. What the argument refutes, he told Brown, is "a narrow kind of physicalism, a physicalism which says that the entire world including consciousness is fully structural" ([0:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bT74sMNfA8E&t=2510)). The book has a name for such views. A theory is structuralist when on it "the complete structural truth … metaphysically necessitates all phenomenal truths" (chapter 15). That is close enough to the view I defended in [*What a Description Answers To*](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/what-a-description-answers-to.html) that his argument is aimed at me. It deserves an answer that does not dodge.

The answer is unusual in how much it grants. I accept the principle that carries his argument from what Mary cannot deduce to what is not necessary. I accept that Mary learns something. I dispute one premise, about what a concept of an experience is a concept of. A case that Alter himself states and sets aside is the test of it.

## The argument

Alter gives the argument three steps (section 1.4 of the book).

The first step is an epistemic gap. Mary learns the complete physical truth from her lessons. She can deduce whatever follows from it. And on leaving the room she learns new truths about what seeing in color is like. So some truths about experience cannot be deduced from the complete physical truth.

His explanation of the first step is what makes the book distinctive. What Mary learns in the room is *structural*: "expressible by statements containing only logical, mathematical, nomic, and perhaps spatiotemporal vocabulary" (2.2). Such information is objective because it is structural. "Sufficiently intelligent Martians could understand such information." What she learns on release "cannot be deduced from structural truths but can be learned by having relevant experiences" (2.1). He takes from David Lewis a point that supports this. Lessons about non-physical things would not help Mary either, "if those further lessons are also conveyed in black and white." So the contrast that matters is between structure and something else, and not between the physical and the non-physical.

The second step is a modal gap. If the phenomenal truths cannot be deduced from the physical ones, they are not necessitated by them either. Alter calls a claim of this kind a bridge principle, since it leads from what can be known to what must be so. His ground for it is that there are no primitive necessities. Two terms are needed to say what those are. A truth is known a priori when reasoning alone is enough to know it, and a posteriori when it takes investigation of how the world is. A *primitive necessity* is "an a posteriori necessity that is not necessary in virtue of following a priori from a priori truths and non-modal truths about the actual world" (7.2). His model of an honest necessity is Euclid's: being a triangle necessitates the angle sum, and the one can be deduced from the other.

The third step is an ontological gap. If the physical truth does not necessitate the phenomenal truth, physicalism is false. The alternative he favours is Russellian monism, on which physics describes structure and something with an intrinsic nature underlies it.

He claims no more for the argument than the balance of evidence. "When I describe the knowledge argument as sound, I do not mean to imply that one cannot reasonably doubt its soundness" (6.4.2). I argue to the same standard.

## What I grant

*Beneath structure there is no nature.* The previous essay did say that structure is not all there is. What a description answers to is production, one occurrence coming from another, and production is not structure. But it is not a nature either. An occurrence is nothing apart from what it came from, so it has no character of its own that a lesson could leave out. I cannot escape Alter by finding something at the bottom that Mary was not taught.

*Mary learns something.* [*The Grain of the Hard Problem*](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/the-grain-of-the-hard-problem.html) said so. An ideal reasoner with the complete physics "can derive the organization at the grain of experience. Chalmers will say that it still cannot derive what that organization is like. We agree."

*Some truths are not structural.* They are the truths stated with concepts that are applied by recognition. [*What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right*](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/what-phenomenal-intuition-gets-right.html) put it this way: "structure cannot deliver *like this*, because *like this* is available only to a system that has undergone the occurrence." Every truth about what occurs is reducible, and every reduction is structural. That is not the same as every truth being stated in structural terms.

*There are no primitive necessities.* The previous essay listed brute necessities among the six things its view rules out.

*Introspection does not reveal how an experience is constituted.* Alter says introspection is not "equipped with a constitution detector" (8.2.3). The earlier essay said that "the constitutive complexity is not presented in recognizing an experiential character."

*Having an experience is not the only way to know what it is like.* Alter rejects that doctrine, with Hume's subject who can supply a missing shade of blue (4.1). *Grain* used the same case.

One consequence has to be faced at once. *What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right* replied to Chalmers that his objection "assumes that phenomenal concepts reveal the full nature of their referents." Alter disowns that assumption, and argues at length that his principle stands without it (8.2). So the published reply does not reach him. A better one is needed.

## What a necessity needs

Alter does not deny that some necessities are known only by investigation. Water is necessarily H2O, and nobody knew it before chemistry. His point is that such necessities are not primitive. "If the underlying explanatory structure of water is H2O, then necessarily water is H2O" is known a priori, as part of having the concept of water. The antecedent is a plain fact about the actual world. The necessity follows from the two. He adopts Chalmers's general statement: "For every a posteriori metaphysical necessity 'Necessarily S', there is a nonmodal truth D such that 'If D, then necessarily S' is a priori" (7.2.3).

Jackson draws the consequence for deduction, and Alter follows him (1.4.2). From the fact that a mug holds water one cannot deduce that it holds hydrogen. Add that water is the watery stuff around here, and that the watery stuff is H2O, and one can. Kripke's cases show necessity without deducibility only when the description is partial. Mary's description is complete.

I accept all of this. It tells me what I owe. If the identity of an experience with an organization is to be a necessity of the honest kind, there must be a conditional known a priori and a non-modal fact that satisfies it. *Grain* claimed that there is: the necessity "is not brute. It follows from an identity together with a discovered ordering of grains."

Spelled out, the conditional has the form of the one about water. If the occurrences that my recognition of red responds to are of a certain kind, then necessarily an experience of red is an occurrence of that kind. The concept says no more. It does not say what the kind is, and it does not say at what grain the occurrences must be described for their kind to show. When I think *this* of a shade, *Grain* said, "nothing in the thought says whether *this* is a pattern at the grain of neural populations, of molecules, or of something coarser than either."

So the fact that satisfies the conditional has two parts, and recognition supplies only one. Recognition sorts occurrences into those it responds to and the rest. The grain comes from the system that has the experiences. On the account of the earlier essays, an experience is a state that does some representing, which they call a vehicle, in the organization that its own regulation maintains. The grain of an experience "is the grain that the vehicles' own regulation respects: the distinctions that its corrections preserve." At that grain, the occurrences my recognition responds to fall in a certain region of organizations. Experiences of red are the occurrences in that region.

Whether the conditional is known a priori depends on how the concept is used, and our use pulls two ways. We defer to what was there over what we recognized, as we do with water. We also take what we recognize to be simple and wholly shown.

Follow the second, and the concept is of a nature that is fully apparent to me when I see red. Then the concept reaches that nature directly, and what my recognition responds to is a further matter. The occurrences might be the nature. They might only accompany it, joined to it by a law. Nothing in the concept says which, so the conditional cannot be known a priori, and if they only accompany it the conditional is false. Follow the first, and the concept is of those occurrences, as the concept of water is of the stuff in the glass. Then the conditional holds in the way the one about water does. I take the first to be the concept and the second to be a mistake about it. The next two sections argue for that.

The conditional holds on the same terms as the one about water, too. "Water" names a kind only if the stuff in the glass is of one kind, and is roughly what we took it to be. The concept of red names the kind of those occurrences to the degree that they form one, and are as recognition presents them. Section five argues that they are, that one mistake aside.

> **Two intensions.** Jackson and Chalmers have a vocabulary for this. A word has two intensions. The first is what the word picks out given how the actual world turns out to be. For "water" that is the watery stuff: H2O here, and something else if the world is Twin Earth. The second is what the word picks out in worlds considered as counterfactual, given how the actual world is. For "water" that is H2O in every one. Jackson calls them the A-intension and the C-intension, and Chalmers the primary and the secondary. A sentence is necessary when it is true by the second in every world. Chalmers holds that a sentence is a priori just when it is true by the first however the world turns out, and Alter adopts this as his bridge principle in its exact form (7.3.2). For "water" the two intensions differ, and that is how a necessity can wait on chemistry. Chalmers holds that for a concept of an experience they coincide, and Alter requires it (7.3.3). On the reading I defend they differ, as they do for "water".

So the dispute is not about the bridge from deduction to necessity. Alter and I both accept it. The dispute is about one concept.

## Fool's orange

The second way of using the concept has a name and a defender. Philip Goff defends it as Revelation: in having an experience, one has its nature apparent to one. A concept of an experience is then transparent, in his word. It "reveals the nature" of what it refers to. Goff argues for Revelation from how confident we are in judgments about our own experiences, for instance that two of them are alike. He argues from it to a form of Russellian monism ([*Consciousness and Fundamental Reality*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780190677015.001.0001), 2017). If the nature of red is apparent to me and no organization is, organization is no part of that nature.

Alter does not rest his argument on Revelation, and his own picture of an experience comes from dividing it in two (8.2.2). On the strong principle, the complete nature of a type of experience is apparent to the one who has it, including "whether and how that type is (actually) constituted." He rejects that. On the weak one, the complete nature is apparent, and that nature "does not concern whether and how that type is (actually) constituted." He says the weak one would suffice to explain our confidence, and that it is compatible with experiences being structurally constituted. On the picture that results, an experience has two layers: a phenomenal nature, which is apparent, and a constitution, which is not.

He then considers an argument that Goff might give for the strong principle, and it contains the case I want. If experiences were structurally constituted, there could be an experience that "feels exactly the same" as an orange one and is not orange, because it is differently constituted. Call it pseudo-orange. Then nobody could be confident that their own experience is orange and not pseudo-orange. Since we can be confident, experiences are not structurally constituted, or else their constitution is revealed.

Alter's reply is that the physicalist need not allow pseudo-orange at all. Standard physicalism "is perfectly compatible with counting any O experience—including an O experience that is not structured as yours is—as a phenomenally orange experience" (8.2.3). Whatever feels that way is orange, however it is made.

That reply treats "orange" as unlike "water." Water is not whatever looks and tastes like water. The stuff on Putnam's Twin Earth looks and tastes like water and is not water, because it is not H2O. Alter told Brown that he is "disinclined to think" that terms like "feeling pain" and "seeing red" are natural-kind terms of the Putnam–Kripke sort ([1:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bT74sMNfA8E&t=4070)). I think they are, with one qualification, and I take the horn he sets aside. There could be pseudo-orange. I will call it fool's orange.

The phrase "feels exactly the same" has two readings, and the earlier essays keep them apart. Two experiences can be the same to recognition: the subject cannot tell them apart. Or they can be the same in what is present. The first does not give the second. "Two occurrences that fall under the same type, and so share a matching profile, need not share exact character," *Grain* said. Exact character "is not known in the sense of being represented. It is present."

With that distinction, two experiences can differ in how they are constituted in three ways.

They can differ below the grain of experience, as neurons differ from silicon. That makes no difference to the experience. "Their material may vary, but the constitutive dynamics must actually be present." Alter's reply fits this case, and here he is right.

They can differ at the grain of experience and below the grain of recognition, in one kind of mind. The subject cannot tell them apart, and they differ in exact character. Both are orange.

Or only the cognitive role is the same. A system makes the same judgments and the same discriminations, and its organization at the grain of experience is different, or is absent. *Grain* argued that this can happen: "A functional description, specified at the grain of cognition, really does underdetermine experience." A duplicate of our discriminative organization that keeps some regulated carrier "would plausibly have experiences of the same types, differing in exact character. One that dissolves the carrier, as some unfoldings may, would be a candidate for having none."

The third case is fool's orange. Recognition responds to it as it does to orange, and the organization is missing, so it is not orange. The second case is to orange as heavy water is to water.

The system in the third case has my concept, so far as a concept is a matter of cognition. Its recognition fires on the same occasions, and the same conditional holds for it. What its recognition responds to is of another kind, so its word "orange" names that kind, as "water" on Twin Earth names the stuff there. If nothing at the grain of an experience is there at all, its word names no experience.

What "orange" names, then, is a region: the organizations that this recognition tracks. This is the overflow of experience beyond cognition, seen from the side of the concept. Recognition is coarser than what it responds to, so one recognition answers to many occurrences.

Here the analogy with water stops, and the essay should say so. H2O draws water's boundary with no help from how water looks. Organizations vary continuously, and the boundary of orange is drawn by recognition's own lumping. The kind is natural in what it is a region of. Where the region ends is relative to the recognizer.

Why read the concept this way and not Alter's? Because of how we treat it when recognition and what is there come apart. Take a series of shades of blue in which each is indiscriminable from the next, even when the two are seen side by side, and the two ends are plainly different. If experiences we cannot tell apart were the same in character, each would be the same as the next, and so the first the same as the last. We do not say that. We say that somewhere two neighbours differed and we could not tell. We defer to what was there over what recognition delivered, and that is how one treats a term for a kind.

Goff's worry remains: how can I be confident that my experience is orange? In the way I am confident that the glass holds water. Recognition is reliable in the conditions it was built for. Judgments at its grain, about kind and similarity, are mostly right. Exact character is present without being known.

Alter has an argument that phenomenal concepts are not blind pointers (8.2.1). Mary's belief that phenomenal redness is *this quality* is informative, so the concept is not a bare demonstrative. I agree. The concept gives access to an experience through its actual character, and gets its presence and its distinctness from other experiences right. It is not opaque. It is not transparent either, since it does not present the grain or the complexity of what it picks out. Alter grants that a "semantic explanation for the epistemic gap … would undermine the inference to the modal gap." He considers one such explanation, radical opacity, and rejects it. The concept I have described is of a middle kind, and it gives such an explanation.

## The apparent nature is not the complete nature

The last section set two pictures side by side. This one argues for mine.

Take the series of shades again. The experiences at the ends differ in character. So somewhere along the series, experiences that the subject cannot tell apart differ in character. Part of the character of each is therefore not apparent to the one who has it. The weak principle of Revelation says that the complete phenomenal nature is apparent. It is false, and for a reason that has nothing to do with constitution.

Alter grants half of this. He follows Michael Tye in holding that Mary, looking at a rose, knows what it is like to see a very determinate shade of red even if she is not "able to recognize things other than the rose as having that very determinate color" (5.1.2.1). So for him too character is finer than recognition. He counts the fine character as known. But nothing she could judge of the shade would differ if it were its neighbour in the series. The fine character is present, and it is not apparent.

Alter's own example goes the same way, as he describes it. "It can be apparent to the wine novice that the taste experiences of two wines are similar, while the complexities of the tastes are not apparent to them" (8.2.3). He offers it to show that confidence about similarity does not reveal a complete nature. On his description it shows more. The complexities belong to the novice's tastes, and the novice misses them. They are character, and not something beneath character. The line between what is apparent and what is hidden does not run between a phenomenal nature and its constitution. It runs through the nature.

The example cannot carry the argument. A critic will say that training changes the taste: the expert's experience has the complexities, and the novice's never had them. Nothing either of them reports decides between that and Alter's description. The argument rests on the series, where nobody is trained and nothing changes but the shade.

Once that is granted, there is no layer left for a fully apparent nature to occupy. There is the occurrence, and recognition presents it coarsely.

A second piece of evidence is one Alter reports (4.3). Paul Churchland predicted, from a model of how opponent cells in the visual system fatigue, that people would see afterimages in colors that no surface has, among them an orange more saturated than any orange object. People then saw them. Alter's interest is in the surprise that remains: that someone who knows the theory "will be surprised at all when she sees that color indicates that she was not able to do the relevant deduction." My interest is in the prediction. The structure of the vehicles told us of a character before anyone had met it.

Alter anticipates a reply of this general kind and has an answer (6.4). Derk Pereboom suggested that introspection may misrepresent experience, as perception misrepresents color on Locke's view. Alter says that the physicalist needs more than the possibility. The inaccuracy must be actual, and it must be radical, since "if any aspect of the nature of phenomenal redness is as introspection represents that nature as being, then knowledge argument proponents could argue that upon release Mary gains information about that aspect."

My claim is not Pereboom's, and it meets the demand differently. Introspection is largely right. It is right that an experience is present, that it has a character, and that it differs from and resembles other experiences. It makes one mistake: "because the constitutive complexity is not presented in recognizing an experiential character, the character is taken to have no constitutive complexity." The aspects it gets right are structural, and Mary could have derived them. "A complete description of the vehicles entails that an experience of red is more like an experience of orange than one of green." The one aspect that would not be structural is simplicity: a nature with no composition, exhausted by my access to it. That is the aspect introspection gets wrong. A single inaccuracy, placed exactly where the non-structural information would have to be, does the work that Alter says needs a radical one.

Simplicity has to be stated with care. It is not the absence of relations. A defender of simple natures grants that red resembles orange more than green, and holds that the resemblance follows from what the two are. Nor is it the absence of presented parts, since orange looks reddish and yellowish to anyone. The claim is that a character such as pure red is not made up of anything, and is all there in how it is presented.

Alter asks for evidence that the inaccuracy is actual. Against the second half of the claim the evidence is the series of shades. Against the first it cannot be more resemblances. It is that the make-up of the vehicles explains the resemblances that simple natures must take as given, and the predicted colors show the explanation at work. There is also an explanation of why a system would make the mistake: a complex activity that is available to it as a unit will be taken for simple, because the unity of the access is read as the simplicity of what is accessed.

He also says why the idea is hard to accept. A Lockean about color keeps the feeling of color and moves it into the mind. The parallel view about the feeling itself has nowhere to move it. The earlier essay answered this. Vision presents surface colors as simple and homogeneous. The early modern relocation "transfers a feature of represented content to the representing occurrence." But "an experience of a homogeneous red surface need not be homogeneous or simple." Denying simplicity feels like denying the feeling because the feeling was first described by borrowing the look of a surface.

Others have argued that nothing in introspection or in theory shows experience to be non-structural, Daniel Stoljar among them (["Four Kinds of Russellian Monism"](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203116623-1), 2014). The nearest published relative of the positive claim is Kristjan Loorits's, that consciousness is "a complex neural pattern that misperceives some of its own highly complex structural properties as monadic and qualitative" (["Structural Qualia"](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00237), 2014). What I add is a grain at which the structure lives, and a mistake confined to one aspect.

## What she can deduce

Return to the woman in the room. Her reasoning has Jackson's form.

The kind I call red is the kind of the occurrences that this recognition of mine responds to. At the grain their regulation respects, those occurrences fall in a certain region of organizations. That is a physical truth about her own brain, and it is in her books. The alien's state lies in that region, or it does not. That is a physical truth about the alien, and it is in her books too. So the alien's state is red, or it is not.

The first two premises carry the account I have been defending. Without the first, what her recognition responds to might only accompany a nature, and the nature would be red. The second is in her books, but the books do not say that this is the grain at which to read the kind. Read at the grain of her neurons, the kind would shut the alien out whatever its organization. Read at the grain of cognition, it would let in anything that discriminates as she does. So I do not claim that her conclusion follows from the physics and her concept alone. It follows given that an experience is the organized occurrence. [*Can Two Minds Share One Experience?*](https://lukstafi.github.io/notes/can-two-minds-share-one-experience.html) put the identity "at the level of a concrete dynamical organization." Sameness of kind is then sameness of organization, and the physics settles that.

Alter's intuition that she cannot tell is strong for a reason. She cannot check her conclusion. She has never had the alien's state beside her own, and being human she never will. The deduction gives her a truth and no new recognition. What she feels is the absence of that recognition. His intuition, stated as a claim, is that the account is false, and the argument against that claim is the one in the last section.

Some cases come out as one would hope. If the alien's space of qualities has no counterpart of her red, the answer is no, and she deduces it. If the alien's organization lies near the edge of her region, the answer is as vague as the edge, which her own recognition draws. If the creature is a bat, she can deduce that its states are of no kind she has a concept for.

## What Mary lacked

Before her release Mary lacked the concept, and the question is why her lessons could not give it to her.

This is not the reply that Mary gains only an ability, or only an acquaintance. Alter argues against both (chapter 5). When Mary sees red she has a revelation, he says, and a gain that carried no information would not be one. I agree. With the concept she knows truths that she could not state before.

A concept applied by recognition gets its way of presenting its object from undergoing the object. *Grain* said why Mary cannot supply that for herself. "Our power to write into our own vehicles is coarse. Imagery passes through the same bottleneck as access, in the other direction. We can interpolate within quality spaces we already have, and cannot add a dimension to them. That is why Mary is stuck." Hume's subject can fill in a shade of blue between two shades he has seen. Mary has no dimension to interpolate along.

This puts me at odds with a premise Alter states outright. A textbook of the complete physical truth, he says, "could be written in a highly mathematical, objective scientific language, which could be understood by any sufficiently intelligent creature, regardless of their sensory capacities," and that is "exactly the sort of book pre-release Mary could fully understand" (1.4.1). I think Mary can state every truth in the book and derive every truth that follows from one. That is less than understanding all of it. To understand the description of an organization, at the level of the organization, is to be able to recognize an instance. Acquiring understanding means reconfiguring whatever does one's representing. For most organizations an instance can be built on paper or in imagination, and recognized there. For an experience the recognizing is done by the system that has it, so the instance has to be built in that system's own vehicles.

Mary's case is the far end of something ordinary. At every level of description above the lowest, some truths are stated with concepts that are applied by recognition and do not say what structure they pick out. We recognize a stone, or a glider in the Game of Life, without the concept telling us what it is made of. Nobody infers a gap in the world from that. The truths are reached by building an instance from the lower-level description and recognizing it under the upper level's types.

Physics is the hard case for this claim, since physics is the science accused of being purely structural. It passes, and Alter's own text shows two reasons.

The first is an old problem that he grants. A theory in which every term is defined by its role is satisfied by any collection of things of the right number, so a purely structural physics would say nothing about the world. To avoid this he keeps the nomic concepts primitive, and does not define them by role (2.3). The physics his argument needs is therefore not purely structural, by his own construction.

The second is how physics is applied. A track in a detector is recognized as an electron's. The concept *electron* does not say what lies beneath physics, as *red* does not say what organization it picks out. To meet an objection of Stoljar's, that spatial and temporal concepts are tied to experience, Alter has to replace the physical concepts with counterparts defined through Ramsey sentences (2.3). The physics Mary is taught is structural only after its concepts have been stripped of what recognition contributes.

Physics seems purely structural for a reason, and the reason is that its recognizers can be swapped. Any instrument and any observer will do. That is the truth in the remark about Martians. For *red* only one kind of recognizer will do, the system that has the experience. Objectivity is the degree to which the recognizer can be replaced, and Mary's case is at one end of the scale.

The claim has to be kept this specific. That a description of an experience does not reproduce the experience is, as the earlier essay said, "true but weak": "Reading a description of viscosity does not make the page viscous either." What explains Mary is an architecture "that can recognize a concrete difference without representing the complexity constituting it."

Alter calls his own explanation of what Mary learns abductive, so it can be met with a rival. Mary learns something on release because the concept she lacked can be had only by a system in which the organization occurs, and nothing in her could bring that about from a description. Lewis's point is predicted by this explanation at least as well as by his. Lessons about non-physical things would not help Mary, because a lesson in any vocabulary is a description.

Two things Alter says along the way show where the rival explanation bites. He stipulates that in the room Mary "does not imagine in color" (1.4.1). And in answering the objection that she lacks concepts, he says that if those concepts can be physically explained, her teachers could give her the explanation, and "that should enable her to construct the relevant concepts" (9.4.3). For a human the second is false, and so the first is not needed. For a reasoner who could do what the second describes, the first removes the ability by fiat.

## A reasoner who can build

*Grain* described such a reasoner. It "reads the physical description, derives the organization at the grain of experience, configures its own vehicles to match, and brings them into its regulation. Then it undergoes and recognizes the result." It needs a path from its reasoning into its experiential carrier, a carrier general enough to realize the target, and regulation that takes the result in. Nothing in principle rules these out for an engineered mind. For such a reasoner Mary's lessons would be enough.

The obvious objection is that this is cheating, as it would be if Mary were shown a photograph of a tomato. *Grain* gave a criterion. "A technology cheats when it causes the experience independently of what the reasoner knows." The photograph cheats. Anyone who looked would have the experience, and garbling Mary's physics would change nothing. The constructor's route runs through its grasp of the description, "so a garbled description would yield a different result."

The criterion needs a second clause. Daniel Dennett imagined a robot, Locked RoboMary, whose color system is disabled. As Alter quotes him, she "builds a model of herself," works out the state B that the model enters on seeing a tomato, and "puts herself into state B" (4.2.1). Her route runs through what she knows. But the fact that B is the red state reaches her as "the state a tomato causes." That is a label. She does not know why this configuration should be red. A genuine constructor knows why: what the organization is more like and less like, and what it opposes. A route cheats when the information about which experience is being built arrives other than structurally.

In the terms of the previous essay, a label is a lookup table. The cheating constructor stands to the genuine one as the dust man's table stands to an admissible map.

It follows that a genuine constructor is possible only if red is structural. If red were an atom of quality there would be no why, only labels, and every route would cheat. So "could a reasoner build red without cheating?" and "is red structural?" are one question.

Alter's verdict on the robot is that she "does not deduce phenomenal truths from physical truths. Instead, she puts herself in a dispositional state that constitutes possessing the relevant phenomenal knowledge. This is hardly better than if she had simply unlocked her color-vision system" (4.2.3). The strongest form of the objection is this. That a reasoner can produce the experience by building the organization shows only that the organization suffices here. A dualist with laws linking brain to experience predicts the same success. Deduction would show that the phenomenal truth follows from what the physical truth says.

I agree that production is not deduction, and I do not rest the necessity on it. The necessity is derived, as section three required and section six carried out. Construction does a different job. It is how a reasoner who lacks the concept gets it from the description.

It also does a second job, which matters more. The human who has seen red cannot check her conclusion about the alien. The constructor can. It builds the alien's organization, binds it beside its own red in one field, and recognizes. If it built two organizations alike and could tell them apart when bound, the account would be wrong. The account is open to an experiment, and that is more than can be said for an intrinsic nature that no description reaches.

Alter illustrates what he will not count with two geometers (4.2.4). One proves that the angles of a trapezoid sum to 360 degrees. The other uses a device that puts anyone who contemplates the axioms into the state of knowing the sum: "that process is plainly not deduction." The device fails *Grain*'s test, since a garbled axiom would make no difference. Amy Kind adds a third geometer, who needs a diagram (["What Counts as Cheating?"](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11406-024-00717-5), 2024). If Euclid's diagrams are indispensable his proofs are not pure deduction, and using one is no foul. The constructor belongs with her. *Grain* compared its method to what Kant called the construction of a concept in intuition.

Alter concedes more than his verdict suggests. "Perhaps Mary could in some way exploit her physical knowledge to acquire phenomenal knowledge of color experiences without having color experiences. Dennett's RoboMary case indicates that such a thing is possible" (4.5).

His own test for a gain of information points the same way. A traveller who has explored a perfect simulation of New York, he says, has no justified "Aha!" on arriving in the city, and learns nothing of its character by arriving (5.2.3). A constructor that had built red would have none on leaving the room. The simulation is a cheat by *Grain*'s test and the constructor is not, but the case shows what the revelation follows: whether the experience has occurred, by whatever route.

## What is taken on trust

The account generalizes across experiences and across experiencers only weakly, and the essay should say how weakly.

The weakness begins inside one subject. Since blue is structural and not an atom, experiences I cannot tell apart may differ. When I take today's blue to be yesterday's, I trust that they do not differ in some way I fail to notice across the two occasions. Across subjects the trust is larger. The woman in the room concludes that the alien's state is red as hers is because their organizations fall in one region. That the same organization is the same experience is the account. She takes it on trust, and for her it is a leap.

A constructor is better placed, in two ways. It can bind any two experiences it chooses beside each other, so whatever can be told apart side by side is told apart. And it knows the organization it built. It shares our limit on what can be known by acquaintance, since for any mind that knowledge is bounded by recognition. But it knows, from the account, whether two shades that look the same are the same experience. What it still takes on trust concerns differences that no binding reveals, which make no difference it could recognize. Its leap is across an insignificant gap. Ours can be across a large one.

One caveat. *Grain* noted that "each comparison supplies a new context, and the context is part of what fixes exact character." Binding two blues makes a new context. The constructor built the joint organization, though, so it knows what the binding preserved in each part.

Alter says that his argument can reasonably be doubted. Mine can be doubted here, where it takes on trust that the same organization is the same experience. That trust is of a kind a constructor could test. Alter's alternative asks for trust in a nature that no description reaches, and nothing could test that.

## By reasoning alone

Alter's argument turns on what can be known a priori. His bridge principle is stated in those terms, and so is the usual way of sorting physicalists. So it matters what reasoning alone includes. It has always included more than deduction from sentences.

Three cases show it. Kind's third geometer proves her theorem with a diagram, and what she knows is counted a priori. Timothy Williamson's example is "All crimson things are red" (["How Deep is the Distinction between A Priori and A Posteriori Knowledge?"](https://philosophy.web.ox.ac.uk/sitefiles/aprioriaposteriori.pdf), 2013). One comes to know it by imagining crimson and applying the concept of red to what one imagines. To imagine crimson is to have, faintly, an experience of crimson. Tradition says that the experience only enables the knowledge, and counts the knowledge a priori. Williamson's own conclusion is that the distinction is superficial, since much the same skills are at work whichever way a case is classed. The third case is the proof of the four-color theorem, which no person has checked by hand. Thomas Tymoczko concluded that the theorem is known empirically, on the reliability of a machine. Tyler Burge replied that the knowledge is a priori all the same.

In each case an instance is built, on paper, in imagination or in a machine, and something is recognized in it. The building does not report how the world happens to be. It brings what a description describes within reach of a concept.

The deduction that Jackson and Chalmers appeal to has this shape too. Nobody derives by logic, from where the molecules are, that the mug holds water. One works out from the description how the stuff in the mug would look and behave, and sees that the concept of water applies. Ned Block and Robert Stalnaker argue that the truths about water are not entailed a priori by the truths of microphysics ("Conceptual Analysis, Dualism, and the Explanatory Gap", 1999). David Chalmers and Frank Jackson answer that they are, given complete enough information, and that "a priori entailment does not require explicit analysis" (["Conceptual Analysis and Reductive Explanation"](https://consc.net/papers/analysis.html), 2001). A subject judges whether a concept applies "under the hypothesis that the scenario in question obtains." I side with Chalmers and Jackson, and with the reading of the word that their answer needs: reasoning alone includes applying a concept to a situation built from its description. The previous essay said the same of every reduction. It is "a construction carried out under the upper level's types, and not a deduction in the lower level's vocabulary."

The constructor does what these reasoners do, with a better path into its own vehicles. Chalmers's account of the a priori idealizes the reasoner: "If I cannot know that P independent of experience, but another less limited being could do so, then it is a priori that P" (["Does Conceivability Entail Possibility?"](https://consc.net/papers/conceivability.html)). His ideal reasoner, as *Grain* said, "is idealized in capacity but not in architecture." I see no reason to stop there. A reasoner who imagines crimson already writes into its own vehicles, coarsely. What separates such a reasoner from one who cheats is not that one of them has had an experience. It is *Grain*'s test with its second clause: the route runs through what the reasoner knows, and the information about which experience is being built arrives structurally.

None of this closes the gap for Mary. She cannot build, so no reasoning of hers gets from the description to what red is like, and when she leaves the room she learns. The gap is one of architecture.

Nor does it make the account a priori. That an experience is the organized occurrence is a theory. But a theory can be learned a posteriori while what it says follows from the physics by reasoning. That water is H2O was learned from chemistry, and Chalmers and Jackson hold that complete information entails it all the same. For the constructor the same nearly holds of red. It builds the organization and recognizes red. What it cannot get by building is that the same organization is the same experience wherever it occurs. That is the trust of the last section.

The trust is a step of abduction, and abduction is where the strict and the liberal readings of the word part. Chalmers and Jackson grant that we choose among hypotheses about the world "through the use of simplicity considerations such as Ockham's razor," and not by observation or deduction alone. Their claim concerns what is left once the information is complete. Then, they say, a subject can "conclusively know" the rest. The trust does not meet that standard. With the complete physics in hand, that the same organization is the same experience is the simpler hypothesis. It is not a thing conclusively known.

The usual label depends on all this. Chalmers sorts physicalists by whether the physical truths entail the truths about experience a priori. Type A says they do. Type B says the physical truths necessitate the others, and that this is known only a posteriori. *What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right* said that the position "is type B," and for reasoners like us it is. The taxonomy is defined for an idealized reasoner, though. If building an instance and recognizing it counts as reasoning, as it does for the geometer and for crimson, the position is type A for a reasoner who can build, except for the trust. A reader who asks for conclusive knowledge will say that the exception is enough to make it type B. If only deduction from sentences counts, it is type B for every reasoner, and then the truths about water are not entailed a priori either. I would sooner say this than choose a letter.

Under either letter the account differs from the view that Chalmers attacks as type B. He charges that view with a brute necessity, a psychophysical law under another name: an experience goes with a brain state in every possible world, and nothing explains why. The account leaves no room for such a law. A law joins two things, and on the account there is one. An experience is the organized occurrence, as water is H2O. What the account adds to the physics is not a further fact about the world. It says which of the things the physics already describes our concept names.

## Zombies, and the usual strategy

Physicalists who accept that Mary learns something usually explain it by a feature of phenomenal concepts. The concepts are recognitional, or they contain their referents as a quotation contains a word, and this isolates them from physical concepts. Alter spends a chapter on the strategy (chapter 9) and turns Chalmers's dilemma against it. Let C be the claim that we have concepts with the relevant feature, stated without presupposing experience. Either a physical duplicate of us without C is conceivable, or it is not. If it is, C is not physically explicable. If it is not, zombies have C. But "zombies do not share our epistemic situation." So C does not explain ours.

The dilemma supposes that a zombie is conceivable: that it "cannot be ruled out as incoherent by a priori reasoning" (7.3.1). A zombie is a physical duplicate of a person with no experience. On the account, a zombie is conceivable as far as the trust of section nine leaves room. For a human that is some way, and for a reasoner who can build it is very little. What makes a zombie seem plainly possible is something else, a forgetting of the grain of physics. *Grain* traced the forgetting. A description of what a system does, at the grain of cognition, really does leave its experience open. The zombie intuition "extends the verdict from functional descriptions to physics," because "our frame presents functional and physical descriptions alike as third-person structure." The two differ in grain, "and the frame has no way to register the difference." A duplicate at the grain of cognition may differ in experience or lack it. That is fool's orange. A duplicate at the grain of physics cannot.

Alter's most exact statement of his argument has three steps (7.3.3). The physics does not entail the truths about experience a priori. So there is a way the world might turn out, for all reasoning can tell, with the physics and without the experience. So the world could have been that way. He notes that the last step fails for terms like "water," which he calls Twin-Earth-able. That the watery stuff might turn out to be XYZ does not show that water could have been XYZ. So he requires that the truths about experience be stated in terms of another sort, whose "modal profiles are determined a priori." He allows that the terms of physics may be like "water," and that is how Russellian monism enters his conclusion.

Fool's orange denies the requirement. I say of the terms for experience what he allows of the terms of physics. If "orange" is like "water," then whether the physics entails the truths about orange is the same question as whether it entails the truths about water, for a reasoner who has the concept and knows which thing in the description she is. The last section sided with Chalmers and Jackson about water. So the first step is where I leave the argument. It holds of Mary in her room, who lacks the concept. It does not hold of the woman who has the concept, apart from what she takes on trust. The second step rests on a view of possibility, and the next section takes that up.

The dilemma has one answer for both kinds of duplicate. The feature of my concepts that I appeal to is that recognition is coarser than the occurrence it responds to. That has two parts. The coarse recognition is a matter of cognition, and any duplicate has it. The finer occurrence is a fact about vehicles. A cognitive duplicate may lack it. A physical duplicate has it, and to conceive that duplicate as a zombie is to suppose the account false. The part every duplicate shares explains what we all judge: that Mary learns something, and that zombies seem possible. The part about vehicles explains why my judgments are about something. So the feature explains my situation given the account.

That a physical duplicate has the finer occurrence rests on a fact that might have been otherwise for all we could tell in advance: our physics is finer than the grain of our experience. *Grain* allowed observers whose physics is coarser than their own experience. I now doubt that such creatures are possible. It is a guess, and it goes by mechanism. If evolution can make an organization do work, measuring devices can reach that organization too. Nothing in this essay depends on the guess.

The earlier essays already stood apart from the usual strategy. "The usual strategy treats the isolation of phenomenal concepts as a basic feature of how they work. Here it follows from the overflow." And they said of the usual strategy what Chalmers's second horn says: on it, nothing in the world answers to what the physical story seems to leave out.

Alter's test for an explanation of our situation is that "a sufficiently capable reasoner who understands C would be well-positioned to understand our epistemic situation" (9.4.1). The grain account passes it. A concept that closed the gap "would have to specify the grain of its referent, as physical concepts do, while bearing its mode of presentation, as recognitional concepts do," and recognition reaches its object only at the grain of discrimination. A reasoner who read that could predict that Mary learns something, that zombies seem conceivable, and that the seeming is partly right, since a functional description does leave experience open.

Two positions that Alter discusses are close to mine. Jesse Prinz: "[m]aintaining availability is an actual neural process, and Mary cannot undergo that neural process, or any other, simply by reading about it." Robert Howell: phenomenal knowledge "requires actually having the phenomenal states in question." Alter answers both with the dilemma. I differ from both in what I say of the woman who already has the concept. Given the account, she can deduce.

Alter also presses a point of Jackson's and Chalmers's (7.1). When one learns an old truth under a new guise, one generally learns a new truth as well, about the guise. My reply is that the guise is itself an organization, coarser than what it presents, and that the truths about it are structural truths Mary already had. The observer who knows all the physics and "may have no idea" what it is like for Mary lacks the concept.

## Possible and conceivable

Behind the bridge principle stands a view of possibility. For Chalmers the basic modality is tied to "rational consistency, apriority, or conceivability," and "to introduce a further primitive, restricting the space of worlds, is to introduce an unnecessary wheel" (as Alter quotes him, 7.2.5). What is coherent to describe is possible, once the description is ideal.

I hold that a description does not make a possibility. A situation is possible when a support for it could be rebuilt, level by level, down to what generates. I mean this metaphysically: a metaphorical God could do the rebuilding. To conceive a situation is to imagine the rebuilding in a toy model. The toy model is a proxy. It is good where it keeps what matters, and it fails where the imagining skips a step. The zombie is conceived by skipping one. To imagine a physical duplicate in the full detail of the physics is to specify the organization at the grain of experience, and then there is nothing left to subtract.

Chalmers will say that restricting the possible to what can be built is his unnecessary wheel. It is not a further primitive. On the account of the previous essay, modal facts at each level are constructed from the level below, and at the bottom there are no counterfactuals at all, only generation. That removes a modal primitive. It does rest on a postulate of that essay: what descriptions answer to is not one more description. Without the postulate, every structure that can be coherently described would have the same standing, and his view of possibility would follow.

His own definition of ideal conceivability already leans on possibility: a statement is ideally conceivable "when there is a possible subject for whom S is prima facie conceivable, with justification that is undefeatable by better reasoning." He grants that the notion of possibility enters there, in what he calls a roundabout way. Which subjects are possible is then part of the question. On his view they are those that can be coherently described. On mine they are those that could be built.

I do not claim that this meets Alter's principle in general. His principle wants every necessity to follow a priori from non-modal truths about the actual world. What I can claim in general is narrower: no necessity about what occurs is brute. For the one necessity this essay needs, I have given the a priori conditional and the fact.

Both sides have a picture with God in it. Alter describes physicalism as the view that once God fixed the physical truth there was no further work to do (1.4.3). On the account here God builds from below, and in building the physics of a person has already built the organization at the grain of that person's experience.

## Russellian monism

Russellian monism has three parts ([Alter and Pereboom](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/russellian-monism/)). Physics describes the world only in its structure and dynamics. There are quiddities, properties that underlie that structure. And consciousness is constituted at least in part by them. Alter reaches the view from the knowledge argument: if the argument is sound there are structural and non-structural properties, and a theory is likelier to be true if it gives both kinds a part in physical causation (15.1).

The quiddity is the second layer of section four, given a place in the world's furniture. *Grain* said where the idea of it comes from. The one alleged instance of a nature that is not structural is our own experience. It seems so because a structured occurrence is recognized without its structure, and the recognition adds the clause *and that is all*. "On our account, then, the paradigm of an intrinsic nature is structure below the grain of recognition. The Russellian takes the one place where structure hides from us as evidence of something that is not structure." The two views also put experience on opposite sides of physics. For the Russellian it lies beneath physical structure. For me "physics is structure at one grain and experience is structure at another," and the grain of experience is the coarser.

The history is curious. Kant held that we know things only by their relations, and he held it of inner sense as of outer: what I know of my own states is how they appear. What they are in themselves is hidden. Russell kept this for physics and reversed it for experience, where he took a nature to be shown. The view here keeps Kant's half about experience, and puts no natures behind.

Four arguments follow from points already made.

*The Russellian's Mary is no better off.* Alter says the view is reductionist: given the truths about quiddities as well, Mary could deduce all the facts about consciousness. By Lewis's point, which Alter endorses, lessons about quiddities conveyed in black and white would not help her. So the truths about quiddities that matter can be grasped only by acquaintance, and his Mary needs exactly what mine does. Pereboom made the neighbouring point about powers: "Wouldn't Mary be in a position to fully understand such powers while she is still in the room?"

*The advantage is one he half withdraws.* Asked by Brown why the knowledge argument cannot be run again against quiddities, he says that "by hypothesis we don't understand these properties" ([1:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bT74sMNfA8E&t=5835)). Brown replies that the physicalist can say the same about a future physical theory. Alter allows that "there may be parity," and that his advantage "might be a Pyrrhic victory" ([1:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bT74sMNfA8E&t=6320)). I think the sides are not on a par. The structural side has an account of why the gap appears and of what would close it. His side has a placeholder, which he describes himself as "a big mystery at the center" ([2:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bT74sMNfA8E&t=7200)).

*There is something that is not structure on my side too, and it does different work.* The previous essay granted that structure needs something that is not structure, and said that it is production. A critic will call that a quiddity of my own. A quiddity is a nature: what a thing is apart from its relations. Experience is said to be made of it. Production has no character apart from what came from what. There is nothing in it to be acquainted with. A stone is produced as much as an experience is, so production is no special ingredient of experience. Nor does experience depend on which rule does the generating. For humans and for the machines we build, the grain of experience lies far above the grain of physics, and I decline to say what lies beneath the lowest level we could reach.

*Quiddities are fitted into causation by a law.* Robert Howell objects that if the tie between a quiddity and the disposition it underlies could have been otherwise, the quiddity is "just along for the causal ride" (["The Russellian Monist's Problems with Mental Causation"](https://doi.org/10.1093/pq/pqu058), 2015). Alter's answer is that the tie may hold by law. He posits "quiddity laws: laws governing relations among quiddities and the physical dispositions they underlie," and allows that they are "metaphysically contingent categorical-grounding laws" (15.2.4). But a contingent law that ties something non-structural to the physics is what he holds against the dualist, whose interaction of mind and body "is due to contingent psychophysical laws" and is not integration enough (15.1). If the tie is necessary instead, which he leaves open, it is a necessity that nobody can derive, since by his own account we do not understand these properties. And the models he gives of a base that grounds a disposition are a ball's round shape, the chemistry of a fragile vase and the steel of a corkscrew. In each the base is more structure, and the disposition can be worked out from it with no law of grounding. Where a law is needed, the base is not grounding in that way.

On the view here an experience has its part in causation as any pattern does. Interventions on the organization at its grain make a difference to what follows. No law joins the experience to the physics, because it is not a second thing.

There is also the question the Russellian has to answer in the end: how small quiddities combine into the experience of a person. An answer would be an account of organization from the small to the large. That is a structural account, and it is the kind the Russellian said was not enough.

Against structuralism itself Alter and Pereboom give one sentence. Views on which physics involves structure all the way down "seem to yield a world devoid of substance or qualities." The seeming is the one *Grain* explained.

## What would change my mind

- Evidence that some experiential character has no constitutive complexity at any grain. That would be introspection being right about simplicity.
- A failure of structure to predict character where the model of the vehicles is good. The chimerical colors went the right way.
- A reason of principle why no reasoner could have a path from its understanding into its own experiential carrier.
- A case, considered as actual, where treating a phenomenal concept as a kind term gives plainly the wrong thing to say.
- Two organizations that a constructor builds alike and can tell apart when bound.
- Whatever would defeat the previous essay's view of structure, and in particular its unproved requirement on physics.

## The room

The woman in the room can work out the answer. She cannot feel that it is right, because she cannot have the creature's state beside her own. What she takes on trust is that the same organization is the same experience, and a mind that could build the creature's state in itself would have less to take on trust.

Alter and I share two things that most of his critics deny. Mary learns something. And nothing is necessary without a ground. What we disagree about is whether a concept of an experience reaches a nature that is all there on its surface. The shades of blue say it does not.
