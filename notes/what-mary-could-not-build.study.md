The answers state what the essay argues. It is written in the first person by Łukasz Stafiniak, with Claude, as a reply to Torin Alter's *The Matter of Consciousness* and to his conversation with Richard Brown. Section numbers in the answers, such as 7.3.3, are Alter's. The essay argues to the standard Alter sets for himself, the balance of evidence, and three of the questions below ask what it takes on trust or leaves open.

### The woman in the room {kind=state}

What case does the essay open with, how does it differ from Jackson's Mary, and why does Alter move to it? What does each side say the woman can do?

::: answer
Jackson's Mary has learned all the physics of color vision from black-and-white lessons and has never seen a color. When she sees a tomato she learns something, so, the argument goes, the physical truths were not all the truths. The standard reply is that she lacked a concept, and nobody can deduce a truth they lack the concept to state. Alter accepts the reply: nothing interesting follows from her failing to derive the concept. So he gives her the concept. Experienced Mary has seen red, is back in the room with a complete physical description of a creature from another planet, and is asked whether the state it is in when it looks at a tomato is the one she knows as red. Alter holds that she cannot work it out, and calls this a very strong intuition. The essay holds that she can, given one thing: the account on which an experience is the organized occurrence.
:::

### Three steps {kind=state at=the-argument}

State the three steps of Alter's argument. What does he mean by structural information, by a bridge principle, and by a primitive necessity?

::: answer
The first step is an epistemic gap: Mary learns the complete physical truth, can deduce what follows from it, and still learns new truths on release, so some truths about experience cannot be deduced from the physical truth. What she learns in the room is structural, expressible with only logical, mathematical, nomic and perhaps spatiotemporal vocabulary, and so open to any intelligent creature. The second step is a modal gap: what cannot be deduced from the physical truths is not necessitated by them. A claim of that kind is a bridge principle, since it leads from what can be known to what must be so. Its ground is that there are no primitive necessities, which are necessities known a posteriori that do not follow a priori from a priori truths and non-modal truths about the actual world. The third step is an ontological gap: if the physical truth does not necessitate the phenomenal truth, physicalism is false. Alter's favoured alternative is Russellian monism, and he claims no more for the argument than the balance of evidence.
:::

### What the essay grants {kind=recall at=what-i-grant}

The essay calls its answer unusual in how much it grants. List what it grants, and say why the reply the earlier essay made to Chalmers does not reach Alter.

::: answer
Beneath structure there is no nature: production is not structure, but an occurrence is nothing apart from what it came from, so it has no character that a lesson could leave out. Mary learns something. Some truths are not structural, namely those stated with concepts applied by recognition, although every truth about what occurs is reducible and every reduction is structural. There are no primitive necessities. Introspection does not reveal how an experience is constituted. And having an experience is not the only way to know what it is like, as Hume's missing shade of blue shows. The earlier reply said that Chalmers's objection assumes that phenomenal concepts reveal the full nature of their referents. Alter disowns that assumption and argues that his principle stands without it, so a better reply is needed.
:::

### The conditional about the kind {kind=explain at=what-a-necessity-needs}

On the view Alter and the essay share, what does a necessity known only by investigation need? State the conditional the essay offers for red, and say what recognition contributes to the fact that satisfies it and what it does not.

::: answer
It needs a conditional known a priori and a non-modal fact that satisfies it, as "If the underlying explanatory structure of water is H2O, then necessarily water is H2O" is known from the concept of water and chemistry supplies the antecedent. For red the conditional is: if the occurrences that my recognition of red responds to are of a certain kind, then necessarily an experience of red is an occurrence of that kind. The concept says no more. It does not say what the kind is, or at what grain the occurrences must be described for their kind to show. So the satisfying fact has two parts. Recognition sorts occurrences into those it responds to and the rest. The grain comes from the system that has the experiences: it is the grain that the vehicles' own regulation respects. At that grain the occurrences recognition responds to fall in a region of organizations, and experiences of red are the occurrences in that region.
:::

### Two strands of use {kind=distinguish at=what-a-necessity-needs}

Whether the conditional is known a priori depends on how the concept is used. What are the two strands of our use, what follows from each, and on what terms does the conditional hold?

::: answer
We defer to what was there over what we recognized, as we do with water. We also take what we recognize to be simple and wholly shown. On the second strand the concept is of a nature fully apparent in seeing red, so it reaches that nature directly, and what recognition responds to is a further matter: the occurrences might be the nature, or might only accompany it, joined to it by a law. Nothing in the concept says which, so the conditional cannot be known a priori. On the first strand the concept is of those occurrences, as the concept of water is of the stuff in the glass, and the conditional holds as the one about water does. The essay takes the first to be the concept and the second to be a mistake about it. The conditional also holds on the same terms as water's: the concept names the kind of those occurrences to the degree that they form one and are as recognition presents them.
:::

### Two intensions {kind=state at=what-a-necessity-needs}

What are the two intensions of a word, in Jackson's and Chalmers's vocabulary? What does Chalmers hold about a concept of an experience, and what does the essay hold?

::: answer
The first intension is what the word picks out given how the actual world turns out to be. For "water" that is the watery stuff: H2O here, and something else if the world is Twin Earth. The second is what the word picks out in worlds considered as counterfactual, given how the actual world is, which for "water" is H2O in every one. Jackson calls them the A-intension and the C-intension, and Chalmers the primary and the secondary. A sentence is necessary when it is true by the second in every world. Chalmers holds that a sentence is a priori just when it is true by the first however the world turns out, which is Alter's bridge principle in exact form. For "water" the two differ, and that is how a necessity can wait on chemistry. Chalmers holds that for a concept of an experience they coincide, and Alter requires it. The essay holds that they differ, as they do for "water".
:::

### Revelation, strong and weak {kind=distinguish at=fools-orange}

What is Revelation, and who defends it? How does Alter divide it, and what picture of an experience results?

::: answer
Revelation is the doctrine that in having an experience one has its nature apparent to one. Philip Goff defends it, and on it a concept of an experience is transparent. He argues for it from how confident we are in judgments about our own experiences, and from it to a form of Russellian monism: if the nature of red is apparent and no organization is, organization is no part of that nature. Alter does not rest his argument on it and divides it in two. On the strong principle the complete nature of a type of experience is apparent, including whether and how it is constituted, and he rejects that. On the weak one the complete nature is apparent, and that nature does not concern constitution. He holds that the weak one would explain our confidence and is compatible with structural constitution. The resulting picture has two layers: a phenomenal nature, which is apparent, and a constitution, which is not.
:::

### Three ways to differ in constitution {kind=case at=fools-orange}

Alter says a physicalist may count anything that feels orange as orange, however it is made. The essay separates three ways two experiences can differ in how they are constituted. Give the verdict on each, and say where the comparison with water stops.

::: answer
They can differ below the grain of experience, as neurons differ from silicon. That makes no difference to the experience, and here Alter's reply is right. They can differ at the grain of experience and below the grain of recognition, in one kind of mind. The subject cannot tell them apart, they differ in exact character, and both are orange; this case is to orange as heavy water is to water. Or only the cognitive role is the same: a system makes the same judgments and discriminations while its organization at the grain of experience is different or absent. That is fool's orange, and it is not orange. Such a system has the concept so far as a concept is a matter of cognition, and its word names another kind, or no experience at all. What "orange" names is a region: the organizations that this recognition tracks. The comparison with water stops at the boundary. H2O draws water's boundary with no help from how water looks, while the boundary of orange is drawn by recognition's own lumping.
:::

### Why a kind term {kind=argue at=fools-orange}

What evidence does the essay give that we use a concept of an experience as a term for a kind? How does it answer Goff's worry that we could then not be confident our experience is orange?

::: answer
The evidence is how we treat the concept when recognition and what is there come apart. Take a series of shades of blue in which each is indiscriminable from the next, even when the two are seen side by side, and the two ends are plainly different. If experiences we cannot tell apart were the same in character, each would be the same as the next, and so the first the same as the last. We do not say that. We say that somewhere two neighbours differed and we could not tell. That is deference to what was there over what recognition delivered, which is how one treats a term for a kind. To Goff's worry the essay answers that I am confident my experience is orange in the way I am confident that the glass holds water. Recognition is reliable in the conditions it was built for, and judgments at its grain, about kind and similarity, are mostly right. Exact character is present without being known.
:::

### Against the second layer {kind=argue at=the-apparent-nature-is-not-the-complete-nature}

How does the series of shades bear on the weak principle of Revelation? What does Alter grant here, following Tye, and why does the essay say the wine novice cannot carry the argument?

::: answer
The experiences at the ends of the series differ in character, so somewhere along it experiences the subject cannot tell apart differ in character. Part of the character of each is therefore not apparent to the one who has it, and the weak principle, which says the complete phenomenal nature is apparent, is false, for a reason that has nothing to do with constitution. Alter follows Tye in holding that Mary, looking at a rose, knows what a very determinate shade is like even if she could not recognize that shade elsewhere. So he grants that character is finer than recognition, and counts the fine character as known. The essay replies that nothing she could judge of the shade would differ if it were its neighbour in the series, so the fine character is present and not apparent. Alter's wine novice, to whom the complexities of a taste are not apparent, goes the same way on his own description. But a critic will say that training changes the taste, and nothing either taster reports decides between the two readings. The argument rests on the series, where nobody is trained and nothing changes but the shade. If the line between apparent and hidden runs through the nature, no layer is left for a fully apparent nature to occupy.
:::

### One mistake, placed exactly {kind=object at=the-apparent-nature-is-not-the-complete-nature}

Alter says that a physicalist who appeals to inaccurate introspection needs the inaccuracy to be actual and radical. How does the essay answer? What is the simplicity it denies, and what is the evidence against each half of it?

::: answer
The essay holds that introspection is largely right: an experience is present, has a character, and differs from and resembles others. Those aspects are structural, and Mary could have derived them. It makes one mistake, taking the character to have no constitutive complexity. Simplicity is the one aspect that would not be structural, so a single inaccuracy placed there does the work Alter says needs a radical one. Simplicity has to be stated with care. It is not the absence of relations, since a defender of simple natures grants that red resembles orange more than green. It is not the absence of presented parts, since orange looks reddish and yellowish to anyone. The claim is that a character such as pure red is not made up of anything, and is all there in how it is presented. Against the second half the evidence is the series of shades. Against the first it cannot be more resemblances: it is that the make-up of the vehicles explains the resemblances that simple natures must take as given, as when Churchland's model predicted afterimage colors nobody had met. The mistake is also explained: an activity available as a unit is taken for simple, because the unity of the access is read as the simplicity of what is accessed.
:::

### Her deduction {kind=derive at=what-she-can-deduce}

Set out the woman's reasoning. Which premises carry the account, what would the kind be if it were read at another grain, and why is Alter's intuition strong all the same?

::: answer
The kind I call red is the kind of the occurrences that this recognition of mine responds to. At the grain their regulation respects, those occurrences fall in a certain region of organizations, which is a physical truth about her own brain and is in her books. The alien's state lies in that region or it does not, which is a physical truth about the alien. So the alien's state is red, or it is not. The first two premises carry the account. Without the first, what her recognition responds to might only accompany a nature, and the nature would be red. The second is in her books, but the books do not say that this is the grain at which to read the kind. Read at the grain of her neurons, the kind would shut the alien out whatever its organization; read at the grain of cognition, it would let in anything that discriminates as she does. So the conclusion follows given that an experience is the organized occurrence, and not from the physics and her concept alone. Alter's intuition is strong because she cannot check: she has never had the alien's state beside her own. The deduction gives her a truth and no new recognition.
:::

### Why the lessons could not give the concept {kind=explain at=what-mary-lacked}

Why could Mary's lessons not give her the concept? How does this account differ from the replies that she gains only an ability or only an acquaintance, and which premise of Alter's does it reject?

::: answer
A concept applied by recognition gets its way of presenting its object from undergoing the object, and a human's power to write into her own vehicles is coarse. We can interpolate within quality spaces we already have, as Hume's subject fills in a shade, and cannot add a dimension to them. Mary has no dimension to interpolate along. This is not the ability or the acquaintance reply. Alter argues that a gain carrying no information would not be the revelation Mary has, and the essay agrees: with the concept she knows truths she could not state before. The premise it rejects is that a textbook of the complete physical truth is one Mary could fully understand. She can state every truth in it and derive what follows, which is less than understanding it. To understand the description of an organization, at the level of the organization, is to be able to recognize an instance. For most organizations an instance can be built on paper or in imagination. For an experience the recognizing is done by the system that has it, so the instance has to be built in that system's own vehicles.
:::

### Is physics purely structural? {kind=object at=what-mary-lacked}

The essay says Mary's case is the far end of something ordinary. Physics is the hard case for that claim. What two reasons does it draw from Alter's own text, and how does it explain why physics seems purely structural?

::: answer
At every level above the lowest, some truths are stated with concepts applied by recognition that do not say what structure they pick out: we recognize a stone, or a glider, without the concept telling us what it is made of. The first reason physics passes is an old problem Alter grants. A theory in which every term is defined by its role is satisfied by any collection of the right number, so he keeps the nomic concepts primitive, and the physics his argument needs is not purely structural by his own construction. The second is how physics is applied: a track in a detector is recognized as an electron's, and to meet Stoljar's point that spatial and temporal concepts are tied to experience he has to replace the physical concepts with counterparts defined through Ramsey sentences. Physics seems purely structural because its recognizers can be swapped. Any instrument and any observer will do, which is the truth in the remark about Martians. For red only the system that has the experience will do. Objectivity is the degree to which the recognizer can be replaced.
:::

### Cheating {kind=distinguish at=a-reasoner-who-can-build}

What would a reasoner need in order to build an experience from its description? State the test for cheating with its second clause, and apply it to the photograph, to Locked RoboMary and to Alter's geometer with a device. Why are "could a reasoner build red without cheating?" and "is red structural?" one question?

::: answer
It needs a path from its reasoning into its experiential carrier, a carrier general enough to realize the target, and regulation that takes the result in. A technology cheats when it causes the experience independently of what the reasoner knows. The photograph cheats: anyone who looked would have the experience, and garbling Mary's physics would change nothing. Alter's device, which puts anyone who contemplates the axioms into the state of knowing a theorem, fails the same test. The second clause is that a route cheats when the information about which experience is being built arrives other than structurally. Locked RoboMary's route runs through what she knows, but that state B is the red state reaches her only as a fact about what a tomato causes, which is a label. A genuine constructor knows why this organization is red: what it is more like and less like, and what it opposes. If red were an atom of quality there would be no why, only labels, and every route would cheat. So a genuine constructor is possible only if red is structural.
:::

### Two jobs for construction {kind=explain at=a-reasoner-who-can-build}

Alter's verdict is that the robot does not deduce, and the strongest form of the objection is that a dualist with laws predicts the same success. What does the essay concede, what two jobs does it give construction, and what does Alter's own traveller show?

::: answer
The essay concedes that production is not deduction and does not rest the necessity on it. The necessity is derived, from the conditional about the kind and the woman's deduction. Construction does a different job: it is how a reasoner who lacks the concept gets it from the description. Its second job matters more. The human who has seen red cannot check her conclusion about the alien, and the constructor can. It builds the alien's organization, binds it beside its own red in one field, and recognizes. If it built two organizations alike and could tell them apart when bound, the account would be wrong, so the account is open to an experiment. Alter's traveller who has explored a perfect simulation of New York has no justified "Aha!" on arriving, and learns nothing of the city's character by arriving. A constructor that had built red would have none on leaving the room. The simulation is a cheat and the constructor is not, but the case shows what the revelation follows: whether the experience has occurred, by whatever route.
:::

### What is taken on trust {kind=limits at=what-is-taken-on-trust}

What does the account take on trust within one subject, across subjects, and for a constructor? How does the essay compare its own trust with what Alter's alternative asks for?

::: answer
Within one subject: since blue is structural and not an atom, experiences I cannot tell apart may differ, so when I take today's blue to be yesterday's I trust that they do not differ in some way I fail to notice. Across subjects the trust is larger. The woman concludes that the alien's state is red because their organizations fall in one region, and that the same organization is the same experience is the account itself, which for her is a leap. A constructor is better placed. It can bind any two experiences beside each other, so whatever can be told apart side by side is told apart, and it knows the organization it built. What it still takes on trust concerns differences no binding reveals, so its leap is across an insignificant gap. One caveat is that binding makes a new context, and context helps fix exact character. The essay says its argument can be doubted here. But this trust is of a kind a constructor could test, while Alter's alternative asks for trust in a nature that no description reaches, which nothing could test.
:::

### What reasoning alone includes {kind=case at=by-reasoning-alone}

Alter's bridge principle and the usual labels are stated in terms of the a priori. What three cases does the essay use to show what reasoning alone includes, what do they have in common, and how does the deduction about water fit?

::: answer
Kind's geometer proves a theorem with a diagram, and what such a geometer knows is counted a priori. Williamson's example is "All crimson things are red": one comes to know it by imagining crimson and applying the concept of red to what one imagines, and tradition says the experience only enables the knowledge. The proof of the four-color theorem was checked by no person: Tymoczko concluded that the theorem is known a posteriori, and Burge replied that the knowledge is a priori all the same. In each case an instance is built, on paper, in imagination or in a machine, and something is recognized in it. The building does not report how the world happens to be. It brings what a description describes within reach of a concept. The deduction about water has this shape: nobody derives by logic, from where the molecules are, that the mug holds water. Block and Stalnaker argue that such truths are not entailed a priori by microphysics, and Chalmers and Jackson answer that they are. The essay sides with Chalmers and Jackson and with the reading of the word their answer needs. The constructor does what these reasoners do, with a better path into its own vehicles.
:::

### The label, stated conditionally {kind=distinguish at=by-reasoning-alone}

How does Chalmers define type A and type B? Under which readings of "a priori" is the essay's position each, and what part does abduction play? How does the account differ, under either letter, from the type B that Chalmers attacks?

::: answer
Type A says there is no epistemic gap between the physical truths and the truths about experience. Type B says there is a gap in what can be known and none in the world. For reasoners like us the position is type B, since Mary cannot build and so learns on release: the gap is one of architecture. The taxonomy is defined for an idealized reasoner. If building an instance and recognizing it counts as reasoning, the position is type A for a reasoner who can build, except for the trust. The trust is a step of abduction: with the complete physics in hand, that the same organization is the same experience is the simpler hypothesis and not a thing conclusively known, so a reader who asks for conclusive knowledge will say the exception makes it type B. If only deduction from sentences counts, it is type B for every reasoner, and then the truths about water are not entailed a priori either. Under either letter the account lacks what Chalmers charges type B with, a principle that has the epistemic status of a fundamental law and the ontological status of an identity. It reaches its identity as chemistry reached water's, and it leaves no room for a psychophysical law, since a law joins two things and on the account there is one.
:::

### The dilemma and two kinds of duplicate {kind=object at=zombies-and-the-usual-strategy}

State the dilemma Alter turns against explanations that appeal to a feature of phenomenal concepts. In what sense does the essay allow that a zombie is conceivable, what does it say makes one seem plainly possible, and how does it answer the dilemma?

::: answer
Let C be the claim that we have concepts with the relevant feature. Either a physical duplicate of us without C is conceivable, in which case C is not physically explicable, or it is not, in which case zombies have C; but zombies do not share our epistemic situation, so C does not explain ours. On the account a zombie is conceivable as far as the trust leaves room, which for a human is some way and for a reasoner who can build is very little. What makes a zombie seem plainly possible is a forgetting of the grain of physics: a functional description really does leave experience open, and the intuition extends that verdict to physics, because our frame presents both as third-person structure. A duplicate at the grain of cognition may differ in experience or lack it, which is fool's orange. A duplicate at the grain of physics cannot. The feature the essay appeals to is that recognition is coarser than the occurrence it responds to, and it has two parts. The coarse recognition is a matter of cognition, and any duplicate has it. The finer occurrence is a fact about vehicles, which a cognitive duplicate may lack and a physical duplicate has. The shared part explains what we all judge. The part about vehicles explains why the judgments are about something.
:::

### Where the essay leaves Alter's exact argument {kind=argue at=zombies-and-the-usual-strategy}

Give the three steps of Alter's most exact statement of his argument. What requirement does he place on the terms for experience, why, and what does the essay do with it?

::: answer
The physics does not entail the truths about experience a priori. So there is a way the world might turn out, for all reasoning can tell, with the physics and without the experience. So the world could have been that way. Alter notes that the last step fails for terms like "water", which he calls Twin-Earth-able: that the watery stuff might turn out to be XYZ does not show that water could have been XYZ. So he requires that the truths about experience be stated in terms of another sort, whose modal profiles are determined a priori. He allows that the terms of physics may be like "water", which is how Russellian monism enters his conclusion. Fool's orange denies the requirement: the essay says of the terms for experience what he allows of the terms of physics. Then whether the physics entails the truths about orange is the same question as whether it entails the truths about water, for a reasoner who has the concept and knows which thing in the description she is. Having sided with Chalmers and Jackson about water, the essay leaves the argument at its first step. That step holds of Mary in her room, who lacks the concept, and not of the woman who has it, apart from what she takes on trust.
:::

### Possible and conceivable {kind=distinguish at=possible-and-conceivable}

What view of possibility stands behind the bridge principle, and what does the essay put in its place? Why is the essay's restriction not an extra primitive, what does it rest on, and what does the essay decline to claim?

::: answer
For Chalmers the basic modality is tied to rational consistency, apriority or conceivability, so that what is coherent to describe is possible once the description is ideal. The essay holds that a description does not make a possibility. A situation is possible when a support for it could be rebuilt, level by level, down to what generates, and to conceive a situation is to imagine the rebuilding in a toy model, which fails where the imagining skips a step. The zombie is conceived by skipping one: to imagine a physical duplicate in the full detail of the physics is to specify the organization at the grain of experience. This is not a further primitive, because on the previous essay's account modal facts at each level are constructed from the level below, and at the bottom there is only generation. It does rest on that essay's postulate that what descriptions answer to is not one more description; without it every coherently describable structure would have the same standing and Chalmers's view would follow. His own definition of ideal conceivability also leans on which subjects are possible. The essay does not claim to meet Alter's principle in general, only that no necessity about what occurs is brute, and that for the one necessity it needs it has given the conditional and the fact.
:::

### Four arguments against Russellian monism {kind=object at=russellian-monism}

What are the three parts of Russellian monism, and where does the essay say the idea of a quiddity comes from? What is curious about the history? Give the four arguments.

::: answer
Physics describes the world only in its structure and dynamics, there are quiddities that underlie that structure, and the quiddities bear on consciousness. The essay's diagnosis is that the one alleged instance of a non-structural nature is our own experience, which seems so because a structured occurrence is recognized without its structure. The history is curious because Kant held that we know things only by their relations, in inner sense as in outer, while Russell kept this for physics and reversed it for experience. First, the Russellian's Mary is no better off: lessons about quiddities in black and white would not help her, so she needs what the essay's Mary needs. Second, Alter half withdraws the advantage: he says we do not understand these properties, allows that there may be parity, and calls the view's centre a big mystery. Third, production is not a quiddity: it has no character apart from what came from what, nothing in it to be acquainted with, and is no special ingredient of experience. Fourth, quiddities are fitted into causation by a law. A contingent law of that kind is what Alter holds against the dualist, a necessary tie would be one nobody can derive, and his own models of grounding, such as a ball's round shape, are cases where the base is more structure and no law is needed. On the essay's view an experience has its part in causation as any pattern does, with no law, because it is not a second thing.
:::

### What would change the author's mind {kind=limits at=what-would-change-my-mind}

What does the essay list as evidence that would change its author's mind?

::: answer
Evidence that some experiential character has no constitutive complexity at any grain, which would be introspection being right about simplicity. A failure of structure to predict character where the model of the vehicles is good. A reason of principle why no reasoner could have a path from its understanding into its own experiential carrier. A case, considered as actual, where treating a phenomenal concept as a kind term gives plainly the wrong thing to say. Two organizations that a constructor builds alike and can tell apart when bound. And whatever would defeat the previous essay's view of structure, in particular its unproved requirement on physics.
:::
