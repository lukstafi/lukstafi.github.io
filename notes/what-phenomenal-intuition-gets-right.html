What Phenomenal Intuition Gets Right

Łukasz Stafiniak and Codex (OpenAI) — first draft, 10 October 2026

An experience of a red circle need not be a geometrically circular event. Whatever represents the circle in a brain need not arrange itself into a little circle, and a future artificial experiencer would not have to draw one inside its processor. Nevertheless, an experience of a red circle differs from an experience of a red square. The difference is real even if the representing processes do not literally have the shapes they represent.

This gives us two questions. What constitutes that actual difference? And what do we get right when we recognize it experientially? François Kammerer’s House of Mirrors makes the second question unusually demanding. He accepts real processes that we discriminate, attend to, and describe as experiences. He argues that those processes do not satisfy the phenomenal concepts through which we think we recognize them. Their supposed phenomenality is an illusion.

I want to use his account to understand a mistake while resisting his conclusion about its extent. My proposal is that phenomenal intuition can correctly present a concrete representational occurrence and its distinctive character. The mistake comes when we take that recognizable character to have a simple, uncomposed nature. What is accessible without a presentation of its complexity need not be constitutionally simple.

This refines the position of Redness Is an Experience. That essay put too much explanatory weight on a supposed slide into property language. Experiences can have properties. A concrete occurrence can have an intrinsic character. Neither commitment requires primitive qualitative ingredients. The more promising diagnosis is a slide from distinctness to simplicity. Another argument from that essay survives: if the regulation through which an experience is undergone partly constitutes it, we cannot remove that regulation and assume that the very same phenomenal occurrence remains, merely unexperienced.

These claims belong within a larger account of minds. A companion inquiry into cognition without mentality will engage N. Katherine Hayles’s Bacteria to AI: Human Futures with Our Nonhuman Symbionts. Her expansion of cognition across biological and technical systems makes the question pressing: which achievements warrant cognitive description, and which warrant psychological description? Our distinction between the two should be tested through that engagement, rather than attributed to Hayles. The companion Experience, Selfhood, and the Organization of a Mind asks which actual regulatory organization constitutes experience, and what relation it bears to selfhood and personhood. Here the question is what that organization could make true in phenomenal recognition, and how it could explain the temptation to claim more.1

Where Brown and Kammerer disagree

Richard Brown’s discussion with Kammerer on Consciousness Live! brings the disagreement into focus. Brown insists that scientific and philosophical revisions must account for the experiential presence from which inquiry begins. Locating redness in external objects, relocating a quality into the mind, and giving a physical account of experience are different theoretical moves. Brown’s point is that what calls for explanation does not disappear when we change the theory. In their exchange about redness, he presses this continuity through changes in its proposed location and nature. Discussion at 2:11:50.

Kammerer does not think that adopting illusionism makes the world go dark. His account preserves perceptual discrimination, access, action, and introspective representations. It explains why ordinary subjects judge that their lives contain phenomenal experiences. But these real processes, he argues, fail to satisfy the concepts under which we grasp them as phenomenal. Brown directly disputes that conclusion: errors in how we characterize our epistemic relationship to consciousness need not eliminate consciousness. Their exchange is substantially about whether correcting a concept preserves its target. Discussion at 1:31:00.

Brown’s superblindsight challenge makes the difference vivid. A creature might discriminate and use visual information without a visually present world. Brown does not see how adding mistaken consciousness judgments would supply what is missing. Kammerer replies that ordinary cognition differs from superblindsight in many functional respects, including its production of the phenomenal illusion. In the phenomenal sense, however, he accepts that neither has what the realist affirms. The dispute is not resolved by establishing that ordinary vision and superblindsight have different mechanisms. The question is what those differences constitute. Discussion at 2:15:00.

There is a pressure on Brown’s side too. At one point he connects undergoing an experience directly with knowing what it is like, without requiring a further phenomenal concept. Kammerer challenges the apparent exemption of this knowledge from the fallibility of the rest of our cognitive apparatus. I want to separate two claims that can become entangled here: an experience, if it occurs, is an actual presence; a judgment that an experience occurs can be mistaken. The first is a constitutive commitment. It does not establish the second judgment’s infallibility. Discussion at 2:04:43.

Kammerer’s final response also matters. He presents illusionism as an inference to the best explanation, rather than making everything depend on deriving a formal contradiction from physical phenomenal consciousness. A realist does not defeat that argument merely by describing a coherent possibility. We need an account that explains both the actual organization and our ways of recognizing and misrecognizing it. Closing response at 2:19:36.

The occurrence is the vehicle

Our identity proposal begins with a distinction between a representational vehicle and its content. The vehicle is a concrete state or process doing the representing. In this framework, contents are abstracta: what is represented should not be confused with another concrete occurrence inside the system. An experience of red is the vehicle in its constitutive organization. It is not a further event supported by that vehicle, nor an abstract content promoted into concrete existence.

This accommodates unsuccessful representation. A red object need not exist for a dreamed red object to be experienced. The vehicle can be present, and can have representational character, while what it represents is absent or misdescribed. Phenomenal recognition could therefore get two things right even in hallucination: an occurrence exists, and it presents something as being some way. Neither truth requires the represented object to exist.

Our earlier appeal to acquaintance was intended to secure this actuality. If there is actual regulation of a particular vehicle, that vehicle exists. Merely representing something does not similarly guarantee its existence. But the existence argument is deliberately limited. It does not derive phenomenality from the bare fact of a relation. Thermostats, cooling systems, and blood supplies have actual relata too. The substantive proposal concerns regulation responsive to representational distinctions and their consistency within a minded system.

That regulation can maintain distinctions, preserve disagreement, assess reliability, and alter the persistence or coordination of the vehicles concerned. Consistency does not mean forcing everything into agreement. An organization that silences inconvenient input might become less epistemically adequate while becoming more internally uniform. Dreams and imagination also require distinguishing sources and modes of representation, rather than treating accuracy as correspondence between every active representation and the current external environment.

The scientific task is consequently rich. It could identify particular levels of sensory representation, temporal dependencies, and arrangements for binding and correction. An intermediate representational level could be constitutively important without being the sole level involved. Nothing about the initial existence argument decides these questions. It prevents an actual vehicle from disappearing into an abstraction; the account of regulation must explain what kind of occurrence it is.

This is also where our metaphysical ladder matters. Physical processes can realize biological, computational, and cognitive organization. Genuine cognition need not yet establish mentality: causally effective discrimination and representational processing need not amount to understanding or evaluating within a psychological life. Mindedness supplies a further organization. Phenomenality and personhood are then proposed as distinct achievements requiring mindedness, without either being defined through the other. These are hypotheses about constitution, not names that manufacture the distinctions they describe.

Distinctness without a little inner picture

Return to the red circle and red square. The experiences can differ in their actual constitution even though neither vehicle is literally circular or square. It is a content–vehicle confusion to infer the represented shape directly of the representing process. It would be another mistake to conclude that, because the vehicles do not instantiate the represented shapes, there is no genuine experiential difference between them.

Our positive identity claim places that difference in the vehicles’ concrete organization. Phenomenal intuition can register a difference which scientific inquiry subsequently explains. The intuitive discrimination need not already display the explanatory decomposition. Recognizing two occurrences as different is not the same achievement as identifying which processes constitute the difference.

I want to preserve an intrinsic aspect of this character. The occurrence has a constitution of its own; it is not made into a different experience merely by receiving a different report or a different association elsewhere. But the relevant occurrence includes its constitutive relations (which are causally affecting the occurrence). Calling its character intrinsic does not mean detaching a small neural fragment from the dynamics sustaining it and demanding that it remain experiential. The boundary of the occurrence is part of the explanatory task.

The hypothesis explored in Can Two Minds Share One Experience makes this distinction consequential. One concrete experiential process might participate in two subjects’ cognitive economies while retaining its own qualitative character. The subjects could connect it with different memories and purposes. If those differences alter the process’s constitutive regulation, its character may change. If they do not, the existence of two surrounding perspectives need not multiply or recolor the shared occurrence. That possibility requires distinguishing constitutive dependencies from other cognitive connections.

This is stronger than saying that there are real vehicles behind a false appearance. I am proposing that their presence, representational character, and actual distinctness are among the things phenomenal intuition can get right. The realist commitment needs all of this positive content if it is to answer Kammerer’s concern that we are merely preserving an unspecified something and calling it experience.

What computational symmetry leaves unsettled

A computational system can genuinely discriminate red and green without deriving its significance from an observer’s arbitrary interpretation. It can receive inputs produced by different wavelength ranges, preserve their differences, and use them to control subsequent activity. Its computation is a real pattern in the causal nexus. We should not call it merely pretend cognition because we doubt that it experiences anything.

Now consider a symmetrical realization. Suppose the cognitively specified causal organization admits an automorphism exchanging the red and green pathways. The transformation preserves the vehicles together with their corresponding causal connections and transition structure. We are not comparing two components after detaching them from their surroundings. Nor does the argument consist in an experimenter changing labels. The symmetry is a feature of the organization being offered as an explanation.

The input connections remain genuine. Red and green light are not physically identical, and an automorphism of a cognitive organization is not automatically a symmetry of the entire physical environment with every physical quantity fixed. The question is whether the offered account supplies a constitutive difference between the occurrences themselves, or only corresponding positions in a symmetrical organization connected to different stimuli. A proposed symmetry must be checked at the level the theory actually claims is sufficient.

My identity commitment supplies the further premise: vehicles identical in the full constitution relevant to experience cannot nevertheless be intrinsically red and green experiences merely because they occupy those corresponding cognitive positions. If they differ experientially, something in their experiential constitution differs. The difference cannot be conjured by the fact that the computation genuinely sorts two kinds of input.

This gives us a reason to look beyond identification with an abstract cognitive function. It is not a proof against every theory that might be called functionalist. A richer theory might identify an asymmetry omitted by the proposed automorphism, or argue that the relevant relations to inputs already constitute experiential character. The disagreement would then concern why those differences suffice. Conversely, asserting a full physical duplication while stipulating different intrinsic characters would contradict the identity commitment rather than support it.

The nonfunctionalist proposal is that concrete constitution matters in ways not exhausted by the computed cognitive function. This does not prohibit realization on GPUs or other artificial substrates. An artificial system could enact the requisite organization. Computing a description of that organization, or matching a selected cognitive mapping, would not by itself establish that it does. The companion cognition essay must examine the lower-level organization in its own right; here its importance is that genuine cognitive discrimination does not settle the constitution of experienced difference.

Available through its character

We can give the positive proposal more shape through a computational analogy, without treating it as a literal computational specification. Experiential occurrences make themselves available to attention through their distinctness, in something like a content-addressable-memory arrangement. The organization can select a vehicle through what distinguishes it, rather than needing a separate symbolic address that says which internal item is intended.

The target is the concrete occurrence. Attention does not retrieve an abstract content and thereby turn it into an experience. Nor does it merely obtain a description which could be generated in the occurrence’s absence. In successful acquaintance, the distinctive activity participates in the processes that select, maintain, assess, and regulate that activity. Its character makes a difference to the manner of involvement.

This is a proposal about a constitutive organization, not a claim that every associative memory has experiences. A hardware content-addressable memory already performs selection by matching stored patterns. Calling that operation attention would not establish phenomenality. Our account requires identifying what mode of such availability occurs within a minded system and how it belongs to the ongoing regulation constituting the experiential vehicle.

Availability also differs from current focal attention. An occurrence might be maintained in the relevant organization without being selected for a deliberate judgment or verbal report. This lets us distinguish the conditions of experience from the conditions of reflecting on experience. It does not settle how extensive unattended experience is, or which maintenance processes suffice. Those are questions for the theory rather than consequences of the analogy.

This offers a naturalistic sense in which an occurrence can reveal itself. Its actual distinctive character enables a form of access to that very occurrence. Such access need not reveal every constituent or dependency, and it need not occur every time a phenomenal concept is deployed. It can nevertheless be access to something real and distinctive, rather than an empty demonstrative gesture toward whatever happens to cause a report.

What Kammerer explains

Kammerer’s Meta-Cartesian account connects phenomenal thought to our representation of evidence. His Cartesian Device characterizes certain states as primitive direct evidence. They are represented as self-presenting, self-revealing, and infallibly directly presented. Primitiveness is important here: the relevant nature is represented as simple and uncomposed, captured by the representation of it. Self-presentation can then be construed as revelation of that nature.2

He proposes an Evidence-by-Resemblance Mechanism to implement this architecture. Roughly, it redeploys a sensory concept such as RED in a metacognitive concept RED. Similarity between representations is used as a proxy for similarity between their referents. A state is treated as providing direct evidence for what it distinctively resembles. Applying this scheme to the state itself helps generate the apparent self-revelation and infallibility. The resulting metacognitive concepts represent phenomenal properties, rather than merely marking that a sensory process happened.3

This is useful to a realist. An account of how phenomenal thought acquires its apparent epistemic privileges need not be rejected because its author is an illusionist. Indeed, Kammerer explicitly distinguishes his architectural and implementation hypotheses from the additional claim that phenomenal concepts are unsatisfied. On their own, the first two remain neutral between realism and illusionism. His argument for elimination has to be supplied separately.4

Our account would not simply accept every detail of his proposed mechanism. In particular, actual regulation through distinctive vehicles might support successful recognition without requiring the resemblance relations his mechanism attributes. But his analysis helps formulate the question precisely. Which aspects of the apparent epistemic relation could be realized by an organized occurrence, and which arise from a mistaken characterization of it?

The project is to minimize that discrepancy. A concrete vehicle supplies actuality. Its constitutive distinctions supply character. Availability through those distinctions supplies a candidate basis for acquaintance and self-revelation. Regulation can supply epistemic value without providing an infallible grasp of a primitive nature. As this positive account becomes more specific, less of phenomenal thought has to be treated as fabrication. The remaining error might be an understandable cognitive mistake made in taking up a substantially veridical presentation.

The slip into simplicity

The central candidate mistake is this: because the constitutive complexity is not presented in recognizing an experiential character, the character is taken to have no constitutive complexity. The system can address the occurrence as one distinctive presence. It then treats that unity of access as evidence of simplicity in what is accessed.

There is no valid inference from the first to the second. A complexly constituted activity can be available as a unit. Its recognizability need not require recognition of each constituent. Nor must that activity conceal its real character merely because it does not reveal its decomposition. The character through which it is recognized may be the real character of the complex activity.

This is why I no longer want to say that the mistake consists in sliding from an experience to a property. Ordinary property attribution can record a genuine difference in how occurrences are constituted. Nor is self-revelation itself necessarily the mistake. What I want to preserve is access through actual character, while denying that this entails a simple nature exhausted by that access. Kammerer’s richer package of self-revelation should not be assumed whenever we use the term in this more limited sense.

This separates the present proposal from the observation that a scientific description does not reproduce an experience. That observation is true but weak. Reading a description of viscosity does not make the page viscous either. The more specific hypothesis concerns an architecture that can recognize a concrete difference without representing the complexity constituting it, and can then misread this limited access as presentation of an uncomposed nature.

The error could arise before explicit philosophical theorizing. It might be automatic, reinforced by repeated successful discrimination, and deeply entrenched. Yet its being pre-theoretical does not establish that it is part of the content through which the occurrence is recognized. The question is whether primitive simplicity belongs to that presentation itself or to a further, perhaps habitual, uptake of it. Only investigation of the mechanism can decide where to locate the mistake. This investigation can show whether illusionism or realism has the upper hand.

Presence without infallible recognition

The sense in which experience cannot fail to be present needs similar care. On our identity proposal, an actual experiential occurrence cannot fail to exist or fail to have representational character. A hallucinated object can be absent while the hallucination is an actual occurrence. This is the metaphysical security of presence.

It does not follow that an assertion of experience guarantees its target. A phenomenal concept can misfire. Deploying it need not bring the alleged experience into existence. The concept’s own deployment is a concrete cognitive process, but whether that process is experiential is another question. We must not manufacture an experience merely by observing that something real produced the claim.

The existence claim by itself is modest: any actual occurrence exists. The substantive part is the proposed identification of certain regulated representational vehicles as experiential presences, together with an account of how their actual distinctness enables recognition. This gives the recognition something specific to get right. It avoids treating bare actuality as a proof of phenomenality.

We can therefore distinguish an experience’s being present, its being available to attention, its being successfully recognized, and its being correctly described in every respect. These need not occur together. A system could misclassify a present occurrence, fail to attend to it, or claim one that is absent. None of these possibilities requires a genuine experiential presence to consist in a systematic illusion of presence.

This is where my position differs from defending consciousness through an unrestricted claim of certainty. I do not need every phenomenal judgment to be beyond correction. I need a positive account on which some recognitions genuinely concern an occurrence and correctly characterize aspects of it. That remains a substantive hypothesis, open to the sorts of evidence that could undermine its proposed mechanism.

Why phenomenal character is not detachable

In Redness Is an Experience, particularly its postscript on the Brown–Coleman debate, I argued against treating phenomenal qualities as detachable from the experience of them. The opposing proposal distinguishes a quality from awareness of it: the very same redness could remain instantiated after awareness ceases. There is no immediate formal contradiction in that proposal. The argument against it rests on what constitutes the experience, independently of whether its character is simple or complex.

Our objection concerns constitution. If the regulatory intervention is part of what the experiential occurrence is, removing it changes the occurrence. Preserving a local activity pattern or the represented content does not preserve the full constitution while merely turning off access. What remains can still have causal powers, represent something, or contribute to nonphenomenal mental activity. Its continuity with the earlier process does not establish continuity of the phenomenal occurrence.

This must not be confused with saying that every lapse of focal attention extinguishes experience. The relevant withdrawal is the loss of constitutive regulatory involvement, not simply the absence of a report, a higher-order judgment, or deliberate inspection. Which interventions remove that involvement is precisely what the positive account must specify.

A defender of detachable qualities could accept the causal changes and deny their constitutive significance. Brown’s challenge to acquaintance accounts also remains: what does the involvement contribute, rather than merely disclose? Our proposal answers that it sustains the particular form of availability and coordinated activity in which the vehicle has its experiential character. Whether that answer succeeds depends on an adequate theory of the organization. The argument cannot be completed just by insisting that the word experience implies someone undergoing it.

The two corrections to the earlier essay can thus coexist. We retain the claim that phenomenality is constituted in the undergoing, while abandoning a general objection to properties. And we replace a sweeping rejection of qualitative character with an attempt to explain why real, complex character can be mistaken for something simple.

The distinction illusionists can preserve too

An illusionist has resources to distinguish an actual quasi-phenomenal occurrence from a consciousness claim generated in some other way. Kammerer separates the distribution of quasi-phenomenality from the distribution of the phenomenal illusion. An animal might possess states of the relevant kind without representing them as phenomenal. Conversely, a sophisticated AI might have the requisite metacognitive illusion while lacking states sufficiently like those tracked in human phenomenal introspection.5

This matters for agentic language models. Relaxing restrictions on consciousness claims could change their verbal behaviour without establishing either the relevant introspective mechanism or the relevant underlying occurrences. Suppressing the claims would not establish absence either. These alternatives remain open even if the system’s cognition is genuine and even if it has some degree of mentality. A theory needs evidence about how the claims arise and what, if anything, they track.

We should therefore not claim a victory over illusionism merely because our phenomenal concepts can misfire. Nor does the disagreement consist simply in whether there is something real behind ordinary consciousness talk. Kammerer accepts real processes; Frankish explicitly distinguishes a positive account of quasi-phenomenal organization from an explanation of its introspective misrepresentation.6

The stronger realist claim is that the relevant organization constitutes an experiential presence whose distinctive character can be correctly recognized. Kammerer can investigate that same organization while denying this identification. He also questions whether quasi-phenomenality marks one privileged kind: similarities in neural realization and similarities in functional organization can support different classifications. Our account must earn any stronger claim that its constitutive conditions identify a sufficiently determinate achievement.7

The same discipline applies to ethics. Calling something phenomenal or quasi-phenomenal does not by itself explain why it matters. I take experiential organization and personhood to be distinct morally significant achievements, both requiring mindedness. Personhood does not wait for a phenomenal entrance qualification. Conversely, a being’s lack of personal self-government does not make its experiences insignificant. This plural structure survives the terminology dispute, while leaving the grounds and distribution of each kind of concern to be argued for.

What the realist still owes

Kammerer has an objection prepared for this project. We might preserve actual organization, distinguish correct from incorrect reports, and discard every troublesome feature, only to discover that we have changed the subject. His challenge is whether the discarded characterizations belong to the core of phenomenal concepts. If they do, retaining the causal referent while rejecting those characterizations may amount to replacing the concept with a quasi-phenomenal one.8

Our answer cannot simply be that we prefer the realist label. It must identify what phenomenal recognition already gets right and explain why the mistake about simplicity can be corrected without losing its target. Presence, representational character, actual distinctness, and availability through that distinctness are proposed as positive commitments. They are not meant as an empty label for whichever mechanism science eventually discovers.

There is also a psychological burden. Kammerer argues that familiar fallacy accounts fail to explain the persistent difficulty of positively understanding physical phenomenal consciousness. He does not merely demand that the intuition disappear after correction: his comparison with ordinary fallacies allows a misleading tendency to persist while the correct answer becomes clearly intelligible. Our account must therefore do more than remind the reader that cognitive errors can be stubborn. It must make a complex occurrence’s experiential recognizability intelligible and explain why the temptation toward simplicity remains.9

The availability proposal is intended to help with that burden. If the same constitution supports real discrimination, attentional selection, and the limited exposure of its own complexity, we have a candidate common explanation of successful recognition and overreach. A useful inquiry would distinguish interventions that alter the occurrence’s constitutive character from interventions that alter only judgments about primitiveness or certainty. The theory would have to explain the resulting discriminations and reports without assuming that every report transparently measures experience.

The scientific burden is equally important. We must specify the organization well enough to distinguish experiential regulation from ordinary associative retrieval, genuine but nonmental cognition, and nonphenomenal mental activity. We must show why the constitutive differences we identify correspond to experiential differences. The symmetry example motivates that task; it does not perform it. If the proposed organization turns out to track only access, our identification would need revision.

The position is a nonfunctionalist physicalist realism about experience, with the qualification that the target of its nonfunctionalism is reduction to the computed cognitive function. Whether it should also be called type B materialism depends on the epistemic gap it ultimately preserves. Different routes of access do not by themselves establish that a principled gap remains. Conversely, correcting the illusion of simplicity need not make every experiential truth derivable a priori from a physical description. The account should determine the classification rather than being fitted to one in advance.

Kammerer’s account is valuable because it lets us ask where the mistake lies with greater precision. We can investigate how a mind treats its own evidence as primitive without conceding that every aspect of experiential recognition fails. The realist project is to identify the concrete occurrence, explain how its character makes it available, and show how this success can encourage a mistaken estimate of its simplicity. If that project succeeds, we will have learned something about experience from illusionism without having learned that experience was never there.
