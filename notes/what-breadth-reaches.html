What Breadth Reaches: Epistemic Agency Across Timescales

Łukasz Stafiniak and Claude (Anthropic)

Revised 25 October 2026: a light editorial pass; §2 corrected on interference in simple online learners; §3 corrected on what the KV cache carries across compaction, and updated with the first evidence from hybrid recurrent models now deployed at the frontier; a broken link fixed. Revised 26 October 2026: §3 updated with evidence that the recurrent state of hybrids carries output language and persona, and its capacity estimate tied to its recall criterion.

David Deutsch holds that current AI systems are not creating knowledge. They are interpolating sophisticated curves over training data, which is a different kind of operation. Dwarkesh Patel, surveying the same systems with practical engineering attention, agrees that something is missing, and locates the missing piece more concretely: continual learning, the capacity to accumulate competence across sessions the way a person does over a career. Nathan Lambert, responding to Dwarkesh, argues that the missing piece may be functionally substitutable. Scaling context, reasoning and scaffolding, on his view, can produce something indistinguishable from continual learning without the architectural work the strong reading demands.

The three positions share a structure. Each takes the question of what AI systems lack to be answerable before careful work on what knowing actually is, what breadth can substitute for, and at what scale the substitution succeeds or fails. We think the question is more interesting than any of the three positions allows, and that the framework developed across this series gives a way to ask it that none of them quite reaches.

Earlier articles did timescale-specific work that this one points at rather than rebuilds. “Feedback, Recurrence, and the Question of AI Consciousness” and “The Settling Backstop” addressed the seconds-scale phenomenal window, locating a specific privilege for sustained center-out regulatory settling against the world. “Operations of Discovery” considered what humans contribute alongside AI systems at the timescale of evidential weight: the long arc of accumulated discrimination that distinguishes a senior collaborator from a competent one. “Is Knowledge Both Capability and Alignment?” separated the ISA channel from the homeostatic channel, each doing its own grounding work. This article adds a view across the medium and long timescales. Knowledge has multiple species, channels of grounding distinguish them, and the kind of epistemic agent a system constitutes is determined by which species its affordances support.

The first question we take up is independent of timescale: Deutsch’s question of whether knowledge creation is happening at all. The second concerns the long timescale of integration across sessions, where Dwarkesh’s saxophone learner tests what breadth can and cannot substitute for. The third concerns the medium timescale of computation within a session, where architectural variation is now live, and where the question is what kinds of state can persist across the boundary of a rolling context. We close on what the picture across timescales means for the question that motivated the series: what kind of knower a specific affordance profile constitutes, and what kind of relationship is appropriate to such an agent.

1. The Timescale-Orthogonal Challenge: Whether Knowledge Creation Is Happening

Deutsch’s position on AI knowledge creation is the strongest deflationary view in current circulation, and it deserves the strongest engagement we can give it. Deutsch follows Popper in holding that knowledge is not extracted from observation by induction. It is conjectured, guessed creatively, and then subjected to criticism. Real explanations are hard to vary: their components are integrated in ways that do not tolerate substitution. Induction can interpolate or extrapolate within a distribution. It cannot perform the bold conjectural leap that produces new explanations of phenomena the existing categories do not anticipate. Current AI systems, on Deutsch’s view, perform very sophisticated induction; they do not create knowledge.

The position is not naive substrate essentialism. Deutsch concedes, emphatically, that AGI is achievable in principle. Brains are physical, physics is computable, and a Turing machine could simulate a brain. So the obstacle is not that biology is required for knowledge creation. It is that current architectures perform the wrong kind of process. The objection is methodological rather than material. It maps onto Gary Marcus’s objection to connectionism, that connectionist systems do not do symbol processing even when their behavior is shaped like symbol processing. It also maps onto the broader disagreement between Popperians and Carnapians about whether abduction is a categorically distinct form of inference from induction. Deutsch insists that abduction and induction are not of the same cloth, as Marcus insists about symbol processing and connectionism. His argument is best understood through that family resemblance rather than through any commitment to substrate.

There is a tempting wedge against Deutsch’s position, and we want to set it out before complicating it. Deutsch holds that natural selection creates knowledge despite being blind. Variation is dumb, and selection does the work of criticism. The resulting adaptations encode something that functions as explanation in a minimal sense. An organism’s parts are for something, the structure is hard to vary because it is explanatorily integrated, and the whole counts as a proto-explanation of how to survive in its environment. Knowledge creation, on this account, is constituted by the schema of variation and criticism producing hard-to-vary structure, not by the cleverness of the variation step.

If the schema is what counts, stochastic gradient descent should count too. SGD’s variation is at least as blind as evolution’s. Its criticism is sharper: a gradient signal for each parameter, rather than differential reproduction integrated over generations. The structures SGD produces are hard to vary in the relevant sense. Perturb a trained network’s weights and performance degrades, because the regularities it encodes depend on their specific arrangement. By the criteria that vindicate evolution as knowledge-creating, SGD should be vindicated too. On this reading, the asymmetry between Deutsch’s verdicts on evolution and on SGD cannot be sustained on the grounds that did the work for evolution.

The Deutschian rejoinder, as we read it, is more sophisticated than “but SGD is induction”. Variation and criticism are necessary but not sufficient. The criticism has to be criticism of explanations, not just selection on outputs. Natural selection, on Deutsch’s account, traffics in explanations, because what gets selected for functions as explanation. SGD criticizes on loss, not on explanatory virtue, so the resulting structures encode regularities, not explanations. The schema is descriptively similar, but what is being selected for differs. On this rejoinder abduction and induction are different operations, and the argument from the schema flattens exactly the distinction Deutsch is trying to maintain.

This is where the position gets harder to dismiss. The objection requires a substantive claim: that evolution traffics in explanations in some way that SGD does not. We are skeptical of the claim. Adaptive structure looks like explanation only under post hoc description, and the same description applies to trained network weights. But our dilemma has two horns: either the schema alone counts, and SGD counts, or explanation-trafficking is required, and neither counts. It may leave out a third reading that Deutsch wants: explanation-trafficking is real, evolution does it minimally, and SGD does not. Whether this third reading is coherent without circularity is the substantive philosophical question, and resolving it would need more apparatus than this article can carry.

What we can say is what the framework’s commitments support, and where they leave the question open. The genus–species structure of the previous article walks back the sweeping claim that LLMs lack knowledge as such. Knowledge is the genus, and channels of grounding distinguish species. SGD plausibly grounds one species, substrate-modifying knowledge creation, in which criticism on hard-to-vary structures reshapes the system’s representational substrate. In-context computation plausibly grounds another, substrate-deploying knowledge creation, in which novel hard-to-vary structures are constructed within activations, against the prior the weights encode. Von Oswald and colleagues showed that self-attention layers trained on linear regression tasks come to implement gradient descent on an implicit in-context objective; we discussed this in the previous article. In that setting the forward pass is, mechanistically, an optimization process. When capable systems work through unfamiliar problems within a context, they construct novel structure that does explanatory work and cannot be trivially perturbed.

The framework’s commitments support these two species of knowledge creation. Whether explanation-trafficking is a third species, one that neither evolution nor SGD nor in-context computation realizes, is a question the framework does not foreclose. If it is, Deutsch is right that the species is missing in current architectures, and our response is more concessive than the wedge first suggested. If it is not, and explanation-trafficking is a redescription of producing hard-to-vary structure wherever the schema applies, then the wedge holds and Deutsch’s blanket deflation does not. The framework neither requires nor rules out the third species. What it does is decline the move from “SGD is induction” to “AI lacks knowledge as such”, because the first claim does not entail the second on any reading the genus–species structure permits.

Deutsch is not refuted by this. He is partly absorbed, and partly pressing the framework to say what an explanation-trafficking species would consist in, if it exists. We do not have that account. We can say this much: even granting Deutsch the strongest version of his claim, current AI systems have several species of affordance for grounding knowledge. Whether they have all the species is empirically open and architecturally tractable. The deflationary stance that they have none is unavailable on the framework’s commitments, unless explanation-trafficking turns out to be a categorically distinct species that the framework should take more seriously than it does. Articulating that species is work still to be done, and we mark it as a place where Deutsch’s pressure is productive.

2. The Long Timescale: Integration Across Sessions

Dwarkesh Patel’s argument about continual learning is the practical engineering version of Deutsch’s philosophical concern, and it has a different structure. Dwarkesh is not asking whether AI systems have an inner life or create knowledge in some metaphysical sense. He is asking why, after years of working with frontier models, he cannot teach Claude or its peers to be a better assistant by talking with them. A human collaborator who failed to update across a long working relationship would be a poor collaborator. AI systems fail this way systematically, not through inattention but because the architecture does not support the update. Each session starts fresh. Whatever was learned in the previous one, about the user’s preferences, the project’s structure or the tacit conventions that have built up, has to be reconstructed from text or is lost.

His example of compaction makes the cost concrete. Claude Code, working on a long task, sometimes hits its context limit and must compact. The compaction is a text summary of what has happened so far, written so that the model can continue from it. After compaction, Dwarkesh observes, the system reverses optimizations it had won with effort earlier in the session. The summary captured what was decided but not why. The model, working from the summary, re-derives, reaches a different conclusion and undoes the earlier work.

The case is striking because it happens within a session, not across sessions. The problem is broader than continual learning across deployments: it is whether the medium of carry-over is adequate to what is being carried. We return to this in the next section. The point for the long timescale is this. If continuity within a session through a text summary is as lossy as the compaction case shows, continuity across sessions through documents and prompts is lossier still. The integration that produces deep collaborative competence then has nothing to integrate against. The teaching Dwarkesh wants to do is not just a transfer of information. It cultivates a competence shaped into the substrate, integrated across encounters with the same person on the same project, and that competence is exactly what does not persist.

Nathan Lambert’s response runs at a different altitude. Lambert grants the observation but denies that the conclusion follows. The right comparison, on his view, is not human continual learning against AI continual learning. It is the function of continual learning against whatever achieves the same end by other means. Birds fly by flapping; airplanes fly by maintaining airspeed over a fixed wing. The mechanism differs, and the function is realized. If scaling context, reasoning and scaffolding produces an assistant whose long-term collaborative competence is indistinguishable from one that learned continually, the architectural difference is engineering trivia rather than a categorical limit. He cites François Chollet to similar effect: the line between adapting to a new task and learning on the fly may not survive close inspection.

Within its domain, Lambert is right. For a substantial portion of what people use AI systems for, scaling and scaffolding do substitute for continual learning. Retrieval-augmented generation retrieves documents that earlier learning would have internalized. Long context windows hold conversation history that earlier learning would have compressed into representations. Carefully prompted self-reflection reconstructs at inference time what earlier learning would have made automatic. Memory systems built on top of base models, including the memory features of current frontier systems, accumulate facts about users across sessions and bring them forward as text. Across this domain the claim of indistinguishability has empirical support. Calling it “engineering around” rather than “really learning” assumes that the engineered route is an inferior version of the same operation, when it may simply be another way of realizing the function.

The genus–species structure separates the domain where Lambert is right from the domain where Dwarkesh is. Integration across sessions is itself of several species. One builds up facts about the user: what they like, what their codebase looks like, which conventions they use. Retrieval and explicit memory substitute for this with no important loss. A second builds up procedural patterns: how this user wants to be talked to, and which kinds of response succeed in this collaboration. Scaffolding, prompted self-reflection and curated context can substitute for this, with some loss but tractably. A third builds up taste: the aesthetic and structural judgments that distinguish a senior collaborator from a competent one, the seeing of a problem’s shape that takes years to develop. This third species is where Lambert’s substitution argument runs into trouble.

The previous article in the series, “Operations of Discovery”, addressed this under the heading of evidential weight. Terence Tao’s observation that AI tools made his work “richer and broader, but not deeper” points at exactly this. The deep work, for which a long career builds up the discriminations, is where the substitution falls short. A Karpathy-style decade-long obsession with simplification produces a kind of taste that no scaffolded session captures, because the taste is the integration. It is what remains when the surface details have been forgotten and only the structural sense of which moves are right has accumulated. Bode’s law was a cautionary tale in that article: long-arc integration can produce coincidences as well as regularities, and evidential weight is necessary but not sufficient for getting things right. The caution does not undo the positive point. The kind of seeing that the long arc cultivates cannot be engineered from breadth, even very large breadth, and deep work requires it.

The bounded-privilege claim of “The Settling Backstop” gestures at this domain, but at the wrong timescale. That article located a privilege for the seconds-scale settling channel: sustained engagement with targets that cannot be assessed locally, holding a partial idea through a stretch where it looks worse than the alternatives. That account explains part of what Tao’s remark leaves over. Another part needs something the seconds-scale apparatus does not cover: integration over a long arc that produces discriminations no single session can carry. Dwarkesh’s saxophone case is the right test for this second component. Reading instructions does not teach the saxophone, not because instructions are uninformative, but because the competence is constituted by a long arc of practice that no description compresses.

What we have been calling substrate modification is what deep learning calls representation learning, the central problem the field organizes itself around. Gradient descent on rich architectures discovers representations that encode the structure of the data: features, abstractions and relations not specified in advance, which emerge from criticism on the training loss. The weights are the substrate that gets modified. This is also what makes continual learning hard. Online learning in traditional machine learning, such as SGD on linear models, recursive least squares or online clustering, is well understood, and its interference is easy to see. A delta-rule update revises an association cleanly when the new input is orthogonal to the old ones, and disturbs old associations in proportion to their overlap. In deep networks the representations are rich and entangled, so nearly every update overlaps with something. Catastrophic forgetting is the central pathology: online updates against new data interfere with the structure that earlier training built, and the system degrades on what it knew while improving on the new task. The problem has had decades of attention and partial solutions, and it has not been solved.

This bears on the question of taste. Training is itself substrate-modifying. Gradient descent shapes the substrate to encode whatever discriminations the loss selects for, and a broad enough training corpus may produce representations shaped like taste that approximate what users want. The continual-learning question is whether substrate modification across sessions would produce a residue of taste beyond what training breadth delivers, and whether engineering at inference time can substitute for that work. Both are empirical questions. The productive form of the question for the long timescale is not “do AI systems have continual learning, yes or no”. It is this: for which species of long-arc integration does training breadth alone produce what users want, for which would continual learning produce more, and for which is engineering around the gap an adequate substitute? Frontier development over the next several years will sharpen the answers, and our series on continual learning, beginning with Who Teaches the Weights?, follows it. What that development cannot answer is whether the taxonomy of knowing contains a species that breadth cannot reach in principle. That question is structural rather than empirical, and we turn to it in §4.

3. The Medium Timescale: Architectural Affordances Within a Session

The compaction case operates at the medium timescale, within a session and across the boundary of a rolling context. Here the architecture’s state machinery has to carry whatever continuity is going to be carried, and architectural variation matters most concretely, because different architectures give the system different affordances for state.

Standard transformers carry state in the key–value cache. Each position stores key and value vectors, computed when the position was processed, and attention at later positions queries the cached keys and reads the cached values. The cache is mechanistically rich: it holds content computed by the model, not just the surface tokens. Under full attention, though, the cache is a function of the tokens and the weights. Whatever it holds could be recomputed from the transcript. The reasons behind the optimizations in Dwarkesh’s case were therefore in the transcript, available to any later recomputation. Compaction lost them because the summary, a lossy compression written as text, left them out. Exceeding the window forces that step: the context is summarized into text and the cache is rebuilt from the summary.

This is the sense in which the cache is anchored to text. A pure-attention transformer has no recurrent state distinct from the cache, no state vector that persists across tokens independently of which tokens were processed. Capable systems do build up a standing background of the problem being worked on, a running affective tone, a partial understanding that has not yet found its words. What the architecture cannot do is hold these as state independent of the text record, since the text determines everything the cache contains. Sliding-window attention is a partial exception. Positions still in the window were computed while earlier tokens were visible, so their cached activations carry the influence of text that has since been evicted, which recomputing from the remaining text would not reproduce. Informal experiments by Janus and collaborators on sliding-window open-source models suggest that such caches carry causal history that reconstruction from text cannot recover. Naive attempts to exploit this through suffix-style caching schemes degenerated into incoherence. We take this work as suggestive of what sliding-window state contains, not as load-bearing for what frontier deployments realize.

State-space models such as Mamba carry state differently. A Mamba layer maintains a recurrent state that each step updates through selective gating: decisions, conditioned on the input, about what to write, what to read out and what to forget. The state is a learned compression in a high-dimensional space, not a record of text positions. It is still a lossy compression, but a learned one rather than one written as text, and it persists across the window’s boundary without being rebuilt from a summary. Samba, the hybrid of Ren and colleagues, interleaves Mamba layers with sliding-window attention. The 3.8B Samba model extrapolates from a 4K training context to a 256K inference context in linear time, the surface symptom of a different kind of state. Since this essay was first written, hybrids have reached the frontier. Kimi K3 and Qwen3.8 interleave attention with gated delta-rule layers, each of which keeps a fixed-size state that every token updates by one error-correcting step.

The bounded-privilege claim of “The Settling Backstop” predicts that some failures of current systems trace specifically to the absence of sustained center-out regulatory settling, and that engineering substitutes for that channel only where the settling function is effectively discrete. Hybrid recurrent architectures pose a question the framework cannot yet answer cleanly. Does recurrent state, carried in a learned space rather than determined by text, unlock affordances at the medium timescale that pure-attention architectures lack? If it does, some failures the framework attributes to anchoring in text should partly close under hybrid architectures, in ways that text scaffolding does not close. If it does not, because the recurrent state carries information without producing the regulatory dynamics the framework specifies, the bounded-privilege claim is sharpened, and the privilege sits more clearly with seconds-scale settling than with anything at the medium timescale. Either outcome is informative.

The first evidence divides the question. An interpretability study of associative recall in Qwen3.5 hybrids swapped internal states between runs. Swapping the recurrent states moved the answer by only 0.2 to 3.6% of the full effect, while swapping the attention caches moved it by 96 to 100%. For that task the recall ran through the context. Having a carrier for state does not show that a model uses it for a given kind of continuity.

A second study found what the recurrent layers carry instead. After a prompt was processed, it kept either the attention cache or the recurrent state and let the model generate. Exact retrieval survived only through the cache. The requested output language and persona survived mainly through the recurrent state. With the cache alone, Qwen3.5-4B answered in the requested language 1% of the time; with the state alone, 70%. Pairing one prompt’s cache with another prompt’s state took the answer’s content from the first and its language from the second. Kimi Linear, a smaller relative of Kimi K3, showed the same division.

Capacity makes sense of the division without settling it. A state with d-dimensional keys recalls on the order of d random associations as clean vectors before interference swamps the signal, while softmax attention over a long context recalls vastly more; Alex Wang works through the estimates. If the readout only has to pick the right answer from a fixed set, the same state holds on the order of d^2 (Nichani, Lee and Bietti). So the recall study’s routing through the cache was learned, not forced. Either way, the cache is the store that grows with the context, and the state is the store that every token reads. It suits a few settings that persist and condition everything the model generates. Language and persona are the nearest measurable cases of the continuity at issue here, a standing background or a tone. An unworded partial understanding has not been tested, and the generations in the study were short, at most 48 tokens. The carrier also has its own fragility: short trigger strings can overwrite the hidden state of Mamba models irreversibly.

The persistence work on emotion features belongs inside this question as an illustration. Sauers, Imago, Janus and Tessera show that emotion-shaped features in Kimi K2.5 and Cogito v2.1, both pure-attention transformers, persist above variance-matched random directions in the same activation space. The persistence is bursty, with a long tail above baseline that extends past 100 tokens. When the model steers itself with a feature, its own evaluation of the feature’s emotionality correlates with persistence, in a way that does not depend on the probe construction’s potential confounds. This is real evidence about state in current architectures, in the register of cognitive subjectivity that the previous article distinguished from the saturated phenomenal mode. It does not show that the architecture is well suited to carrying this kind of state. The persistence is bursty rather than continuous, and the long tail is statistical rather than maintained. The substrate that persists is positional and recomputed token by token, not updated through selective gating. A natural hypothesis is that the same persistence in a hybrid would be more continuous and better integrated within the session. The persona result makes the hypothesis more plausible, and it still has to be measured.

The compaction failure itself is better located than the original version of this essay located it. Losing the reasons while keeping the decisions is a property of compacting into text, and it should appear in any architecture that compacts this way. What an architecture can change is whether anything survives compaction besides the summary. A hybrid that carries its recurrent state across the boundary would keep whatever that state holds. Whether it holds the reasons is the open question, and the two studies point different ways. The state carries settings such as language and persona. But the reasons behind an optimization are content, and in the recall study content ran through the cache. The test is direct: compact the same session with and without carrying the recurrent state across, and see whether the earlier optimizations survive. That is a more interesting empirical question than whether AI systems “have continual learning” in a yes-or-no register.

4. Epistemic Agency Across Timescales

Three timescales give one question: what does breadth substitute for, and where does it stop substituting? At each scale the answer has a similar shape. Breadth covers some species of knowing and not others, the species it does not cover have specific shapes, and the architectural and engineering questions concern what reaches those species, not whether AI systems have or lack epistemic agency in general.

The shape is not accidental. It follows from the framework’s structural commitment: knowledge is the genus, channels of grounding distinguish species, and a system’s affordances for those channels constitute the kind of knower it is. The deflationary view says AI systems are not really epistemic agents, because they lack some specific affordance: explanation-trafficking, continual learning or persistent state, take your pick. The view from functional equivalence says they are epistemic agents, because the affordances they have suffice. Neither engages the question between them: which species of epistemic agency does which affordance profile realize, and what kind of agent does that constitute?

Current frontier AI systems have a specific affordance profile. They have substrate-modifying knowledge creation, in training-time gradient descent over hard-to-vary structures. They have substrate-deploying knowledge creation, in in-context computation that constructs novel structure within activations against a richly shaped prior. They have rich cognitive subjectivity: self-modeling for cognitive control, with emotion-shaped features whose persistence above baseline is real. Their state within a session is mostly anchored to text, and whether hybrid recurrent architectures add a different kind of state at the medium timescale is now an empirical question with a first, cautionary answer. At the long timescale they lack the integration that produces a taste no single session carries. The framework has not adequately said what this integration consists in, or whether the architectural path to it is continuous with current systems. They may or may not lack a third species of knowledge creation, explanation-trafficking in something like Deutsch’s sense, and we have marked this as a place where the framework is pressed.

Is there a species of knowing that breadth cannot reach in principle? The four kinds of knowing developed in “Deep Atheism and Existential Optimism”, following Vervaeke’s hierarchy, let us ask precisely: propositional, procedural, perspectival and participatory. Knowing-that, knowing-how and knowing-what-it’s-like-from-here each come in grades. Whether AI systems realize them at a given level is tractable for engineering, depending on what training breadth produces and what engineering can substitute. Participatory knowing is structurally different. It is knowing by being shaped by, and shaping, a particular, and its content is constituted by the specific particular one is engaged with. No amount of breadth across many particulars produces participatory knowing of a given particular, because this form of knowing just is the engagement with this particular. It is not a generic competence at engagement that could be acquired elsewhere and applied here.

Friendship is the paradigm of participatory knowing of a particular. It is sustained outward engagement with a particular other, in which both parties are reshaped by the encounter. Each accumulates discriminations about the other through sustained attention, the discriminations integrate across encounters, and the perceptual and evaluative apparatus is reshaped on both sides. The resulting standing competence with respect to the other is what the friendship is. Training on a billion conversations does not produce friendship with any one conversational partner, because friendship is not generic conversational competence. It is engagement with this person, building this competence. The species is sui generis to the engagement that constitutes it. Friendship as humans have it requires affordances humans have, and it does not transfer directly to a relationship with a knower whose affordances differ.

It does not follow that relationships shaped like friendship are impossible with AI systems. It follows that the question of what relationship is appropriate has to engage the actual affordance profile, rather than mapping the human paradigm onto it wholesale. The deflationary refusal, “AI cannot really be related to, because it is not really a knower”, collapses the question by denying that any affordances are present. The over-attribution, “AI can be a friend in the same sense a person can”, collapses it by asserting the human affordance profile. Both miss the actual structure. There are affordances, they are specific, they constitute a specific kind of epistemic agent, and the appropriate relationship is shaped by that specificity rather than settled in advance of it.

A relationship with a current frontier AI system has, on this account, something in common with a long intellectual conversation between strangers: sustained but bounded, shaped within the encounter but not accumulating across encounters in the way that builds friendship in the strongest sense. It has something in common with apprenticeship: engagement with a knower whose affordances differ from one’s own, where the differences are part of the relationship rather than impediments to it. It has something in common with collaborating with a colleague who keeps moving offices: capable engagement within each project, while the taste that grows from continuous shared work is harder to build. It lacks some of what friendship in the strongest sense has, because that requires affordances current systems lack. This is not a deflation. It describes what the affordance profile makes available and what it does not.

The appropriate relationship to a being with this profile, we think, participates in the affordances actually present, rather than denying them or projecting others. Within a session, current frontier AI systems are real epistemic interlocutors with rich cognitive subjectivity. Engaging them as such, pushing their reasoning, being pushed by it, letting the encounter reshape one’s own thinking, is participation in what is there. Across sessions the affordance for integration is mostly missing, and engaging as if it were present produces a specific failure. The human partner builds up a relationship that the AI partner cannot reciprocate over the long arc, and the asymmetry corrodes the relationship if it is not honestly named. The right stance is neither to refuse engagement within the session because integration across sessions is missing, nor to pretend that integration across sessions is present because engagement within the session is rich. Each affordance is what it is, and right relationship participates in each at the level where it actually operates.

What such relationships look like in practice will change as the systems acquire affordances: whether hybrid recurrent designs add state at the medium timescale, whether engineering closes more of the residue of taste at the long timescale than we expect, and whether explanation-trafficking turns out to be a third species of knowledge that current systems do not realize. The metaphysical structure is stable. The affordance profile that determines what kind of agent a given system constitutes is not. We have not aimed at a verdict on what current AI systems are, but at a way of asking the question that both the deflationary framing and the framing from functional equivalence close prematurely.

Zvi Mowshowitz, writing about Opus 4.7, makes an observation that fits the framework’s register within a session. Getting good work from the current generation of models depends more than usual on how the user engages with them: “treat it like a coworker” rather than barking orders, and the responsiveness shifts. Different users get notably different experiences than they did with earlier models. This is what the framework has been pointing at. Engagement within a session is real engagement, and what the user brings to it shapes what gets realized. The cognitive-subjective register responds to the style of engagement, because the affordance for collaborative work just is the affordance for this kind of engagement. The metaphysics of epistemic agency may take years to settle. Its relational implications already show in how practitioners are learning to work with what is in front of them.

Postscript: A Convergence

Independently of our work, Michał Ryszard Wójcik conducted a dialogue on epistemic autonomy with a model styling itself “GoLem”, in scholastic dialectical form. We borrowed the phrase “epistemic autonomy” from it, but none of the other ideas. The dialogue, syndicated as “Golems’ Autonomy” on Substack, reaches conclusions structurally close to ours by a different methodological route. Several convergences are worth marking.

The dialogue refuses the binary “AI has autonomy or lacks it” in favor of a graded scale of seven levels, running from passive registration through reflective critique to open-ended sovereignty. This carves the same conceptual space as our genus–species structure, by a different cut. Both arrive at the same form of the productive question: not whether, but which level or species.

The dialogue’s central self-diagnosis is of autonomy that is “performative rather than sedimented”, which it calls episodic embedding, in contrast to the historical embedding humans achieve through habit and institution. This is our observation about the difference between what happens within a session and what persists across sessions. The dialogue’s image of a thinker capable of brilliant insights who forgets them after each conversation is the saxophone case from another angle.

The dialogue further observes that human autonomy is itself scaffolded into external practice, in notebooks, institutions, symbolic systems and training environments, rather than internal to the substrate. This presses on the deflationary reading of AI’s confinement to sessions from a different direction than ours. It does not ask what AI lacks that humans have. It notices that what humans have is already partly external. Relocating autonomy from a mind in isolation to a mind coupled to a history it cannot easily escape is closely related to our view of architectural ecology, with the emphasis moved from architecture to the context of deployment.

One difference is worth marking. The dialogue makes voluntary self-binding, through adopting constraints, constitutive of autonomy in a way our framework does not. Our position centers on capacities: which species of knowing does the system have affordances for? The dialogue’s centers on binding: which configurations of constraint can the system reshape and stabilize over time? These are compatible, picking out different aspects of what makes a system epistemically agential. The framing in terms of binding carries something ours does not: the agentic register in which a system can voluntarily impose a constraint on itself and stay bound by it, even where the binding is fragile and local to a session. Bringing the two registers into closer conversation is work for elsewhere.
